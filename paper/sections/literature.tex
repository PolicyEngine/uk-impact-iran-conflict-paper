\section{Related literature}
\label{sec:literature}

\subsection{Compensate incomes, or subsidise prices?}

The grey-literature consensus of the 2022--23 European energy crisis is that governments should let retail prices rise and compensate households through income transfers, on the ground that a price subsidy destroys the conservation incentive when supply is scarce \citep{ari2022}. The Bruegel fiscal tracker supplied the indictment: only around a third of European support was targeted \citep{sgaravatti2023}; \citet{amores2023} reach compatible conclusions in EUROMOD, and the ECB took the same line \citep{ecb2023}. That slogan --- compensate incomes, never prices --- is the backdrop to the Chancellor's rejection of a repeated Energy Price Guarantee.

The peer-reviewed frontier does not agree. \citet{levell2025}, solving for optimal energy-crisis policy on UK data, find that the package the UK actually implemented reduced both the mean and the dispersion of welfare losses at an efficiency cost of twelve per cent of its revenue cost, and that the optimal policy includes a \emph{strictly positive price subsidy}. The subsidy shrinks as the transfer instrument becomes better able to condition on income and past usage, but does not vanish, because usage carries information about need that income does not. Where the transfer instrument is coarse --- and the British one is, since roughly 40 per cent of households struggling to heat their homes are not on means-tested benefits \citep{jrf2026} --- the case for a price instrument survives the theory. The UK's live choice between a social tariff, a universal discounted block \citep{jrf2026} and a levy-side rebate \citep{ippr2026} is an empirical instance of that question, being decided without the corresponding UK evidence.

\subsection{Incidence: pounds versus per cent}

\citet{fetzer2024} match 22.2 million Energy Performance Certificates to meter records and establish that exposure in \emph{cash} rises with affluence: more affluent areas occupy larger, less efficient dwellings, so the Energy Price Guarantee benefited them disproportionately. They are explicit that this is the only distributional statement their data support --- ``we can only conduct our distributional analysis in absolute terms, as we cannot compute energy expenditures as a share of income''. That disclaimer is material: the paired cash-and-percentage profile below leans on them for the cash arm only, and the crossed pattern this paper displays is assembled from two sources rather than read off one. The countervailing fact is older and less contested: because energy is a necessity with a falling Engel curve, the same shock costs poorer households more as a \emph{share} of resources. \citet{advani2013} report a UK energy budget share of 15.8 per cent of expenditure in the bottom decile against 3.3 per cent in the top. Inflation therefore differs across the distribution, though the sharpest UK measurement of that divergence is not an energy result: \citet{chen2024} find grocery-price inflation over 2021Q3--2023Q3 cumulating 7.7 percentage points lower for the top expenditure decile than the bottom, driven by ``cheapflation''.

Three UK comparators can be verified against their sources. \citet{advani2013} give a budget-\emph{share} ratio of 4.8; \citet{ons2022energyprices}, on the \emph{level} of gas and electricity spending as a share of disposable income, 7 per cent against 2 per cent, a ratio of 3.5; and \citet{nef2022}, on the \emph{increase} in energy costs from the April 2022 cap rise --- the closest analogue, being a shock rather than a level --- 6 per cent against 0.75, a ratio of 7.5. Three features govern the comparison. The denominator matters most: expenditure-share ratios are mechanically flatter than income-share ratios, and the gradients here are on net income throughout. Shock-based measures are steeper than level-based ones, so NEF is the relevant anchor. And all four ratios are \emph{pre-policy}: \citet{rf2022chilling} show that measured net of the \pounds 400 Energy Bills Support Scheme energy shares flatten substantially, and a gradient stated net of support is a different number from one stated gross of it. A fourth figure sometimes quoted from that report, a ratio of around 1.75 net of support, appears only in a chart and I do not rely on it.

The headline decile-one to decile-ten ratio of Section~\ref{sec:results-decile}, \genDecileRatioPct{} to one, sits \emph{above} every published UK comparator including NEF's 7.5. That is a reason for caution rather than a claim to have found a steeper gradient. Measured from decile two --- removing the decile where a fifth of households have non-positive equivalised income --- the same specification gives \genDecileRatioFromDecileTwoPct{} to one, inside the published bracket. The bottom decile is where this paper's gradient and the published record part company, and it is also where its denominator is weakest.

\subsection{Vertical and horizontal redistribution}

\citet{cronin2019} show for US carbon pricing that horizontal redistribution \emph{within} income deciles exceeds vertical redistribution between them: two households at the same income face wholly different exposure depending on dwelling, fuel, location and composition. \citet{douenne2020} makes the point for France with a sharper edge --- flat recycling of carbon tax revenue is progressive on average yet leaves a large mass of horizontal losers among rural and off-gas-grid poor households. \emph{No UK counterpart exists.} Britain has a large off-gas-grid population and an inefficient housing stock, so the French result should be expected to travel, but nobody has established it. \citet{sallee2019} supplies the bound: when transfers condition on observables rather than on the heterogeneity that determines exposure, compensating all losers is infeasible, and pushing the uncompensated share towards zero requires overcompensating many non-losers. The right question of a social tariff is therefore not what it delivers to the bottom decile on average but what share of losing households \emph{within} each decile it leaves whole --- the number the UK debate has not been given, and which Section~\ref{sec:policy} reports.

\subsection{What a static microsimulation cannot see}

\citet{kanzig2023} is the principal threat: poorer households suffer disproportionately not mainly through energy budget shares but through income and employment, with general-equilibrium and indirect effects accounting for roughly two-thirds of the total response, so a static consumption-side microsimulation recovers at most a third of true incidence. \citet{goulder2019} decompose the mechanism --- use-side effects regressive, source-side effects progressive, microsimulation seeing only the use side. \citet{ohlendorf2021} show in meta-analysis that whether an energy price change is judged progressive depends systematically on methodological choices, a warning against a single headline sign. \citet{pallotti2024} find that euro-area heterogeneity in 2021--23 was driven by nominal net asset positions rather than by energy baskets at all; PolicyEngine UK carries wealth imputed from the Wealth and Assets Survey, so a British version is possible, but I do not attempt it.

Two anchors are conventional. The first-order approximation used below is Deaton's, an upper bound on the compensating variation once substitution is allowed; where demand systems are required the standard references are \citet{deaton1980} and \citet{banks1997}, and \citet{odonoghue2023} set out the closest procedural precedent for combining price shocks with tax-benefit microsimulation. Because the model has no demand-response block, any elasticity has to be imported, and the sweep in Appendix~\ref{app:elasticity} relies on two sources: \citet{labandeira2017}, whose post-1990 meta-analysis gives per-carrier short-run means, and \citet{priesmann2025}, whose income-varying German estimates find gas and fuel elasticities falling in absolute magnitude as income rises. The single figures reported against each named specification in Table~\ref{tab:elasticity} are \emph{not} coefficients from either paper: they are this paper's own spend-weighted averages of the published per-carrier coefficients, weighted by UK expenditure shares and, in the Priesmann case, interpolated across income deciles. They will not be found in either source.

\subsection{Geopolitical risk and the macro side}

\citet{caldara2022} construct the Geopolitical Risk index; \citet{caldara2025} find that geopolitical risk raises inflation while lowering activity, the stagflationary configuration the UK experienced after February 2026; \citet{verduzco2026} show oil \emph{inventories} to be the key propagation margin for supply-reducing geopolitical events. The most useful complication is \citet{boe2025}, who separate ``Acts'' from ``Threats'' and find that realised military events produce \emph{negative} oil price and inflation responses on average, markets having priced the threat in advance; any claim that the 2026 episode was inflationary must be defended against that possibility, and the scenario calibration addresses it. The structural foundations are \citet{hamilton1983}, \citet{kilian2009} and \citet{baumeister2019}. On the current episode, \citet{dallasfed2026} map Hormuz-closure scenarios into US inflation, \citet{dallasfed2026b} put the 2026 shortfall in global oil supply at more than twice the peak disruption of 1973 and estimate the US GDP response at around one-sixth of the decline elsewhere --- implying a substantially larger effect on an economy like the UK's, for which \emph{the counterpart is unwritten} --- and \citet{imf2026ports} show that a 100-hour increase in port-to-port shipping time raises inflation by around 0.5 percentage points. The scenario backdrop is \citet{weo2026}, \citet{worldbank2026}, \citet{niesr2026spring} and \citet{niesr2026summer}; the only published UK household aggregate against which these results can be checked is \citet{rf2026outlook}.

\subsection{Positioning}

Three gaps follow. The international consensus against price subsidies has never been tested against the peer-reviewed result that the optimum contains one, in the country where that result was estimated and on the choice now being made. The pounds-versus-per-cent tension has never been displayed simultaneously on the same British households, and the within-decile uncompensated-loser count has never been reported for the UK proposals. And the Seventh Carbon Budget established ``macroeconomic model plus distributional annex'' as the expected format for UK official analysis \citep{youngman2026}, yet no one has built the macro-to-microsimulation link that format presupposes. That link is what this paper constructs.
