\section{Data and methods}
\label{sec:methodology}

The analysis maps macroeconomic oil and gas scenarios into a household price vector, computes first-order cost changes on nationally grossed microdata, crosses losses in pounds against losses as a share of income, and scores five policy responses. The price shock is applied on the consumption side rather than as a parameter reform, because PolicyEngine UK has no price channel: household energy quantities are imputed inputs, fixed by the data and moved by no policy parameter. Appendix~\ref{app:modelboundary} documents which objects are and are not present in the model.

\subsection{Step 1: from a macro scenario to a household price vector}
\label{sec:step1}

Household energy prices in Great Britain are not market prices. For the roughly four in five households on a default tariff the price per kilowatt hour is a regulated ceiling set quarterly by Ofgem as a mechanical function of forward wholesale prices observed in an earlier averaging window, plus network, policy, operating and margin allowances. Any macro-to-micro exercise for the UK must pass through the cap formula rather than a spot price.

\paragraph{Scenarios.} The \emph{baseline} follows the NIESR Summer 2026 Outlook \citep{niesr2026summer}, Brent averaging \$103; the \emph{adverse} case follows NIESR's alternative \citep{niesr2026spring} at \$140; the \emph{realised} case uses observed 2026 outturns --- gas peaking \genGasPeakPence{} pence per therm and Brent \genOilPeakUsd{} dollars per barrel above pre-war, a \genOilPeakPct{} per cent move --- extended at forward-curve levels. The realised path is the central case; the other two bound it.

\paragraph{Wholesale to cap.} A proportional wholesale move enters the cap as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:passthrough}
\Delta \ln p^{\text{cap}}_{f,t} \;=\; \phi_f \, \Delta \ln p^{\text{wholesale}}_{f,t-\ell},
\end{equation}
with a fuel-specific pass-through $\phi_f$ and a lag $\ell$ of one and a half quarters. Two attenuations apply \emph{independently} rather than composed: the bill-level pass-through $\phi_{\text{gas}} = \genBillLevelPassThrough{}$, the share of a proportional wholesale gas move reaching a gas bill, and a gas-to-electricity ratio $\psi = \genElecToGasPassThroughRatio{}$, calibrated to the product of the marginal-pricing frequency and the wholesale share of the electricity cap. Neither is 0.45: that is the wholesale \emph{share} of a dual-fuel bill, which bounds $\phi$ from above but is not a pass-through. The lag is derived, not assumed: Ofgem's observation window closes about seven weeks before the charge period and covers roughly the preceding quarter, putting its midpoint four to five months ahead of the bill it prices. The phase-in profile is built from each cap period's \emph{actual} observation window and the share of it falling after 28 February 2026, not from a linear ramp slid along a calendar --- which is what makes the lag an institutional property rather than a fitted parameter, and why Appendix~\ref{app:caplag} measures a residual. The annual domestic price is the consumption-weighted average of the quarterly cap levels over the window; the steady-state alternative is a labelled variant, and the choice moves the aggregate from \pounds \genSteadyStateAggBn{} to \pounds \genAggSpendRealisedBn{} billion.

\paragraph{The modelled window.} Every annual figure is a total over \textbf{March 2026 to February 2027}, from which the phase-in weights, seasonal consumption weights and pump damping fraction all derive. Annualising the two legs over different windows and summing them is not admissible. Calendar 2026 reports twelve months of denominator against ten of shock and truncates the cap leg exactly where the cap's lag puts it; a 2026Q4--2027Q3 window misses the pump peak. The twelve months from the shock contains one full heating cycle and both cap resets. Its one cost is that calendar 2026 is the window of the Resolution Foundation's \pounds 11 billion \citep{rf2026outlook}, which is therefore retained as a separate run.

\paragraph{The pre-war cap is solved, not observed.} The forward-curve window behind the July 2026 cap of \pounds 1{,}663 already contained the war, so using it as a pre-shock baseline while assigning that quarter full war pass-through subtracts part of the shock from itself: the domestic leg is understated at source and, the pump leg not being, the motor-fuel share is inflated by exactly that amount. This is a defect of identification, not calibration. The pre-war level and the gas peak-to-sustained fraction are therefore solved jointly from both published caps, after adding back the roughly \pounds 45 by which Ofgem's temporary removal of VAT on electricity holds the October cap below the formula level. The solution is a pre-war cap of \pounds \genCapBaselineLevel{} and a gas sustained fraction of \genGasSustainedFraction{} per cent, reproducing both published caps to \pounds \genCapFitMaxErrorGbp{} --- which is not evidence, two equations in two unknowns leaving zero degrees of freedom, and establishes only that the solver converged. On that path the October cap moves \genCapAnchorMovePct{} per cent and July already carries \genCapBaseMovePct{} per cent, giving a war-consistent October level of \pounds \genCapAnnualised{}. All cap figures are on the reduced typical-consumption basis of 1 July 2026 \citep{ofgem2026tdcv}, and comparisons across that boundary are not made.

What the solve is fragile to is the assumed monthly shape of the gas peak, which is not observed at the frequency the formula consumes it. Sweeping that profile moves the solved cap from \pounds \genGasProfileCapMinGbp{} to \pounds \genGasProfileCapMaxGbp{}, the aggregate loss from \pounds \genGasProfileAggMinBn{} to \pounds \genGasProfileAggMaxBn{} billion and the motor-fuel share from \genGasProfileFuelShareMinPct{} to \genGasProfileFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent, with \genGasProfileNonIdentifiedCount{} of \genGasProfileCellCount{} cells admitting no solution (Appendix~\ref{app:domesticleg}). That is the paper's principal fragility, and a range that wide on the composition result is a finding about what these data can identify.

\paragraph{Bill factors, not unit rates.} Because $\phi_f$ is a share of the \emph{bill}, the calibration delivers bill uplifts. The \emph{steady-state} factor is \genGasUnitRatePct{} per cent on gas and \genElecUnitRatePct{} per cent on electricity; the \emph{annual applied} factor, which every result is computed on, scales those by the phase-in weight to \genAnnualPhaseInGasPct{} and \genAnnualPhaseInElecPct{} per cent. Standing charges do not move with wholesale costs, so the implied unit-rate increases are larger than either bill figure.

\paragraph{Asymmetric gas and electricity.} Gas is shocked separately because it is 62 per cent of UK final household energy consumption \citep{desnz2025ecuk} and because gas-fired plant sets the system marginal price roughly 85 per cent of the time, so a gas shock propagates into the electricity cap but attenuated. Sweeping the marginal-pricing share from 0.70 to the naive symmetric 1.00 moves the mean loss by \genAsymMeanShiftPct{} per cent and leaves the decile ratio essentially frozen (Appendix~\ref{app:asymmetry}); what it moves is \emph{composition}, the gas share of the domestic loss running from \genGasShareDomesticLow{} to \genGasShareDomesticHigh{} per cent. The distributional conclusions do not turn on this parameter; the separation is kept because a gas-only social tariff, an electricity-side rebate and the block's choice of unit rate land differently depending on which fuel carries the loss.

\paragraph{Pump prices.} Retail motor fuel is not capped and pass-through is fast, so this channel carries no cap lag. The proportional pump move is set directly from observed outturns --- roughly $+20$ per cent for petrol and $+36$ for diesel at the 2026 peak --- rather than from a duty-and-VAT accounting identity, which enters as a rationale for the calibrated numbers rather than as something the model evaluates. Those moves are \emph{peaks}, and an annual figure requires an annual average: that pump prices pass through in weeks and the cap in quarters is a claim about \emph{lag}, not \emph{duration}, and licenses applying the move sooner, not applying the peak for a year. The main specification therefore damps the pump path at a sustained fraction of \genPumpSustainedFraction{} per cent, derived from the observed monthly pump profile over the same window. The two fractions are close, gas being the larger, because on the solved baseline the war's effect on the forward curve was persistent enough that the cap carries most of it into the window; since the motor-fuel share depends almost entirely on the \emph{ratio} of the two, this is the single calibration that most moves the composition result. The undamped-peak variant is retained as an explicit upper bound, the \emph{peak-fuel} specification, at \pounds \genPeakFuelAggBn{} billion and \pounds \genPeakFuelLossMean{} per household.

\subsection{Step 2: first-order incidence on the consumption side}
\label{sec:step2}

For household $h$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:firstorder}
\Delta C_h \;=\; \sum_{f} q_{hf} \, \Delta p_{f},
\end{equation}
where $q_{hf}$ is the baseline quantity of fuel $f$ --- gas, electricity, petrol and diesel, kept separate --- and $\Delta p_f$ the modelled proportional price change. This is the \citet{deaton1980} first-order approximation to the compensating variation, substitution entering only at second order, so it is an explicit \emph{upper bound} on the true welfare loss. Holding quantities fixed holds litres fixed, so fuel duty is unchanged by construction and only the VAT-inclusive spend rises.

The shock never enters modelled net income; energy spend is not a tax-benefit object in this model. PolicyEngine UK supplies the \emph{baseline}: net income, the imputed energy quantities, the equivalisation and decile construction, the poverty indicators and the survey weights. No demand system is attempted --- the energy quantities are imputed rather than observed with independent price variation, there is no household-level price variation under a national cap, and cross-sectional expenditure variation identifies Engel curves rather than price responses --- so the zero-elasticity assumption is explicit and primitive, and demand response is relegated to Appendix~\ref{app:elasticity}, where measured as a welfare loss it shaves at most \genWelfareShavedHighEpsPct{} per cent off the total.

\paragraph{Reporting conventions.} Losses are reported in pounds a year and as a percentage of equivalised household net income after housing costs on the HBAI scale, the concept the published literature uses; the unequivalised gradient is reported once as a robustness line. Percentages are the \emph{aggregate ratio} --- weighted loss over weighted income within the group --- rather than a mean of household ratios, because equivalised income is zero or negative for a non-trivial part of the distribution. Households with non-positive equivalised income are dropped from every percentage statistic and the dropped weight reported: \genDecileOneZeroIncomeShare{} per cent of decile one, and all \genCoverageExcludedHouseholdsM{} million households (\genCoverageExcludedSharePct{} per cent) that fall outside deciles one to ten and carry the model's \(-1\) sentinel. Decile-level shares of the aggregate loss are normalised on the deciles displayed.

\subsection{Step 3: crossing the two incidence facts}
\label{sec:step3}

Both metrics are computed on the same households and crossed: by income decile, within income decile, and by region. The paired decile profile is the paper's headline object, and the divergence between the two orderings is what makes the compensation problem in Section~\ref{sec:policy} hard. The within-decile spread is reported alongside, since \citet{cronin2019}, \citet{douenne2020} and \citet{sallee2019} establish that horizontal dispersion routinely exceeds the vertical gradient. Geography is region and country: the enhanced FRS release carries no constituency weight matrix, being calibrated to 149 area targets, and its local-authority field is a degenerate default identical for every household. No constituency statistic appears anywhere in this paper and none has been imputed to stand in for one.

\subsection{Step 4: scoring the policy responses}
\label{sec:step4}

Five responses are scored against the shocked baseline. Three do not exist in PolicyEngine UK and are computed from the model's own eligibility and household variables, which determines what each scored number means.

\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item \textbf{Means-tested social tariff (35 per cent discount)} on the \emph{shocked} domestic bill for households receiving any means-tested benefit, the population constructed from the eight means-tested entitlements the model carries.
\item \textbf{Universal discounted block (JRF).} A 50 per cent discount on the first half of \emph{typical} consumption plus a \pounds 60 per-child allowance, applied to the \emph{level} of the block, which is what JRF propose \citep{jrf2026}; defining the block on a fixed quantity rather than as a proportion of each household's own bill is essential, since a proportional discount would pay most to the largest users. The modelled cost is \pounds \genJrfGapModelledBn{} billion against JRF's \pounds \genJrfGapSponsorBn{} billion; both are sized on Ofgem's typical-consumption basis, and the gap is the discount \emph{rate}, which JRF do not publish and this paper set at \genJrfModelledDiscountPct{} per cent (Section~\ref{sec:results-policy}).
\item \textbf{Warm Home Discount expansion (\pounds 150)}, a flat payment to the means-tested population, modelled as reported rather than as legislated, no published Government document having been located.
\item \textbf{VAT zero-rating on domestic fuel and power.} LCF domestic energy spend is VAT-inclusive, so the gain is $b\,(1 - 1/1.05)$: at most \genVatCutMaxOffsetPct{} per cent of a domestic bill against applied rises of \genAppliedGasUnitRatePct{} and \genAppliedElecUnitRatePct{} per cent, and it reaches no motor fuel.
\item \textbf{Flat per-household rebate (IPPR)}, a uniform \pounds 183 credit funded by clawing back regulated network windfalls \citep{ippr2026}, financing scored separately.
\end{enumerate}

Five metrics are reported: two conventional between-decile measures, the \emph{cost per pound of bottom-decile gain} (a dimensionless ratio bounded below by one, carrying no currency symbol) and the \emph{share of spend reaching deciles one to three}; the within-decile \emph{share of losers left uncompensated}; and two continuous measures, the \emph{share of the aggregate loss offset} and the \emph{mean and median residual loss}. The continuous measures are necessary because the count is knife-edge --- it treats a household left a pound short as it treats one left five hundred pounds short --- and is applied to a loss that is itself an upper bound.

Instruments are scored at a common exchequer envelope of \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion as well as at each sponsor's own cost, since comparing a \pounds \genVatCutCostBn{} billion instrument with a \pounds \genRebateCostBn{} billion one measures generosity rather than design. But the envelope must be spent on something an instrument can do: scaling parameters until it is exhausted produces a \genSocialTariffScaledParameterPct{} per cent bill discount, a \pounds \genWhdScaledParameterGbp{} Warm Home Discount and a negative rate of VAT, and a ranking generated at such parameters follows mechanically from paying households more than they lost, so those rows are labelled generically and no conclusion rests on them. The scorecard carries \genEnvelopeRowKindCount{} row types --- at the sponsor's stated cost, at the instrument's own feasible maximum, at a scaled parameter, at widened eligibility with generosity held fixed, and the envelope-capped row, kept separate so that an instrument's own ceiling is not mistaken for the envelope's --- defined in Appendix~\ref{app:envelope}. Comparing the feasible-maximum row against the widened-eligibility row isolates the margin on which an envelope is best spent.

\subsection{Microdata}
\label{sec:data}

All household results run on PolicyEngine UK, an open-source static tax-benefit microsimulation model, on its enhanced Family Resources Survey 2023--24 base: 535{,}080 records grossing to \genTotalHouseholdsM{} million households at 2026. That record count is not a sample of households: it is the FRS sample replicated across a grid of imputation draws, with each survey weight divided across its replicates, so the effective sample is the FRS sample and the count is a property of the imputation design. Three household totals appear in this paper and are not inconsistent: \genTotalHouseholdsM{} million is the grossed population; Table~\ref{tab:decile} sums to \genCoveredHouseholdsM{} million because \genCoverageExcludedHouseholdsM{} million carry non-positive equivalised income; \genTotalHouseholdsMFromMean{} million is the aggregate divided by the mean, which rounds differently.

Variables absent from the FRS are imputed by quantile regression forests from donor surveys: wealth from the Wealth and Assets Survey; consumption from the Living Costs and Food Survey across twelve COICOP categories, with petrol, diesel, gas and electricity carried separately; a VAT expenditure rate from the Effects of Taxes and Benefits dataset; top incomes from the Survey of Personal Incomes. Quantile regression forests preserve the imputed distributions rather than only their means, which matters because the object of interest is dispersion in energy quantities within deciles. The energy variables are calibrated against National Energy Efficiency Data-Framework 2023 kilowatt-hour distributions but carried as annual spending in pounds, so repricing scales a baseline expenditure by a proportional price factor --- equation~\eqref{eq:firstorder} with $q_{hf}\Delta p_f$ read as baseline spend times the proportional price change. Survey weights are re-optimised against 149 administrative targets at national and regional level, which supports the regional aggregation and rules out the seat-level one. Monetary variables are uprated on the OBR's March 2026 Economic and Fiscal Outlook, and baseline prices correspond to the Ofgem Q2 2026 cap.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\paragraph{No price channel.} Energy quantities are inputs, so no shock propagates endogenously: there is no substitution, no induced change in fuel duty and no feedback from prices into any other household variable, and the incidence computed here is arithmetic on a fixed basket rather than a model response. Because three of the five scored instruments do not exist in the model, their costings are this paper's construction from the model's eligibility variables; a reader who disagrees with the parameterisation of the block or the social tariff is disagreeing with this paper, not with PolicyEngine.

\paragraph{Means-tested coverage misses the administrative caseload by a factor of two and a half.} The modelled means-tested population is \genMeansTestedShareHouseholds{} per cent of households, \genMeansTestedHouseholdsM{} million, against a Universal Credit caseload alone of 7.2 million to May 2026; four of the eight means-tested variables resolve but contribute zero households, being legacy benefits essentially fully migrated by the modelling year, so the means test is effectively a four-benefit test. The consequence is one-sided: the reach of the social tariff and the Warm Home Discount expansion is materially understated, their costs understated, and their uncompensated shares correspondingly \emph{overstated}, so those rows are upper bounds rather than estimates. One statistic is deliberately not reported: the share of households losing more than five per cent of net income that sit outside the means-tested system cannot arithmetically fall below about \genLargeLoserCeilingPct{} per cent whatever the incidence of the shock, so it is dropped rather than qualified. The qualitative conclusion --- that a means test conditioned on benefit receipt leaves a large share of losers untouched --- is far more robust than any percentage attached to it.

\paragraph{The gradient is a cross-concept statistic.} Percentages divide by equivalised net income \emph{after} housing costs, while the decile ranking comes from the model's \texttt{household\_income\_decile} variable, which ranks on equivalised \emph{before}-housing-costs income, person-weighted. Agreement with that concept is \genDecileRankAgreementPct{} per cent; with the after-housing-costs concept the burdens use, \genDecileRankAgreementEquivAhcPrecisePct{} per cent. Every decile gradient here is therefore ranked on one income concept and measured on another. The two differ most exactly where the gradient is steepest --- among low-income renters with large housing costs --- so this is not benign noise, and its sign is not determined a priori: attenuation from ranking noise pushes the gradient down, while systematic movement of high-housing-cost households down the after-housing-costs ranking pushes it up. Rebuilding the ranking on the burden concept is the single most useful robustness exercise this paper does not contain.

\paragraph{The energy imputation is wrong in both directions and the errors compound.} Motor fuel is over-imputed, and most at the bottom: \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileOneRawGbp{} of annual spend on the average decile-one household against \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileTenRawGbp{} on the average decile-ten one, an essentially flat profile, where ONS Family Spending for FYE 2025 gives \pounds \genOnsBenchFuelDecileOneGbp{} against \pounds \genOnsBenchFuelDecileTenGbp{} \citep{ons2026familyspending}, annualised at $365.25/7$ weeks. The participation margin shows the mechanism: the microdata give top and bottom deciles an identical share of households recording any motor-fuel spend, where the National Travel Survey finds 40 per cent of the lowest income quintile with no car against 14 per cent of the highest \citep{dft2025nts} --- a table measuring availability rather than access and covering England only, so it bounds the direction of the defect rather than measuring it. Domestic energy runs the other way: mean modelled spend of \pounds \genModelDomesticMeanGbp{} against ONS's \pounds \genOnsBenchDomesticMeanGbp{} and DESNZ's \pounds 1{,}899 for a fixed-consumption dual-fuel bill \citep{desnz2026qep}, roughly a quarter low and below the typical-consumption cap level, which is not a credible place for a population mean. The motor-fuel base is about a quarter too high and the domestic base about a quarter too low, so the motor-fuel \emph{share} of the loss inherits both errors in the same direction. Two ONS-calibrated specifications are therefore carried throughout, one correcting the decile shape of motor fuel and one correcting both levels; both are reweightings onto published aggregates and are illustrative rather than corrected imputations, ONS deciles being unequivalised gross-income deciles.

\paragraph{The means-tested fuel profile is an imputation, and the central policy claim is conditional on it.} Means-tested households are imputed \pounds \genMeansTestedFuelSpendRawGbp{} of annual motor-fuel spending against \pounds \genNonMeansTestedFuelSpendRawGbp{} for everyone else, with \genZeroFuelShareMeansTestedRawPct{} per cent recording none against \genZeroFuelShareOverallRawPct{} per cent overall. Some of that gap is real and some is the same participation defect running the opposite way, and this paper cannot separate the two. A \emph{parity} and a \emph{participation-corrected} specification bound it, moving the means-tested share of the aggregate loss from \genMeansTestedShareOfLossPct{} to \genMeansTestedShareOfLossParityPct{} and \genMeansTestedShareOfLossParticipationPct{} per cent; Section~\ref{sec:policy-eligibility} reports the finding across all three.

\paragraph{The regional profile is not benchmarked.} Region is the finest geography the data supports, but the regional results are validated against no external source and invite the same suspicion as the decile profile: a mean loss in the North East well above Wales and several times London's, in the lowest-income English region, is more plausibly the motor-fuel imputation defect reappearing at regional level than a genuine feature of regional exposure. They are reported as description and nothing is built on them.

\paragraph{No heating-fuel indicator, hence no off-gas-grid split.} There is no primary-heating-fuel or gas-grid-connection variable, so the split \citet{douenne2020} makes central for France cannot be computed. Roughly four million UK properties are off the gas grid, heating oil and LPG sit wholly outside the cap, and these households were most often omitted from 2022 support schemes, so this is a substantive gap rather than a formality.

\paragraph{No general equilibrium.} The model is static and use-side only. \citet{kanzig2023} finds general-equilibrium and indirect effects account for roughly two-thirds of the total response and \citet{goulder2019} that use-side effects are regressive while source-side effects are progressive, so every headline here is a lower bound on total household incidence and an upper bound on the use-side channel conditional on zero substitution. The direction of the omitted component is not guaranteed to reinforce the reported one.

\paragraph{One live policy fact interacts with the scored instruments.} Ofgem's temporary removal of VAT on electricity from 1 October 2026 holds the October cap materially lower than it would otherwise be, and part of the relief the VAT zero-rating instrument is credited with may already sit in the baseline, in which case that row overstates the additional relief available. I flag this rather than adjust for it: disentangling the two would require a cap decomposition Ofgem has not published.

\paragraph{Other model limits.} There is no demand elasticity or pass-through block, so the price path is necessarily constructed outside the model and elasticity enters only through Appendix~\ref{app:elasticity}. VAT enters the household tax concept only as a \emph{delta}: baseline VAT is never deducted from net income, so net income levels are overstated relative to a full indirect-tax concept --- innocuous for the scored VAT reform, which needs only the change, but not for the baseline distribution --- and the VAT imputation rests on an unvalidated 0.38 coverage grossing factor. Alcohol, tobacco, air passenger duty, insurance premium tax and vehicle excise duty are absent, so the indirect-tax base is incomplete. There is no intertemporal margin: households cannot borrow, run down savings or accumulate arrears, which matters because arrears are the most visible manifestation of the 2026 shock. Wealth is imputed from Wealth and Assets Survey round 7 (2018--20), predating the entire rate cycle, which is first-order for any extension in the direction \citet{pallotti2024} indicate. The enhanced microdata require authorised access; code, parameters and aggregate results are public (Appendix~\ref{app:reproduce}).
