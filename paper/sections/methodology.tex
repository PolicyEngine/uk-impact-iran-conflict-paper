\section{Data and methods}
\label{sec:methodology}

The analysis proceeds in five steps. I first map macroeconomic oil and gas scenarios into a path for the Ofgem default tariff cap and a path for pump prices, shocking gas and electricity asymmetrically. I then compute first-order household cost changes as quantity times price change on nationally grossed microdata. Third, I cross the two incidence facts --- losses in pounds and losses as a share of income --- at parliamentary-constituency level. Fourth, I score four competing policy responses as tax-benefit reforms against three reporting metrics. Fifth, I state what the design cannot recover. This section sets out each step, the microdata that carry it, and the limitations that bound the interpretation of every number in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Step 1: from a macro scenario to a household price vector}
\label{sec:step1}

Household energy prices in Great Britain are not market prices. For the roughly four in five households on a default tariff, the price per kilowatt hour is a regulated ceiling set quarterly by Ofgem, and that ceiling is a mechanical function of forward wholesale prices observed in an earlier averaging window, plus network, policy, operating and margin allowances. Any macro-to-micro exercise for the UK must therefore pass through the cap formula rather than through a spot price. I treat the cap as the transmission device and calibrate it in three parts.

\paragraph{Scenarios.} I take three oil and gas paths. The \emph{baseline} follows the NIESR Summer 2026 Outlook, with Brent averaging \$103 per barrel and NBP gas consistent with that path; the \emph{adverse} case follows NIESR's alternative in which Brent reaches \$140 per barrel and gas and oil are sustained roughly 50 per cent above baseline; and the \emph{realised} case uses observed 2026 outturns to date --- UK gas peaking \genGasPeakPence{} pence per therm above its pre-war level and Brent peaking \genOilPeakUsd{} dollars per barrel above it, a \genOilPeakPct{} per cent move --- extended to the end of the modelling horizon at forward-curve levels. Reporting all three is deliberate: the realised path is the policy-relevant object for the Autumn Budget, while the baseline and adverse paths bound it and preserve comparability with the published macro forecasts that motivate the exercise.

\paragraph{Wholesale to cap.} The cap's wholesale allowance is built from forward contracts observed over an averaging window that closes shortly before each quarterly announcement and covers delivery in the subsequent charge period. Mechanically this places a lag of roughly six to nine months between a move in forward wholesale prices and the household bill that reflects it. The wholesale allowance itself is not the whole bill: on recent Ofgem decompositions it accounts for about 40 to 50 per cent of a typical dual-fuel bill, the remainder being network charges, environmental and social obligation costs, operating costs, debt-related allowances and the earnings-before-interest-and-tax margin, none of which respond to a commodity shock at the horizon considered here. The arithmetic I impose is therefore deliberately transparent. A proportional wholesale move of $+X$ per cent enters the cap as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:passthrough}
\Delta \ln p^{\text{cap}}_{f,t} \;=\; \phi_f \, \Delta \ln p^{\text{wholesale}}_{f,t-\ell},
\end{equation}
with a pass-through coefficient $\phi_f \approx 0.45$ for fuel $f$ and a lag $\ell$ of two to three quarters, so that a 20 per cent wholesale increase becomes roughly a 9 per cent cap increase two to three quarters later. Because the cap is reset quarterly and not continuously, I quantise the resulting path into quarterly steps: the annual price faced by a household in the microdata is the consumption-weighted average of the quarterly cap levels prevailing over the modelled year, not the peak. This matters for magnitudes. A shock that is severe but short-lived is substantially attenuated by the combination of the averaging window and the annualisation, and the difference between peak-based and average-based incidence estimates is one of the reasons published bill-rise figures for 2026 differ so widely.

\paragraph{Asymmetric treatment of gas and electricity.} The single modelling choice that makes this a UK paper rather than a generic energy-shock exercise is that gas and electricity are shocked \emph{separately and by different amounts}. Two institutional facts drive this. First, gas is 62 per cent of UK final household energy consumption, the highest share in the G7, so the direct gas channel is unusually large. Second, under British marginal-cost wholesale market design, a gas-fired plant sets the system marginal price roughly 85 per cent of the time, so a gas shock propagates into the electricity cap as well --- but attenuated, because the electricity cap also embeds a much larger share of non-commodity costs (network and policy costs are a substantially larger fraction of an electricity bill than of a gas bill) and because the marginal-pricing share is below one. I therefore apply a fuel-specific pass-through pair $(\phi_{\text{gas}}, \phi_{\text{elec}})$ with $\phi_{\text{elec}} = \psi \, \phi_{\text{gas}}$ and $\psi$ calibrated to the product of the marginal-pricing frequency and the wholesale share of the electricity cap. Symmetric shocking --- the natural default in a model without separate fuel quantities --- would misallocate the burden towards electrically heated and off-gas-grid households and away from the gas-heated majority, and would erase precisely the horizontal variation Section~\ref{sec:results} is built to display. PolicyEngine UK carries \texttt{gas\_consumption} and \texttt{electricity\_consumption} as distinct variables, each calibrated in kilowatt hours, which is what makes the asymmetric treatment implementable at household level.

\paragraph{Pump prices.} Road fuel is treated in parallel but more simply, because retail motor fuel is not capped. I map the Brent path into pump prices through the standard identity that a pump price is the product cost plus refining and retail margin, plus fuel duty at a fixed pence-per-litre rate, with VAT at 20 per cent applied to the whole. Duty being specific rather than ad valorem, the pass-through of a crude move into the pump price is mechanically less than one-for-one, while the VAT layer amplifies it; the realised 2026 outturns of roughly $+20$ per cent for petrol and $+36$ per cent for diesel discipline the calibration and are used directly in the realised scenario. In PolicyEngine UK, household litres are recovered as spending divided by the modelled pump price, so a price path implies both a duty base and an expenditure change without further assumptions.

\subsection{Step 2: first-order incidence}
\label{sec:step2}

Given a household price vector, I compute the cost change for household $h$ as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:firstorder}
\Delta C_h \;=\; \sum_{f} q_{hf} \, \Delta p_{f},
\end{equation}
where $q_{hf}$ is baseline quantity of fuel $f$ and $\Delta p_f$ the modelled price change. This is the first-order approximation to the compensating variation familiar from \citet{deaton1980}: to a first order in prices, the money-metric welfare loss from a price change equals pre-shock quantities times the price change, with the substitution term entering only at second order. Equation~\eqref{eq:firstorder} is therefore an \emph{upper bound} on the true welfare loss, and I state it as such throughout. Households that can shift consumption --- turning down thermostats, driving less, switching heating patterns --- lose less than \eqref{eq:firstorder} implies, and the size of the gap grows with the size of the price change, which makes the bound loosest exactly in the adverse scenario where it matters most.

I do not attempt to close that gap with a demand system. A full QUAIDS specification in the spirit of \citet{banks1997} would in principle deliver price elasticities and hence second-order corrections, but the elasticities are not identified in these data: the energy quantities in the microdata are imputed rather than observed with independent price variation, there is no household-level price variation to exploit under a national cap, and the cross-sectional expenditure variation that identifies Engel curves is not variation in prices. Estimating a demand system on imputed quantities and a single national price would deliver parameters driven by the imputation model rather than by behaviour. I therefore make the zero-elasticity assumption explicit and primitive, report it as a bound, and relegate elasticity variation to the sensitivity analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:elasticity}, where I re-run the pipeline with own-price elasticities in the range used by comparable models --- UKMOD's total cost of ownership routine uses 0.8, and I sweep around it --- to show how far the headline numbers move.

Losses are reported two ways throughout: in pounds per year, and as a percentage of equivalised disposable household income on the households-below-average-income concept, so that the results are directly comparable with the published distributional literature.

\subsection{Step 3: crossing the two incidence facts geographically}
\label{sec:step3}

The distributional literature contains two facts that are usually reported separately and that point in opposite directions. Budget-share analyses find that poor households spend a larger share of income on energy and therefore lose more in percentage terms. \citet{fetzer2024}, matching 22.2 million Energy Performance Certificates to National Energy Efficiency Data-Framework meter records, find the opposite ordering in cash: more affluent areas occupy larger, higher-consumption dwellings and lose more in pounds, which is why the 2022--23 Energy Price Guarantee, a per-unit subsidy, transferred disproportionately to them.

Both statements are true, and the policy interest lies in their intersection. I therefore compute both metrics on the same households and then aggregate them using PolicyEngine UK's 650-constituency weight sets, producing for each Westminster constituency a mean pound loss and a mean loss as a share of income. The paired choropleths in Figure~\ref{fig:maps} are the paper's headline object: to my knowledge no existing UK analysis reports energy-shock incidence at parliamentary-seat level on both metrics simultaneously. I summarise the divergence with the Spearman rank correlation between the two constituency rankings and with the overlap between the two tails --- how many seats appear in both the worst-fifty-in-pounds and the worst-fifty-in-per-cent lists. A low correlation is not a nuisance result; it is the finding that makes the compensation problem in Section~\ref{sec:policy} hard, because a policy calibrated to one ranking necessarily misses the other.

\subsection{Step 4: scoring the policy responses}
\label{sec:step4}

Each candidate response is implemented as a PolicyEngine UK reform and run against the shocked baseline, so that costs and distributional effects are computed inside the same tax-benefit system that generates the counterfactual.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Means-tested social tariff.} A discount on \texttt{domes\-tic\_energy\_consumption} conditioned on receipt of a means-tested benefit or on a passported eligibility test. Implementationally this reuses the existing Energy Price Guarantee subsidy code path in \texttt{gov/treas\-ury/price\_cap\_subsidy/}, which prices against the real Ofgem cap series in \texttt{parameters/gov/ofgem/energy\_price\_cap.yaml}, with an eligibility condition added.
\item \textbf{Universal discounted block (JRF).} A discounted unit rate on the first 50 per cent of typical consumption, plus a per-child allowance, with full-rate pricing above the block. This is a two-tier generalisation of the existing \texttt{monthly\_epg\_consumption\_level} parameter.
\item \textbf{Warm Home Discount expansion.} A parameter change to the existing rebate, taking account of the August 2026 reform that removed the property-cost test and made the payment automatic across England and Wales, extending it to an additional 2.7 million households.
\item \textbf{VAT cut on domestic fuel, 5 per cent to zero.} Implemented through \texttt{reduced\_rate.yaml}. The model's delta-only treatment of VAT (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}) is innocuous here, because only the change in VAT liability is required.
\item \textbf{Flat per-household rebate.} The IPPR network-windfall proposal, a uniform credit of the order of \pounds 183 per household, with the financing side scored separately rather than netted into the household result.
\end{enumerate}

Three metrics are reported for each. First, \emph{cost per pound of bottom-decile gain}: total exchequer cost divided by the aggregate gain accruing to decile one, a targeting efficiency measure. Second, the \emph{share of spend reaching deciles one to three}. Third --- the metric that motivates the exercise --- the \emph{share of losers left uncompensated within each decile}: among households whose energy costs rise, the fraction whose compensation falls short of their loss, computed decile by decile. The first two are between-decile measures and are what most published scorecards report; the third is a within-decile measure, and the literature on horizontal incidence \citep{cronin2019, douenne2020, sallee2019} establishes that within-decile dispersion routinely exceeds between-decile dispersion, so a policy can look well targeted on the first two metrics while leaving a large minority of poor households worse off. PolicyEngine UK's intra-decile impact output supplies this directly.

\subsection{Microdata}
\label{sec:data}

All household results run on PolicyEngine UK, an open-source static tax-benefit microsimulation model of the United Kingdom. Its base dataset is the Family Resources Survey 2023--24. Variables absent from the FRS are imputed by quantile regression forests from donor surveys: wealth from the Wealth and Assets Survey; consumption from the Living Costs and Food Survey across twelve COICOP categories, with petrol, diesel, gas and electricity carried separately; a VAT expenditure rate from the Effects of Taxes and Benefits dataset; top incomes from the Survey of Personal Incomes; and capital gains following Advani and Summers. Quantile regression forests are used rather than conditional-mean imputation so that the imputed distributions, not merely their means, are preserved --- which matters here, because the object of interest is dispersion in energy quantities within income deciles.

Two features of the construction are load-bearing for this paper. First, the LCF-imputed energy quantities are calibrated to National Energy Efficiency Data-Framework 2023 kilowatt-hour distributions, so household gas and electricity are physical quantities rather than deflated spending, and can be repriced under a new cap without double-counting a price change already embedded in the expenditure. Second, survey weights are re-optimised by gradient descent against 1,512 administrative targets, which is what permits the constituency-level aggregation in Step 3. Monetary variables are uprated to the modelling year on the OBR's March 2026 Economic and Fiscal Outlook. Prices in the baseline correspond to the Ofgem Q2 2026 cap.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The design has real and specific limits, and I state them rather than qualifying them.

\paragraph{No general equilibrium.} The model is static and use-side only. \citet{kanzig2023} finds that poorer households lose from energy shocks mainly through income and employment rather than through budget shares, and that general-equilibrium and indirect effects account for roughly two-thirds of the total response. \citet{goulder2019} make the complementary point that use-side effects are regressive while source-side effects are progressive, and that a microsimulation of consumption captures only the use side. Taken together, these imply that the incidence reported here is a component of total incidence, plausibly around a third of it, and that the direction of the omitted component is not guaranteed to reinforce the reported one. Every headline figure in this paper should be read as a lower bound on total household incidence and an upper bound on the use-side channel conditional on zero substitution.

\paragraph{No consumption elasticity or behavioural price response.} PolicyEngine UK has no demand elasticity block and no pass-through module; this is open issue \#1114 in the model repository, which notes that UKMOD's total cost of ownership routine assumes an elasticity of 0.8. The main specification is therefore zero-elasticity by construction, and elasticity enters only as the robustness check in Appendix~\ref{app:elasticity}. Relatedly, the model contains no price block at all: CPI, CPIH and RPI exist only as uprating indices, so the price path in Step 1 is constructed outside the model and injected.

\paragraph{VAT enters only as a delta.} The model's \texttt{household\_tax} concept includes \texttt{vat\_change} but not \texttt{vat}. Baseline VAT is never deducted from net income, so levels of net income are overstated relative to a full indirect-tax concept, and only reforms to VAT --- not the baseline burden --- affect the distribution. Fuel duty and the carbon tax module do enter at level, so the treatment across indirect taxes is inconsistent. For the VAT reform scored in Step 4 only the delta is required, so the reform result is unaffected; the baseline distribution is affected.

\paragraph{The VAT coverage factor.} The VAT imputation rests on a 0.38 coverage grossing factor, following IFS TAXBEN, applied on top of a four-predictor imputation from the Effects of Taxes and Benefits dataset. The factor is not independently validated; it is open issue \#352.

\paragraph{Missing duties.} Alcohol, tobacco, air passenger duty, insurance premium tax and vehicle excise duty are absent from the model. This matters less for an energy shock than it would for a general indirect-tax study, but it means the reported indirect-tax base is incomplete and the compensation metrics do not net out indirect-tax changes outside energy.

\paragraph{Static, no lifecycle.} There is no intertemporal margin: households cannot borrow against a temporary shock, run down savings, or accumulate arrears. Given that energy arrears are the most visible real-world manifestation of the 2026 shock (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), this is a substantive omission rather than a formality.

\paragraph{Data vintages.} Wealth is imputed from Wealth and Assets Survey round 7, covering 2018--20, which predates both the 2022 shock and the subsequent rate cycle; \citet{pallotti2024} show that nominal net asset positions rather than energy baskets drive most household heterogeneity in the euro-area response, so a stale wealth distribution is a first-order concern for any extension in that direction. Benefit take-up is modelled as a scalar rather than a behavioural model.

\paragraph{Constituency weights are synthetic.} The 650-constituency weight sets are re-optimisations of roughly 20,000 sampled households against area-level administrative targets. They are not independent area samples: no constituency contains 650th-of-the-sample genuinely local observations, and within-constituency dispersion is inherited from the national sample conditioned on the targets. Constituency results should be read as the model's best estimate of what a constituency of a given demographic and housing composition looks like, not as a survey of that constituency. Rankings across seats are more reliable than levels within any one seat.

\paragraph{Access.} PolicyEngine UK's enhanced microdata are held in a private Hugging Face repository requiring an access token, so the full pipeline is not reproducible without credentials, although the code, parameters and aggregate results in this repository are public.
