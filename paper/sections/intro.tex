\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

On 28 February 2026 the United States and Israel began sustained strikes on Iran, and within weeks traffic through the Strait of Hormuz was interrupted at intervals. The commodity response was large but not unprecedented. Brent peaked some \$42 a barrel, or 57 per cent, above its pre-war level---comparable to the \$35 increase of 2022---while UK natural gas peaked 78 pence per therm above pre-war, roughly a quarter of the 2022 move. Retail fuel followed, with petrol up 20 per cent and diesel up 36 per cent. Both the International Monetary Fund and the OECD cut their 2026 growth forecasts for the United Kingdom by half a percentage point, the largest markdown applied to any G7 economy.

That the UK should take the sharpest hit is not an accident of forecasting judgement. It follows from three structural features of the British energy system.

First, gas accounts for 62 per cent of final household energy consumption \citep{desnz2025ecuk}, the highest share among the European members of the G7 --- Italy, the next highest, is at 53 per cent. I state it that way rather than as a G7 ranking because no published source ranks household gas shares across the G7 as a whole: the comparable Eurostat series covers Europe and excludes the United States, Canada and Japan \citep{eurostat2018}. A wholesale gas move therefore passes into British domestic bills with unusually little dilution.

Second, gas-fired plant is the marginal generator in the British electricity market roughly 85 per cent of the time, so the same wholesale move arrives a second time through the electricity bill of households that use no gas at all---including, disproportionately, low-income households in electrically heated flats and off-gas-grid rural households.

Third, the retail price cap transmits wholesale prices mechanically but with a lag of roughly one and a half quarters, and wholesale costs make up roughly 40 to 50 per cent of a dual-fuel bill, so the household consequences of a spring 2026 shock land squarely on the winter 2026--27 heating season and on the fiscal year that the Autumn Budget will set. Ofgem confirmed an October cap of \pounds 1{,}723 on 26 August 2026 \citep{ofgem2026cap}, on the reduced typical-consumption basis that took effect on 1 July 2026 \citep{ofgem2026tdcv}, superseding Cornwall Insight's \pounds 1{,}729 forecast \citep{cornwall2026}. The Resolution Foundation estimates that a sustained return to peak prices would leave British households spending an extra \pounds 11 billion on energy and road fuel in calendar 2026 \citep{rf2026outlook}, and that real income growth turns negative for the median working-age household \citep{brewer2026}.

The policy question is live rather than hypothetical. The Chancellor has ruled out universal support of the Energy Price Guarantee kind---at roughly \pounds 20 billion a year, and regressive in cash terms, it is regarded in the Treasury as unrepeatable---and, as trailed in press reporting rather than in any published Treasury document, is said to favour an income-based social tariff instead. I have found no HM Treasury or Budget paper stating that intention, and treat it as a reported signal rather than as policy.

The Joseph Rowntree Foundation, joined by the TUC, counters with a universal discounted block \citep{jrf2026}: the first half of typical consumption sold at a discounted rate, with a per-child allowance, at a cost near \pounds 5 billion, which it argues fully offsets the cost increase for the bottom three deciles and can actually be delivered by winter 2026, which a means-tested social tariff cannot. The statistic that anchors every submission to the Budget is that around 40 per cent of households struggling to heat their homes are not on means-tested benefits. If that figure is right, the distinction between the two proposals is not a matter of generosity but of coverage: a perfectly targeted transfer conditioned on benefit receipt misses two in five of the households the policy is meant to reach. The Warm Home Discount reform reported for August 2026---\pounds 150, made automatic in England and Wales with the property-cost test scrapped, said to add 2.7 million households---would move in the direction of coverage without settling the question. Here too the description rests on press reporting: I have located no published Government document setting out the reform in those terms, and it is stated as reported rather than as fact. The Autumn Budget 2026 decides it.

Answering that question requires evidence of a kind that does not currently exist for the United Kingdom. The macroeconomic forecasters---NIESR, the OBR, the IMF---produce scenario paths for oil, gas, GDP and inflation but stop at the aggregate. The distributional analysts---the IFS, the Resolution Foundation, JRF---produce household estimates but take the price path as a stipulated assumption rather than deriving it from a macroeconomic scenario, and report results by income decile, which is the geography-free summary least useful to a Parliament that votes by seat. Nobody has joined the two.

\subsection*{What identifies the shock: a solved pre-war cap}

Everything in this paper turns on a counterfactual: what a British household would have paid for energy in the twelve months from March 2026 had the war not happened. That counterfactual cannot be read off a published cap, and the central methodological point of this paper is why.

Ofgem's cap is a mechanical function of forward wholesale prices observed in a window that closes about seven weeks before the charge period begins. The war began on 28 February 2026. The observation window behind the July 2026 cap of \pounds 1{,}663 therefore already contained it. Treating that cap as the pre-shock baseline --- while simultaneously assigning the same quarter full war pass-through, as any phase-in profile aligned on the shock date must --- subtracts part of the shock from itself. The domestic leg is understated at source, and because the motor-fuel leg is not, the motor-fuel share of the loss is inflated by exactly the amount the domestic leg loses.

The pre-war cap is therefore \emph{solved} rather than observed. Two published caps and the cap formula are enough to identify it exactly. July 2026 (\pounds 1{,}663) and October 2026 (\pounds 1{,}723, plus roughly \pounds 45 of electricity-VAT relief unrelated to the war) each equal the pre-war level scaled by the war pass-through that had reached its own observation window; two equations, two unknowns --- the pre-war level and the sustained fraction of the wholesale gas peak. The solution is a pre-war counterfactual cap of \pounds \genCapBaselineLevel{}, and it reproduces both published caps to floating point rather than approximately. Section~\ref{sec:step1} sets out the solve, and Appendix~\ref{app:domesticleg} sweeps what it does and does not pin.

Two things follow that a reader should have before any number in this paper.

The first is that the phase-in profile now builds each cap period's actual forward-curve observation window rather than sliding a linear ramp along a calendar. That is what makes the one-and-a-half-quarter lag an institutional fact about Ofgem's methodology rather than a fitted parameter, and it is why the cap-lag sweep of Appendix~\ref{app:caplag} is now a measurement rather than an identity.

The second is a correction to the paper's own central claim, and it is large enough to state before the results. The solved gas sustained fraction is \genGasSustainedFraction{} per cent. It now sits \emph{above} the pump fraction of \genPumpSustainedFraction{} per cent, where on the defective baseline it came out at roughly a fifth --- a third of the pump fraction --- because the solve was absorbing the baseline error. The motor-fuel share of the loss depends almost entirely on the ratio of those two fractions. The large gas-pump asymmetry that generated a comfortable motor-fuel majority across earlier drafts was therefore substantially an artefact of the baseline defect, not a feature of how the two channels transmit. Motor fuel remains the largest single channel, at \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent, but it is no longer a large majority and this paper does not present it as one.

\subsection*{Contributions}

\paragraph{A pipeline.} I map macroeconomic scenarios---NIESR's baseline of \$103 a barrel and its adverse \$140 case \citep{niesr2026spring,niesr2026summer}---into an Ofgem cap path and a pump-price path, quantised into quarterly steps and lagged mechanically, and then pass that price vector through PolicyEngine UK's microsimulation of the 2023--24 Family Resources Survey, fused with the Living Costs and Food Survey for twelve COICOP consumption categories and with gas, electricity, petrol and diesel as separately calibrated variables. Gas and electricity are shocked asymmetrically, because the electricity price is gas-set most of the time but the pass-through coefficient and the lag differ across the two fuels. First-order incidence follows Deaton's approximation---the change in cost is the pre-shock quantity times the change in price---which, absent substitution, is an explicit upper bound on the compensating variation for a given price path.

Three specification choices are stated at the outset because everything downstream depends on them.

The first is the \emph{window}. Both legs of the shock --- the domestic cap leg and the pump-price leg --- are annualised over one named window, the twelve months from the shock, March 2026 to February 2027. Calendar 2026 is retained separately, and only so that the Resolution Foundation's \pounds 11 billion can be compared on its own window.

The second is the \emph{accounting basis}: the annual domestic price is the consumption-weighted average of the quarterly cap levels prevailing over the modelled window rather than the steady-state level the shock eventually reaches, with the steady-state alternative reported throughout as a labelled variant.

The third is the \emph{denominator}: every percentage of income divides by equivalised household net income after housing costs on the HBAI scale, which is the concept the published distributional literature uses; the unequivalised gradient is reported once, as a robustness line.

In the main specification the mean annual household loss is \pounds \genCentralLossMean{}, or \genCentralLossPctIncome{} per cent of equivalised disposable income, and the aggregate is \pounds \genAggSpendRealisedBn{} billion; on the steady-state basis the same figures are \pounds \genSteadyStateLossMean{} and \pounds \genSteadyStateAggBn{} billion. The domestic leg carries \pounds \genDomesticMeanLossGbp{} of the mean loss and motor fuel \pounds \genMotorFuelMeanLossGbp{}. The solved cap path puts the October step at \genCapAnchorMovePct{} per cent, with July already carrying \genCapBaseMovePct{} per cent of the war --- which is the fact the old baseline discarded.

The external check on the aggregate is the Resolution Foundation's independently produced estimate that a sustained return to peak prices adds \pounds 11 billion to household energy and road-fuel spending in calendar 2026 \citep{rf2026outlook}. That is the same object and the same population as mine, but not the same window. Section~\ref{sec:results-aggregate} therefore re-runs \emph{both} legs on calendar 2026, so that the damped and peak-fuel specifications bracket the Resolution Foundation's figure on a single window --- \pounds \genCalendarAggBn{} billion to \pounds \genPeakFuelCalendarAggBn{} billion around their \pounds 11 billion. A bracket assembled from two different windows, as in earlier drafts, was not a comparison at all.

\paragraph{The composition of the loss.} Motor fuel carries \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate loss in the main specification, against \genGasShareOfLoss{} per cent through gas and \genElecShareOfLoss{} per cent through electricity. That is the largest single channel, and it is a bare majority rather than a dominant one. Across the seven specifications in Section~\ref{sec:results-fuel} the share ranges from \genSpecFuelShareMinPct{} to \genSpecFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent, and it moves between \genDomesticLegNbpFuelShareMinPct{} and \genDomesticLegNbpFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent on the pre-war gas price assumption alone. Damping the gas and pump peaks by a common fraction gives \genSymDampMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent; combining that with the steady-state basis gives \genSymDampSteadyMotorFuelShare{} per cent and with ONS-consistent spending levels \genSymDampOnsLevelsMotorFuelShare{} per cent.

The claim this paper defends is therefore the one that holds across the range: motor fuel carries a large part of the loss on every calibration, between a third and two-thirds of it, and the point estimate is not offered as settled. What survives the whole range is the policy point. Every instrument now under discussion---the social tariff, the JRF discounted block, the expanded Warm Home Discount, VAT zero-rating---operates on the domestic bill, and on every calibration a substantial part of the loss runs through a channel none of them touches. That is a constraint of construction rather than of design: when these options are scored below and found wanting, the channel they act on binds before the precision with which they act does.

\paragraph{Distribution.} The third contribution turns on crossing two facts that the literature has established separately but never displayed together. \citet{fetzer2024} show, using 22.2 million Energy Performance Certificates matched to meter-level consumption records, that more affluent English neighbourhoods are more exposed in \emph{pounds}, because they occupy larger and less efficient homes---which is why the Energy Price Guarantee, a subsidy proportional to consumption, benefited them disproportionately. The budget-share literature shows with equal firmness that poorer households lose more in \emph{per cent}, because energy is a necessity with a falling Engel curve. Both are true, and a policy debate that quotes only one of them is conducted at cross purposes.

I show them simultaneously on the same households. In per cent the gradient is steep, \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent in the bottom decile against \genDecileTenLossPct{} per cent in the top. In pounds it is not monotone at all: the largest cash loss falls on decile eight, at \pounds \genDecileEightLossGbp{}, above decile ten's \pounds \genDecileTenLossGbp{} and well above decile one's \pounds \genDecileOneLossGbp{}. Vehicle ownership and mileage, not dwelling efficiency, drive the cash profile, and both peak below the top of the income distribution.

The \emph{direction} of the percentage gradient is robust: the burden falls in income under every specification in the paper. Its \emph{magnitude} is not, and it is reported three ways rather than one. The headline ratio is \genDecileRatioPct{} to one. Measured from decile \emph{two} to decile ten --- which removes the decile in which a fifth of households have non-positive equivalised income --- it is \genDecileRatioFromDecileTwoPct{} to one. Winsorising the burden distribution gives \genDecileRatioWinsorisedPct{} and \genDecileRatioFromDecileTwoWinsorisedPct{} to one on the two bases respectively. The direction is robust across all four; the magnitude varies by a factor of more than two, and no claim in this paper rests on which of the four a reader prefers.

Two measurement problems bear on the magnitude, neither of which this paper resolves. The first is the numerator: the microdata impute an almost flat motor-fuel spending profile across deciles --- \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileOneRawGbp{} at the bottom against \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileTenRawGbp{} at the top, where ONS Family Spending gives \pounds \genOnsBenchFuelDecileOneGbp{} against \pounds \genOnsBenchFuelDecileTenGbp{} \citep{ons2026familyspending} --- and give the top and bottom deciles identical fuel-purchasing rates where the National Travel Survey's household car \emph{availability} table finds 40 per cent of the poorest quintile without a car against 14 per cent of the richest \citep{dft2025nts}. That table covers England only, while the model is UK-wide, so it bounds the direction of the defect rather than measuring it exactly.

The second is the denominator, and it has two parts. On the equivalised after-housing-costs concept, \genDecileOneZeroIncomeShare{} per cent of decile-one households have zero or negative income, and the \genCoverageExcludedHouseholdsM{} million households (\genCoverageExcludedSharePct{} per cent of the total) that fall outside deciles one to ten carry the model's \(-1\) sentinel for negative income. Percentage statistics drop them and report the dropped weight. The other part is a cross-concept problem and it is a limitation rather than a detail: the burdens are measured on equivalised \emph{after}-housing-costs income, but the decile ranking is built on equivalised \emph{before}-housing-costs income, person-weighted. The two concepts agree \genDecileRankAgreementPct{} per cent of the time on the ranking's own person-weighted basis and only \genDecileRankAgreementEquivAhcPrecisePct{} per cent against the burden denominator. The gradient in this paper is therefore ranked on one income concept and measured on another, and it is reported as such wherever it appears.

\paragraph{Policy scoring.} Decile-average tables are the wrong summary for this shock, though the quantitative form of that claim needs care: the mean within-decile p90--p10 range is \genWithinDecileRangePct{} percentage points against an interdecile range across decile means of \genBetweenDecileRangePct{} points, but the comparison is carried by decile one alone. Excluding it the mean within-decile range is \genWithinDecileRangeExDOnePct{} points, below the between-decile range. The qualitative case for within-decile reporting rests on \citet{cronin2019} and \citet{sallee2019} rather than on that inequality, and it is made that way here. I therefore report the share of losing households left uncompensated \emph{within} each decile alongside two continuous measures: the share of the aggregate loss offset, and the mean and median residual loss.

The central finding is that an exchequer envelope buys far more when it is spent on eligibility than on generosity. Held at their \emph{feasible} maxima the social tariff and the Warm Home Discount offset \genSocialTariffEnvelopeOffsetPct{} and \genWhdEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss, because there is nothing left to give the population they can see; spending the same envelope on \emph{eligibility} at each sponsor's own generosity offsets \genSocialTariffEligibilityOffsetPct{} and \genWhdEligibilityOffsetPct{} per cent. Saturation is in fact complete at the sponsor's own parameter: raising the social tariff's discount from 35 to 100 per cent moves the offset from \genSocialTariffStatedOffsetPct{} to \genSocialTariffCappedOffsetPct{} per cent. At that feasible maximum the instrument pays about \pounds \genSocialTariffCappedGainPerEligibleGbp{} to each eligible household against a mean loss among them of \pounds \genMeansTestedMeanLossGbp{}, so it plainly overcompensates and no claim to the contrary is made here.

That finding is conditional on an imputation this paper cannot validate, and the condition is not small. Means-tested households are imputed \pounds \genMeansTestedFuelSpendRawGbp{} of annual motor-fuel spending against \pounds \genNonMeansTestedFuelSpendRawGbp{} for everyone else --- a factor of \genMeansTestedFuelRatioRaw{} --- with \genZeroFuelShareMeansTestedRawPct{} per cent recording no motor fuel at all against \genZeroFuelShareOverallRawPct{} per cent of households overall. Under a parity specification, which gives means-tested households the population's own fuel profile, their share of the aggregate loss rises from \genMeansTestedShareOfLossPct{} to \genMeansTestedShareOfLossParityPct{} per cent, and the eligibility-over-generosity advantage falls from \genTargetingMultipleStated{} times to \genTargetingMultipleUnderParity{}. A participation-corrected specification gives \genMeansTestedShareOfLossParticipationPct{} per cent. The direction of the finding survives all three; the factor does not, and the range rather than the point is what this paper asks to be quoted on.

\paragraph{What this paper does not do.} It contains no general-equilibrium analysis. \citet{kanzig2023} shows that the bulk of the distributional consequence of an energy shock reaches poorer households through income and employment rather than through budget shares, with general-equilibrium and indirect effects accounting for roughly two-thirds of the total response; a static consumption-side microsimulation therefore captures at most a third of true incidence. \citet{goulder2019} makes the complementary point that use-side effects are regressive while source-side effects are progressive, and that a microsimulation of the kind conducted here observes only the use side. \citet{pallotti2024} add that in the euro area most household-level heterogeneity in the 2021--23 episode was driven by nominal net asset positions rather than by the energy basket at all. I do not model consumption substitution---PolicyEngine UK has no demand elasticity or pass-through block---nor do I deduct baseline VAT from net income, since the model carries VAT only as a policy delta. Each of these is stated at the point of use and revisited in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
