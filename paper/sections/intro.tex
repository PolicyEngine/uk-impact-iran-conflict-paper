\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

On 28 February 2026 the United States and Israel began sustained strikes on Iran, and within weeks traffic through the Strait of Hormuz was interrupted at intervals. The commodity response was large but not unprecedented. Brent peaked some \$42 a barrel, or 57 per cent, above its pre-war level---comparable to the \$35 increase of 2022---while UK natural gas peaked 78 pence per therm above pre-war, roughly a quarter of the 2022 move. Retail fuel followed, with petrol up 20 per cent and diesel up 36 per cent. Both the International Monetary Fund and the OECD cut their 2026 growth forecasts for the United Kingdom by half a percentage point, the largest markdown applied to any G7 economy.

That the UK should take the sharpest hit is not an accident of forecasting judgement. It follows from three structural features of the British energy system. First, gas accounts for 62 per cent of final household energy consumption, the highest share in the G7, so a wholesale gas move passes into domestic bills with unusually little dilution. Second, gas-fired plant is the marginal generator in the British electricity market roughly 85 per cent of the time, so the same wholesale move arrives a second time through the electricity bill of households that use no gas at all---including, disproportionately, low-income households in electrically heated flats and off-gas-grid rural households. Third, the retail price cap transmits wholesale prices mechanically but with a lag of two to three quarters, and wholesale costs make up roughly 40 to 50 per cent of a dual-fuel bill, so the household consequences of a spring 2026 shock land squarely on the winter 2026--27 heating season and on the fiscal year that the Autumn Budget will set. Cornwall Insight's August 2026 forecast put the October cap at \pounds 1{,}729, four per cent higher; the Resolution Foundation estimates that a typical working-age household will be \pounds 480 worse off in 2026--27, with real income growth for the poorest fifth cut from 2.8 to 1.2 per cent and median growth turning negative.

The policy question is live rather than hypothetical. The Chancellor has ruled out universal support of the Energy Price Guarantee kind---at roughly \pounds 20 billion a year, and regressive in cash terms, it is regarded in the Treasury as unrepeatable---and has signalled instead an income-based social tariff. The Joseph Rowntree Foundation, joined by the TUC, counters with a universal discounted block: the first half of typical consumption sold at a discounted rate, with a per-child allowance, at a cost near \pounds 5 billion, which it argues fully offsets the cost increase for the bottom three deciles and can actually be delivered by winter 2026, which a means-tested social tariff cannot. The statistic that anchors every submission to the Budget is that around 40 per cent of households struggling to heat their homes are not on means-tested benefits. If that figure is right, the distinction between the two proposals is not a matter of generosity but of coverage: a perfectly targeted transfer conditioned on benefit receipt misses two in five of the households the policy is meant to reach. The August 2026 reform of the Warm Home Discount---\pounds 150, now automatic in England and Wales with the property-cost test scrapped, adding 2.7 million households---moves in the direction of coverage without settling the question. The Autumn Budget 2026 decides it.

Answering that question requires evidence of a kind that does not currently exist for the United Kingdom. The macroeconomic forecasters---NIESR, the OBR, the IMF---produce scenario paths for oil, gas, GDP and inflation but stop at the aggregate. The distributional analysts---the IFS, the Resolution Foundation, JRF---produce household estimates but take the price path as a stipulated assumption rather than deriving it from a macroeconomic scenario, and report results by income decile, which is the geography-free summary least useful to a Parliament that votes by seat. Nobody has joined the two.

This paper makes four contributions. The first is a pipeline. I map macroeconomic scenarios---NIESR's baseline of \$103 a barrel and its adverse \$140 case---into an Ofgem cap path and a pump-price path, quantised into quarterly steps and lagged mechanically, and then pass that price vector through PolicyEngine UK's microsimulation of the 2023--24 Family Resources Survey, fused with the Living Costs and Food Survey for twelve COICOP consumption categories and with gas, electricity, petrol and diesel as separately calibrated variables. Gas and electricity are shocked asymmetrically, because the electricity price is gas-set most of the time but the pass-through coefficient and the lag differ across the two fuels; I show in Section~\ref{sec:results} that this refines the composition of the loss without moving the distributional result, and report that honestly rather than claiming more for it. First-order incidence follows Deaton's approximation---the change in cost is the pre-shock quantity times the change in price---which, absent substitution, is an explicit upper bound on the compensating variation for a given price path. In the central scenario the mean annual household loss is \pounds \genCentralLossMean{}, or \genCentralLossPctIncome{} per cent of equivalised disposable income, and the aggregate is \pounds \genAggSpendRealisedBn{} billion. That mean sits close to the Resolution Foundation's independently produced \pounds 480 for a typical working-age household, which is a useful external check on a pipeline whose components have not previously been joined.

The second contribution is the composition of the loss, and it reframes the policy debate. \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate loss arrives through motor fuel; only \genGasShareOfLoss{} per cent arrives through gas and \genElecShareOfLoss{} per cent through electricity. This is not a domestic-energy-bill shock with a road-fuel footnote. It is mainly a pump-price shock. The implication for the Budget is immediate and, so far as I can establish, has not been made: every instrument now under discussion---the social tariff, the JRF discounted block, the expanded Warm Home Discount, VAT zero-rating---operates on the domestic bill and can therefore reach at most about a third of the loss \emph{by construction}, whatever its targeting properties. When these options are scored below and found wanting, the binding constraint is the channel they act on, not the precision with which they act.

The third contribution is distributional, and turns on crossing two facts that the literature has established separately but never displayed together. \citet{fetzer2024} show, using 22.2 million Energy Performance Certificates matched to meter-level consumption records, that more affluent English neighbourhoods are more exposed in \emph{pounds}, because they occupy larger and less efficient homes---which is why the Energy Price Guarantee, a subsidy proportional to consumption, benefited them disproportionately. The budget-share literature shows with equal firmness that poorer households lose more in \emph{per cent}, because energy is a necessity with a falling Engel curve. Both are true, and a policy debate that quotes only one of them is conducted at cross purposes. I show them simultaneously on the same households. In per cent the gradient is steep and monotone at the ends, \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent in the bottom decile against \genDecileTenLossPct{} per cent in the top. In pounds it is not monotone at all: the largest cash loss falls on decile eight, at \pounds \genDecileEightLossGbp{}, above decile ten's \pounds \genDecileTenLossGbp{} and well above decile one's \pounds \genDecileOneLossGbp{}. The motor-fuel share explains the shape---vehicle ownership and mileage, not dwelling efficiency, drive the cash profile, and both peak below the top of the income distribution. The obvious objection to the percentage gradient is that it is manufactured by small denominators in the bottom decile. It is not. The gradient survives on medians, where decile one loses \genDecileOneMedianLossPct{} per cent against decile ten's \genDecileTenMedianLossPct{} per cent, a ratio slightly \emph{wider} than on aggregate ratios; decile one's median equivalised income is \pounds \genDecileOneMedianIncomeGbp{}, and only \genDecileOneZeroIncomeShare{} per cent of the decile has zero or negative income. The geographic cut is the region, of which there are twelve: parliamentary-constituency estimates are not available on this microdata, for reasons set out in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

The fourth contribution is the count that determines whether a compensation package actually works, and which decile-average tables cannot show: the share of losing households left uncompensated \emph{within} each decile under each proposal. Within-decile dispersion of the burden exceeds the between-decile gradient---the interdecile range across decile means is \genBetweenDecileRangePct{} percentage points against a mean within-decile range of \genWithinDecileRangePct{} points---so the metric is not a refinement but the substance. Under the means-tested social tariff \genSocialTariffUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losers remain uncompensated; under the JRF universal block the figure is the same, \genJrfUncompensatedShare{} per cent. The two proposals differ in \emph{who} they reach, not in how many they leave short. Only the flat per-household rebate compensates a majority of losers, at \genRebateFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent fully compensated, and it does so at the highest cost, \pounds \genRebateCostBn{} billion; universality outperforming targeting here speaks directly to \citet{levell2025} and against the grey-literature consensus that support should always be targeted.

It is as important to say what this paper does not do. It contains no general-equilibrium analysis. \citet{kanzig2023} shows that the bulk of the distributional consequence of an energy shock reaches poorer households through income and employment rather than through budget shares, with general-equilibrium and indirect effects accounting for roughly two-thirds of the total response; a static consumption-side microsimulation therefore captures at most a third of true incidence. \citet{goulder2019} makes the complementary point that use-side effects are regressive while source-side effects are progressive, and that a microsimulation of the kind conducted here observes only the use side. \citet{pallotti2024} add that in the euro area most household-level heterogeneity in the 2021--23 episode was driven by nominal net asset positions rather than by the energy basket at all. I do not model consumption substitution---PolicyEngine UK has no demand elasticity or pass-through block---nor do I deduct baseline VAT from net income, since the model carries VAT only as a policy delta. Each of these is stated at the point of use and revisited in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, where I argue that two of them---UK cap-era wholesale-to-retail pass-through, and the absence of any British counterpart to \citet{douenne2020} on off-gas-grid horizontal losers---are better understood as the next papers than as apologies for this one.
