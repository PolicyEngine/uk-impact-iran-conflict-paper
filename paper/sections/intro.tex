\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

On 28 February 2026 the United States and Israel began sustained strikes on Iran, and within weeks traffic through the Strait of Hormuz was interrupted at intervals. The commodity response was large but not unprecedented. Brent peaked some \$42 a barrel, or 57 per cent, above its pre-war level---comparable to the \$35 increase of 2022---while UK natural gas peaked 78 pence per therm above pre-war, roughly a quarter of the 2022 move. Retail fuel followed, with petrol up 20 per cent and diesel up 36 per cent. Both the International Monetary Fund and the OECD cut their 2026 growth forecasts for the United Kingdom by half a percentage point, the largest markdown applied to any G7 economy.

That the UK should take the sharpest hit is not an accident of forecasting judgement. It follows from three structural features of the British energy system. First, gas accounts for 62 per cent of final household energy consumption \citep{desnz2025ecuk}, the highest share among the European members of the G7 --- Italy, the next highest, is at 53 per cent. I state it that way rather than as a G7 ranking because no published source ranks household gas shares across the G7 as a whole: the comparable Eurostat series covers Europe and excludes the United States, Canada and Japan \citep{eurostat2018}. A wholesale gas move therefore passes into British domestic bills with unusually little dilution. Second, gas-fired plant is the marginal generator in the British electricity market roughly 85 per cent of the time, so the same wholesale move arrives a second time through the electricity bill of households that use no gas at all---including, disproportionately, low-income households in electrically heated flats and off-gas-grid rural households. Third, the retail price cap transmits wholesale prices mechanically but with a lag of roughly one and a half quarters, and wholesale costs make up roughly 40 to 50 per cent of a dual-fuel bill, so the household consequences of a spring 2026 shock land squarely on the winter 2026--27 heating season and on the fiscal year that the Autumn Budget will set. Cornwall Insight's August 2026 forecast put the October cap at \pounds 1{,}729 \citep{cornwall2026}; Ofgem then confirmed \pounds 1{,}723 on 26 August 2026 \citep{ofgem2026cap}, on the reduced typical-consumption basis that took effect on 1 July 2026 \citep{ofgem2026tdcv}, and it is the confirmed figure rather than the forecast that this paper's domestic leg is calibrated to. The Resolution Foundation estimates that a sustained return to peak prices would leave British households spending an extra \pounds 11 billion on energy and road fuel in calendar 2026 \citep{rf2026outlook}, and that real income growth turns negative for the median working-age household \citep{brewer2026}.

The policy question is live rather than hypothetical. The Chancellor has ruled out universal support of the Energy Price Guarantee kind---at roughly \pounds 20 billion a year, and regressive in cash terms, it is regarded in the Treasury as unrepeatable---and, as trailed in press reporting rather than in any published Treasury document, is said to favour an income-based social tariff instead. I have found no HM Treasury or Budget paper stating that intention, and treat it as a reported signal rather than as policy. The Joseph Rowntree Foundation, joined by the TUC, counters with a universal discounted block \citep{jrf2026}: the first half of typical consumption sold at a discounted rate, with a per-child allowance, at a cost near \pounds 5 billion, which it argues fully offsets the cost increase for the bottom three deciles and can actually be delivered by winter 2026, which a means-tested social tariff cannot. The statistic that anchors every submission to the Budget is that around 40 per cent of households struggling to heat their homes are not on means-tested benefits. If that figure is right, the distinction between the two proposals is not a matter of generosity but of coverage: a perfectly targeted transfer conditioned on benefit receipt misses two in five of the households the policy is meant to reach. The Warm Home Discount reform reported for August 2026---\pounds 150, made automatic in England and Wales with the property-cost test scrapped, said to add 2.7 million households---would move in the direction of coverage without settling the question. Here too the description rests on press reporting: I have located no published Government document setting out the reform in those terms, and it is stated as reported rather than as fact. The Autumn Budget 2026 decides it.

Answering that question requires evidence of a kind that does not currently exist for the United Kingdom. The macroeconomic forecasters---NIESR, the OBR, the IMF---produce scenario paths for oil, gas, GDP and inflation but stop at the aggregate. The distributional analysts---the IFS, the Resolution Foundation, JRF---produce household estimates but take the price path as a stipulated assumption rather than deriving it from a macroeconomic scenario, and report results by income decile, which is the geography-free summary least useful to a Parliament that votes by seat. Nobody has joined the two.

This paper makes four contributions. The first is a pipeline. I map macroeconomic scenarios---NIESR's baseline of \$103 a barrel and its adverse \$140 case \citep{niesr2026spring,niesr2026summer}---into an Ofgem cap path and a pump-price path, quantised into quarterly steps and lagged mechanically, and then pass that price vector through PolicyEngine UK's microsimulation of the 2023--24 Family Resources Survey, fused with the Living Costs and Food Survey for twelve COICOP consumption categories and with gas, electricity, petrol and diesel as separately calibrated variables. Gas and electricity are shocked asymmetrically, because the electricity price is gas-set most of the time but the pass-through coefficient and the lag differ across the two fuels; I show in Section~\ref{sec:results} that this refines the composition of the loss without moving the distributional result, and report that honestly rather than claiming more for it. First-order incidence follows Deaton's approximation---the change in cost is the pre-shock quantity times the change in price---which, absent substitution, is an explicit upper bound on the compensating variation for a given price path. Three specification choices are stated at the outset because everything downstream depends on them. The first is the window. Both legs of the shock --- the domestic cap leg and the pump-price leg --- are annualised over one named window, the twelve months from the shock, March 2026 to February 2027. An earlier version annualised the two legs over different windows and labelled the sum ``2026'', which both mis-stated the year and mechanically inflated the motor-fuel share; every annual figure in this paper now refers to that single window and says so. Calendar 2026 is retained separately, and only so that the Resolution Foundation's \pounds 11 billion can be compared on its own window. The second is the accounting basis: the annual domestic price is the consumption-weighted average of the quarterly cap levels prevailing over the modelled window rather than the steady-state level the shock eventually reaches, which is what Step 1 of the method describes and is now what the code does, with the steady-state alternative reported throughout as a labelled variant. The cap lag that places that profile on the calendar is one and a half quarters, derived from Ofgem's own observation window --- which closes about seven weeks before the charge period and covers roughly the preceding quarter --- rather than assumed; three quarters is swept in Appendix~\ref{app:caplag}, and across lags of one to four quarters the mean loss moves by \pounds \genCapLagRangeGbp{}, a small fraction of the central figure. The gas leg's sustained fraction is likewise no longer written down but solved, so that the modelled October cap reproduces Ofgem's confirmed \pounds 1{,}723 \citep{ofgem2026cap} once the roughly \pounds 45 of electricity-VAT relief unrelated to the war is added back. The third is the denominator: every percentage of income divides by equivalised household net income after housing costs on the HBAI scale, which is the concept the published distributional literature uses; the unequivalised gradient is reported once, as a robustness line, so that the choice is visible rather than silent. In the main specification the mean annual household loss is \pounds \genCentralLossMean{}, or \genCentralLossPctIncome{} per cent of equivalised disposable income, and the aggregate is \pounds \genAggSpendRealisedBn{} billion; on the steady-state basis the same figures are \pounds \genSteadyStateLossMean{} and \pounds \genSteadyStateAggBn{} billion. The external check on the aggregate is the Resolution Foundation's independently produced estimate that a sustained return to peak prices adds \pounds 11 billion to household energy and road-fuel spending in calendar 2026 \citep{rf2026outlook} --- the same object and the same population as mine, but not the same window, since my headline runs March 2026 to February 2027. Section~\ref{sec:results-aggregate} therefore re-runs both legs on calendar 2026 so that the comparison is like for like, and states the window on both sides. On either window my main specification sits below the Resolution Foundation's figure and the peak-fuel upper bound of \pounds \genPeakFuelAggBn{} billion above it, so the two bracket the only comparable published figure. That is the appropriate form of external validation for a pipeline whose components have not previously been joined: a range that contains an independent estimate, rather than a point match between objects that are not in fact the same.

The second contribution is the composition of the loss, and it reframes the policy debate --- but it does so through a claim that must be stated at the right strength, because the size of the effect depends on calibration even where its direction does not. Motor fuel carries \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate loss in the main specification, against \genGasShareOfLoss{} per cent through gas and \genElecShareOfLoss{} per cent through electricity. Across the seven specifications I run the motor-fuel share ranges from \genSpecFuelShareMinPct{} to \genSpecFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent, and it moves between \genDomesticLegNbpFuelShareMinPct{} and \genDomesticLegNbpFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent on the pre-war gas price assumption alone. It does not hold in all seven. Damping the gas and pump peaks by a common fraction --- which is what a reader unpersuaded by my argument for fuel-specific damping would impose --- gives \genSymDampMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent, a minority; and combining that symmetric damping with either of the other two accounting choices takes it lower still, to \genSymDampSteadyMotorFuelShare{} per cent on the steady-state domestic basis and \genSymDampOnsLevelsMotorFuelShare{} per cent on ONS-consistent spending levels. The central figure is also higher than the one an earlier draft reported, and for a reason worth naming: anchoring the domestic leg to Ofgem's confirmed cap and shortening the lag both shrink the domestic leg, so the motor-fuel share rises mechanically. The defensible claim is therefore that the motor-fuel share is calibration-dependent across a wide range --- roughly \genSymDampMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} to \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent once the peak-fuel upper bound is set aside, with the ONS-levels calibration at \genOnsLevelsMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent --- and that motor fuel carries a \emph{large} part of the loss on every calibration, rather than that \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent is settled. What survives the whole range is the policy point, and it is the one that matters for the Budget. Every instrument now under discussion---the social tariff, the JRF discounted block, the expanded Warm Home Discount, VAT zero-rating---operates on the domestic bill, and on every calibration in this paper a substantial part of the loss --- between a third and three-quarters of it --- runs through a channel none of them touches. That is a constraint of construction rather than of design: when these options are scored below and found wanting, the channel they act on binds before the precision with which they act does.

The third contribution is distributional, and turns on crossing two facts that the literature has established separately but never displayed together. \citet{fetzer2024} show, using 22.2 million Energy Performance Certificates matched to meter-level consumption records, that more affluent English neighbourhoods are more exposed in \emph{pounds}, because they occupy larger and less efficient homes---which is why the Energy Price Guarantee, a subsidy proportional to consumption, benefited them disproportionately. The budget-share literature shows with equal firmness that poorer households lose more in \emph{per cent}, because energy is a necessity with a falling Engel curve. Both are true, and a policy debate that quotes only one of them is conducted at cross purposes. I show them simultaneously on the same households. In per cent the gradient is steep and monotone at the ends, \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent in the bottom decile against \genDecileTenLossPct{} per cent in the top. In pounds it is not monotone at all: the largest cash loss falls on decile eight, at \pounds \genDecileEightLossGbp{}, above decile ten's \pounds \genDecileTenLossGbp{} and well above decile one's \pounds \genDecileOneLossGbp{}. The motor-fuel share explains the shape---vehicle ownership and mileage, not dwelling efficiency, drive the cash profile, and both peak below the top of the income distribution. The \emph{direction} of the percentage gradient is robust: the burden falls in income under every specification in the paper. Its \emph{magnitude} is not, and two distinct measurement problems bear on it, neither of which this paper resolves. The first is the numerator: the microdata impute an almost flat motor-fuel spending profile across deciles --- \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileOneRawGbp{} at the bottom against \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileTenRawGbp{} at the top, where ONS Family Spending gives \pounds \genOnsBenchFuelDecileOneGbp{} against \pounds \genOnsBenchFuelDecileTenGbp{} \citep{ons2026familyspending} --- and give the top and bottom deciles identical fuel-purchasing rates where the National Travel Survey's household car \emph{availability} table finds 40 per cent of the poorest quintile without a car against 14 per cent of the richest \citep{dft2025nts}. That table covers England only, while the model is UK-wide, so it bounds the direction of the defect rather than measuring it exactly. Correcting the decile shape to the ONS profile takes the decile-one to decile-ten ratio from \genDecileRatioPct{} to \genOnsFuelDecileRatioPct{} to one; correcting both spending levels as well gives \genOnsLevelsDecileRatioPct{}. The second is the denominator, and here the paper's earlier position was wrong and is withdrawn. On the equivalised after-housing-costs concept, \genDecileOneZeroIncomeShare{} per cent of decile-one households have zero or negative income --- not the fraction of a per cent the unequivalised concept suggested --- and every one of the \genCoverageExcludedHouseholdsM{} million households (\genCoverageExcludedSharePct{} per cent of the total) that fall outside deciles one to ten has non-positive equivalised income. Percentage statistics therefore drop non-positive incomes and report the dropped weight, and the low-income tail is treated here as an unresolved measurement problem rather than one the paper has answered. Taken together the gradient runs somewhere between \genOnsFuelDecileRatioPct{} and \genDecileRatioPct{} to one depending on specification, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the specifications side by side rather than choosing between them. The geographic cut is the region, of which there are twelve: parliamentary-constituency estimates are not available on this microdata, for reasons set out in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

The fourth contribution is the policy scoring, and revising it has corrected two of the paper's earlier conclusions. Decile-average tables are the wrong summary for this shock, though the quantitative form of that claim needs care: the mean within-decile p90--p10 range is \genWithinDecileRangePct{} percentage points against an interdecile range across decile means of \genBetweenDecileRangePct{} points, but the comparison is carried by decile one alone. Excluding it the mean within-decile range is \genWithinDecileRangeExDOnePct{} points, below the between-decile range, and on a median-based measure it is \genWithinDecileMedianRangePct{} points. The qualitative case for within-decile reporting rests on \citet{cronin2019} and \citet{sallee2019} rather than on that inequality, and it is made that way here. I therefore report the share of losing households left uncompensated \emph{within} each decile alongside two continuous measures: the share of the aggregate loss offset, and the mean and median residual loss. The first reversal concerns the JRF block. My earlier parameterisation discounted only the shock component of the block, where JRF propose a discounted rate on the level; correctly specified, the block costs \pounds \genJrfCostBn{} billion against JRF's own \pounds \genJrfStatedCostBn{} billion, so what I score is \emph{more} generous than their proposal, not less --- and, scored at that cost, it leaves only \genJrfUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losers uncompensated and fully compensates \genJrfFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent, the best of the five. The second concerns the common-envelope comparison itself. Scored at each sponsor's own stated cost, the instruments are not comparable --- a \pounds \genVatCutCostBn{} billion measure was being judged against a \pounds \genRebateCostBn{} billion one on a knife-edge metric that counts any shortfall as a failure. An earlier version scaled each instrument's parameter until it exhausted a \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion envelope and crowned VAT zero-rating the winner. That comparison was not admissible: zero-rating cannot absorb \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion, because the reduced rate is five points and removing all five costs \pounds \genVatCutCostBn{} billion, so the winning row was a negative VAT rate rather than a VAT policy. The claim is withdrawn, and rows whose implied parameter exceeds what the instrument can deliver are now labelled for what they are --- a proportional domestic-bill subsidy at some rate, not the named policy. What replaces it is a stronger finding. Held at their \emph{feasible} maxima the social tariff and the Warm Home Discount offset only \genSocialTariffEnvelopeOffsetPct{} and \genWhdEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss, because there is nothing left to give the population they can see; spending the same envelope on \emph{eligibility} at each sponsor's own generosity offsets \genSocialTariffEligibilityOffsetPct{} and \genWhdEligibilityOffsetPct{} per cent. Eligibility, not generosity, is the binding margin --- which is the same coverage finding the paper reaches from the incidence side, arriving from the fiscal one. Neither correction disturbs the channel constraint --- no bill instrument reaches the motor-fuel loss at any budget --- and the relevance of both to \citet{levell2025} is that the case for an untargeted component here rests on coverage and saturation rather than on universality as such.

It is as important to say what this paper does not do. It contains no general-equilibrium analysis. \citet{kanzig2023} shows that the bulk of the distributional consequence of an energy shock reaches poorer households through income and employment rather than through budget shares, with general-equilibrium and indirect effects accounting for roughly two-thirds of the total response; a static consumption-side microsimulation therefore captures at most a third of true incidence. \citet{goulder2019} makes the complementary point that use-side effects are regressive while source-side effects are progressive, and that a microsimulation of the kind conducted here observes only the use side. \citet{pallotti2024} add that in the euro area most household-level heterogeneity in the 2021--23 episode was driven by nominal net asset positions rather than by the energy basket at all. I do not model consumption substitution---PolicyEngine UK has no demand elasticity or pass-through block---nor do I deduct baseline VAT from net income, since the model carries VAT only as a policy delta. Each of these is stated at the point of use and revisited in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}, where I argue that two of them---UK cap-era wholesale-to-retail pass-through, and the absence of any British counterpart to \citet{douenne2020} on off-gas-grid horizontal losers---are better understood as the next papers than as apologies for this one.
