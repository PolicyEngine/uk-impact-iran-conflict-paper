\appendix
\section{Sensitivity, robustness and reproduction}
\label{sec:appendix}

Three sweeps are reported, each re-running the full pipeline on the realised 2026 central scenario against the same baseline. They are presented in order of how much they move the answer, and that ordering is itself the appendix's main message: \textbf{demand response dominates, the calendar-year lag comes second, and the marginal-pricing share is a distant third}. A reader deciding where to direct scepticism should direct it at the elasticity assumption and nowhere else.

No constituency-level robustness statistic appears in this appendix. The data release carries no constituency weight matrix, so the seat-level rank correlations an earlier design called for are not computable and none has been substituted (Section~\ref{sec:step3}).

\subsection{Demand response: the sweep that matters}
\label{app:elasticity}

The main specification is zero-elasticity by construction: equation~\eqref{eq:firstorder} evaluates the price change at pre-shock quantities, which is the \citet{deaton1980} first-order approximation and an explicit upper bound on the welfare loss. Elasticity is therefore a robustness check on the main result, not an alternative main result --- an important framing point, because an elasticity-adjusted central estimate would be a number driven by a parameter this paper does not estimate.

The sweep applies the constant-elasticity spend factor $(p_1/p_0)^{1+\varepsilon}$ --- never a linear approximation, which would misbehave at the top of the grid --- over a flat grid from $\varepsilon = 0$ to $\varepsilon = -0.8$, and additionally under five named published specifications: Labandeira, Labeaga and L\'opez-Otero's short- and long-run per-carrier means, Priesmann and Praktiknjo's income-varying short and long run, and a replication of a prior repository's decile-varying configuration.

\paragraph{Magnitude.} This is by a wide margin the largest source of uncertainty in the paper. The aggregate loss runs from \pounds\genElasticityAggZeroBn{} billion at $\varepsilon = 0$ to \pounds\genElasticityAggHighBn{} billion at $\varepsilon = -0.8$, and the relationship is close to linear: each additional $0.1$ of elasticity removes roughly a tenth of the upper bound. Quoted as a range that alone spans a factor of five, which would make the paper uninformative --- so the relevant question is which part of the grid is credible for a within-year price spike.

\paragraph{The credible short-run band is much narrower.} The published short-run estimates cluster tightly. Labandeira's short-run per-carrier means shave \genLabandeiraShortShaved{} per cent off the upper bound; Priesmann's income-varying short run shaves \genPriesmannShortShaved{} per cent. A reader who rejects the zero-elasticity headline outright therefore lands near \pounds 10 billion and a mean household loss in the low \pounds 300s, not somewhere qualitatively different from the \pounds\genAggSpendRealisedBn{} billion and \pounds\genCentralLossMean{} reported in the main specification. Labandeira's \emph{long}-run figures shave 72 per cent, but they embed appliance and vehicle stock turnover and are the wrong horizon for a 2026 spike; they bound the exercise and are not a candidate headline.

\paragraph{What does not change.} Nothing about the distributional story. A flat elasticity rescales every decile by the same factor, so the decile-one to decile-ten percentage ratio stays at \genDecileRatioPct{} to one across the entire flat grid. The income-varying specifications \emph{do} flatten the gradient --- Priesmann's takes the ratio from \genDecileRatioPct{} to \genPriesmannDecileRatio{} to one --- because poor households are modelled as the ones with room to cut back. That flattening is precisely the ``heat or eat'' objection in numerical form: it treats involuntary rationing as welfare-neutral substitution, and treating a household that cannot afford to heat its home as having merely revealed a preference is the substantive reason this paper keeps the upper bound as its headline. Either way the qualitative ordering survives every row of the sweep: the poor lose more in percentage terms and less in cash in all fourteen specifications.

A caveat on the exercise itself: applying a constant elasticity to a capped price is not innocuous, because households facing a standing charge plus a unit rate face a marginal price that differs from their average price, and the block designs scored in Section~\ref{sec:policy} change the marginal price for some households and not others. The sweep uses the unit rate as the relevant margin and does not model standing-charge effects.

\subsection{Cap lag: a windowing artefact, not an effect}
\label{app:caplag}

The lag $\ell$ in equation~\eqref{eq:passthrough} places the quarterly phase-in profile on the calendar starting at 2026Q1. It is the paper's most contestable calibration, so it is swept over one to four quarters and both the annualised 2026 loss and the undiscounted cumulative loss over the whole path are reported. The domestic-energy channel is lagged; motor fuel is not, because pump prices pass through in weeks.

The two columns tell opposite stories, and that is the point of reporting both. The \emph{cumulative} loss is exactly invariant to the lag: \pounds\genCapLagCumulativeMean{} per household, \pounds\genCapLagCumulativeBn{} billion, at every value of $\ell$ from one to four. A lag shifts the path in time; it does not change its size. The \emph{annualised} 2026 figure, by contrast, falls from \pounds\genCapLagAnnualisedLagOne{} at $\ell = 1$ to \pounds\genCapLagAnnualisedLagFour{} at $\ell = 4$, with the paper's $\ell = 3$ giving \pounds\genCapLagAnnualisedPaper{}. That entire movement is a windowing artefact: at $\ell = 3$ only 2026Q4 falls inside the calendar year, so a small fraction of the eventual domestic pass-through is booked to 2026 and the shock is overwhelmingly a 2027 event that the 2026 window happens to clip.

Two consequences for how the paper's numbers should be read. First, any statement of the form ``the 2026 loss is $X$'' is lag-sensitive by roughly \pounds 40 per household across the plausible range, while any statement about the total burden of the shock is not sensitive at all --- so the cumulative figure is quoted as the burden and the annual figure only as calendar-year attribution. Second, the annualised figures are dominated by motor fuel (\pounds\genMotorFuelAnnualisedBn{} billion of the annualised total at $\ell = 3$), precisely because the fast pump channel and the slow cap channel arrive in different years. That is a result worth stating rather than a footnote: the 2026 calendar year is almost purely a pump-price event, and the domestic-bill event lands in 2027, after the Budget that is supposed to respond to it.

\subsection{Marginal-pricing share: composition, not incidence}
\label{app:asymmetry}

The share of the time a gas-fired plant sets the GB marginal electricity price governs how far a wholesale gas move reaches the electricity cap. The main specification uses 0.85; setting it to 1.00 recovers the naive symmetric assumption that the move reaches electricity undamped, and 0.70 bounds it from below.

Stated plainly: it barely moves the answer. Going the whole way from 0.70 to 1.00 changes the mean household loss from \pounds\genAsymMeanLossLow{} to \pounds\genAsymMeanLossHigh{}, a difference of \genAsymMeanShiftPct{} per cent, and the decile-one to decile-ten percentage ratio is frozen at \genAsymRatioLow{} against \genAsymRatioHigh{} across the entire range. Nothing in the paper's distributional conclusions turns on this parameter, and Section~\ref{sec:step1} says so rather than claiming otherwise.

Where it does bite is composition, which is the one column that moves materially: the gas share of the domestic-energy loss falls from \genGasShareDomesticLow{} per cent at 0.70 to \genGasShareDomesticHigh{} per cent at 1.00, against \genGasShareDomesticCentral{} per cent in the main specification. Under the naive symmetric assumption the shock looks like an essentially even gas--electricity event; under the paper's calibration it is meaningfully gas-weighted. That matters for policy design and not for headline incidence: a gas-only social tariff, an electricity-side levy rebate, and the JRF block's choice of which unit rate to discount all land differently depending on which fuel carries the loss.

The honest summary is therefore narrow. The asymmetry is defensible on its merits and is the right modelling choice, but it is a compositional refinement rather than a load-bearing assumption. A referee who insists on the symmetric assumption gets a five per cent larger mean loss and identical distributional conclusions. And it moves the answer an order of magnitude less than the elasticity assumption does --- five per cent against up to 82 per cent --- which is why the uncertainty ranking at the head of this appendix puts it last.

\subsection{Upstream contributions arising}
\label{app:upstream}

Three of this paper's limitations are model gaps rather than data gaps, and each is precise enough to fix. They are contributed upstream to PolicyEngine UK rather than only reported here: a live price channel for the imputed energy variables, so that a cap or pump-price reform moves household spend rather than silently cutting quantities; a Warm Home Discount parameter and variable, which the model currently lacks entirely; and a primary-heating-fuel or gas-grid indicator in the microdata, without which the off-gas-grid question in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} cannot be asked of any UK microsimulation. The dead \texttt{gov.contrib.policyengine.economy.energy\_bills} parameter is flagged for removal or implementation.

\subsection{Reproduction}
\label{app:reproduce}

All code, parameters and aggregate results are in the paper's public repository. The microdata are not: PolicyEngine UK's enhanced dataset is held in a private Hugging Face repository under a licence that does not permit redistribution, so reproducing the household results requires a token with access to it. The aggregate outputs in \texttt{results/} are committed, so every number in the paper can be traced from the JSON and CSV artefacts to the macro that renders it without re-running the microsimulation.

\begin{verbatim}
uv sync
export HUGGING_FACE_TOKEN=hf_...     # needs private dataset access
python analysis/run_incidence.py     # scenarios + policies -> results/*.json
python analysis/run_sensitivity.py   # three sweeps -> results/sensitivity/*.csv
python analysis/emit_tables.py       # results/ -> paper/tables/*.tex
python analysis/emit_tex_values.py   # results/ -> paper/values_generated.tex
python -m pytest tests/              # runs without microdata
cd paper && latexmk -pdf main.tex
\end{verbatim}

Every headline number enters the prose as a macro from \texttt{paper/values\_generated.tex}, emitted mechanically from \texttt{results/}. A macro with no corresponding key in \texttt{results/} renders as \texttt{\textbackslash GENMISSING}, so a stale or partial results tree fails visibly in the compiled PDF rather than silently preserving superseded numbers; the same mechanism is what forced the removal of the constituency and heating-fuel claims from this draft rather than allowing them to survive as unbacked prose. Pinned dependency versions, including the \texttt{policyengine-uk} release used, are in \texttt{pyproject.toml} and \texttt{uv.lock}.
