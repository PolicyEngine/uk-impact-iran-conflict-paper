\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the exercise establishes}

Three things. First, that a macro scenario can be carried through to a UK household price vector in a way that is disciplined by the actual regulatory mechanism rather than by an assumed pass-through: the cap formula, its averaging window, its quarterly quantisation and its non-commodity components together determine how much of a wholesale move reaches a bill and when. Second, that shocking gas and electricity asymmetrically --- as the marginal-pricing structure of the British electricity market requires --- changes the horizontal distribution of losses in ways a symmetric energy shock cannot express. Third, that the two incidence facts the literature reports separately can be crossed at parliamentary-constituency level, and that when they are, they diverge sharply. The seats that lose most in pounds and the seats that lose most as a share of income are close to disjoint sets. That divergence is the mechanism behind the compensation problem: any instrument calibrated on one metric systematically misses the population identified by the other.

The corollary for method is that reporting decile means is not enough. The within-decile dispersion in Figure~\ref{fig:withindecile} exceeds the between-decile gradient, replicating \citet{cronin2019} on UK data, and the uncompensated-loser metric that dispersion motivates ranks the policy options differently from the conventional targeting metrics. A scorecard that reports only the conventional metrics will endorse a design that leaves a large minority of poor households worse off.

\subsection{The Känzig caveat}

The most serious objection to this paper is that it measures the wrong channel. \citet{kanzig2023} shows that when energy shocks hit, poorer households lose disproportionately not because their budget shares are higher but because their employment and earnings are more cyclically exposed, and that general-equilibrium and indirect effects account for roughly two-thirds of the household response. A static, use-side microsimulation captures none of that. \citet{goulder2019} sharpen the point: the use-side effects a consumption microsimulation measures are regressive, the source-side effects it omits are progressive, and the net incidence depends on both. \citet{ohlendorf2021}'s meta-analysis is the summary statement --- whether an energy shock or carbon policy is judged regressive depends substantially on which channels the method admits.

I take this seriously rather than deflecting it, and it constrains the claims in three ways.

First, magnitude. If the use-side channel is roughly a third of the total, the losses reported here are a lower bound on total household incidence, and the appropriate reading of a bottom-decile loss of \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent of income is that it is the part attributable to the price of energy alone, not the household's total exposure to the conflict. A reader wanting a total should multiply, with the acknowledgement that the multiplier is borrowed from a different country and a different identification strategy.

Second, ordering. Känzig's mechanism operates through employment, which is more concentrated among lower-income households than energy budget shares are. The omitted channel therefore almost certainly reinforces rather than offsets the regressivity reported here in percentage terms --- but it may well \emph{reverse} the cash-terms ordering, since employment losses in pounds are larger higher up the earnings distribution. The paired maps in Figure~\ref{fig:maps} are a use-side object; a GE version of the same map would not look the same, and the constituencies most exposed to the employment channel (industrial, energy-intensive-employment seats) are not the constituencies most exposed to the consumption channel.

Third, policy inference. The compensation metrics are internally consistent --- they compare instruments against the same shock in the same model --- so the \emph{ranking} of policies is more robust than the levels. But a policy scored as fully compensating a household on the use-side loss is not thereby fully compensating that household, and no policy in the scorecard addresses the income channel at all. The instruments that do --- automatic stabilisers, the Universal Credit taper, statutory sick pay --- sit outside the shock being modelled and inside the model being used, which is an argument for the GE extension rather than against the exercise.

There is also a countervailing consideration in the other direction. \citet{pallotti2024} find that for the euro-area 2021--23 episode, heterogeneity in household outcomes was driven mainly by nominal net asset positions --- who held nominal debt and who held nominal savings when inflation arrived --- rather than by energy basket composition. If the same holds for the UK, both the use-side channel modelled here and the employment channel Känzig emphasises are second to a balance-sheet channel that requires an up-to-date wealth distribution to measure. The model's wealth imputation rests on Wealth and Assets Survey round 7 (2018--20), which predates the entire rate cycle, so this paper cannot speak to it.

\subsection{Three gaps this paper makes visible}

Each of the three limitations most binding on this analysis is also a well-defined and, as far as I can establish, unfilled research question.

\paragraph{(a) UK wholesale-to-retail pass-through in the price-cap era.} The coefficient $\phi_f$ and lag $\ell$ in equation~\eqref{eq:passthrough} are the load-bearing parameters of Step 1, and they are calibrated from the published structure of the cap rather than estimated. That is because there is no well-cited econometric estimate of wholesale-to-retail energy price pass-through for the UK under the default tariff cap. The pre-cap pass-through literature is not transferable: the cap replaced a competitive-retail pricing mechanism with a formula, changing both the horizon and the functional form of transmission, and the formula itself has been amended repeatedly since 2019 --- most substantially in the 2022 methodology review --- so the estimation problem is a regime-switching one on a short sample. The data exist. Ofgem publishes the cap decompositions, the averaging windows and the announcement dates; NBP and TTF forward curves are observable at daily frequency; DESNZ publishes retail price statistics. The identification comes from the announcement calendar itself: the cap is a deterministic function of forwards over a pre-announced window, so the mapping is estimable and its residual --- the part of retail prices not explained by the formula --- is the object of interest for fixed-tariff and out-of-cap customers. This is the most immediately tractable of the three gaps and would tighten every number in this paper.

\paragraph{(b) Energy arrears as an outcome variable.} The paper's outcome is a cost increase. The observable manifestation of the 2026 shock, however, is debt: JRF report around 1.5 million families in energy arrears and 3.8 million households owing roughly \pounds 7.5 billion. Arrears are not a mechanical function of the cost increase, because they depend on the household's liquidity position, its access to credit, supplier forbearance practice, and the prepayment-versus-credit-meter split. Modelling them requires an intertemporal margin the model does not have, and modelling the policy response to them requires representing debt-related allowances --- which now sit inside the cap itself, so that arrears feed back into everybody's unit rate. That feedback is a genuinely interesting object: a shock that pushes some households into arrears raises the debt allowance in the next cap period and hence raises bills for all households, a within-consumer cross-subsidy with its own incidence. Nobody has quantified it for the UK. It would also change the policy ranking, since a social tariff that prevents arrears has a fiscal return the current scorecard does not credit it with.

\paragraph{(c) Off-gas-grid and rural horizontal losers.} \citet{douenne2020} shows for France that a flat recycling scheme is progressive on average while creating large horizontal losers concentrated among rural and off-grid poor households --- people whose heating fuel is unregulated, whose homes are older and less efficient, and whose transport is non-substitutable. There is no UK counterpart to this result, despite roughly four million UK properties being off the gas grid and despite heating oil, LPG and solid fuel being wholly outside the price cap and outside the Warm Home Discount's implicit design assumptions. Table~\ref{tab:heating} takes a first step by splitting losses on gas-grid status, but the model's energy variables are calibrated on NEED metered gas and electricity, so heating oil is imputed thinly and the off-grid estimates are the least reliable in the paper. A dedicated study --- combining EPC records, off-grid fuel price series and rural transport dependence --- would fill a gap that is both a research gap and, given that off-grid households were the group left out of most 2022 support schemes until belated top-ups, a policy one.

\subsection{Where this leaves the macro-to-micro link}

The Seventh Carbon Budget established ``macro model plus distributional annex'' as the expected format for UK official analysis, but the annex is typically produced by a separate method on separate data, so the macro and micro numbers are not mutually consistent. What this paper does is small but structural: it fixes a single explicit transmission mechanism --- the cap formula --- as the bridge, so that the household results are a deterministic function of the macro scenario and can be regenerated when the scenario changes. The bridge is currently one-way and covers one price channel. Extending it to the income channel, and closing the loop so that the household response feeds back into aggregate demand, is the natural continuation, and gap (a) above is its prerequisite.
