\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the exercise establishes}

Four things, in descending order of how much they change the way the 2026 shock should be discussed.

\paragraph{It is more a pump-price shock than an energy-bill shock.} Between \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} and \genPeakFuelMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate household loss in the central scenario runs through motor fuel --- the lower figure damping the observed pump-price peak to an annual average on the same logic as the cap, the upper applying it undamped for a full year. In the main specification gas accounts for \genGasShareOfLoss{} per cent and electricity for \genElecShareOfLoss{} per cent. The proportions are similar in the adverse scenario and under an ONS-calibrated fuel profile, so the majority share of motor fuel is a property of the shock rather than of a particular calibration, even though its precise size is not. Every instrument in the Autumn Budget debate --- social tariff, discounted block, Warm Home Discount, VAT zero-rating --- operates on the domestic bill and therefore addresses at most about half of the loss by construction. That is the single most consequential finding in this paper and it is not a targeting result: it is a channel result, and it binds before any question of design does. It also explains the shape of the cash gradient, which is driven by vehicle ownership rather than by dwelling size or energy efficiency, and which consequently does not rise monotonically across deciles.

\paragraph{A regulatory mechanism can carry a macro scenario to a household price vector.} The cap formula, its averaging window, its quarterly quantisation and its non-commodity components together determine how much of a wholesale move reaches a bill and when, and this paper fixes that mechanism explicitly rather than assuming a pass-through coefficient. The corollary discovered along the way is that the cap channel and the pump channel arrive in different years: pump prices pass through in weeks, the cap in three quarters, so a calendar-year figure mixes a fully arrived fast channel with a barely started slow one. Appendix~\ref{app:caplag} shows that the cumulative burden is exactly invariant to the lag while the calendar-year attribution moves by roughly \pounds 80 per household across the plausible range. The cumulative figure is the burden of the shock; the annual figure is an accounting statement about which year it lands in.

\paragraph{The burden falls in income and the cash loss does not; the steepness is uncertain by a factor of two.} Decile one loses \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent of income against decile ten's \genDecileTenLossPct{} per cent, a ratio of roughly \genDecileRatioPct{} to one. That ratio is not an artefact of the near-zero-income tail --- it survives on medians as well as on the aggregate ratio --- but the median check answers only the denominator objection. The numerator objection is the live one: the microdata over-impute motor-fuel spend to poor households, imputing an almost flat spending profile (\pounds \genFuelSpendDecileOneRawGbp{} in decile one against \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileTenRawGbp{} in decile ten) where ONS Family Spending gives \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileOneOnsGbp{} against \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileTenOnsGbp{}, and recalibrating to the ONS profile takes the ratio down to \genOnsFuelDecileRatioPct{} to one. What is robust is the pair of qualitative facts, which hold under both specifications; the magnitude of the gradient is not, and is stated here as a range. In cash the ordering is the \citet{fetzer2024} one and considerably messier: \pounds\genDecileOneLossGbp{} in decile one against \pounds\genDecileTenLossGbp{} in decile ten, but with the peak in neither. Within-decile dispersion exceeds the between-decile gradient --- \genWithinDecileRangePct{} percentage points against \genBetweenDecileRangePct{} --- replicating \citet{cronin2019} on UK data, and the uncompensated-loser metric that dispersion motivates ranks the policy options differently from the conventional targeting metrics. A scorecard reporting only decile means measures the smaller of the two effects and endorses a design that leaves a large minority of poor households worse off.

\paragraph{Where an open tax-benefit model stops.} PolicyEngine UK has no price channel: energy quantities are imputed inputs, the regulated cap enters only through defunct subsidy machinery, the one contributed lever named for household energy bills is read by nothing, the motor-fuel price parameter is implicitly unit-elastic, and the Warm Home Discount does not exist. Establishing that precisely, item by item, is a reusable result for anyone attempting macro-to-micro energy work on an open UK model, and it is specific enough to be fixed rather than merely lamented. The corresponding upstream contributions are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:upstream}.

One claim this paper does \emph{not} make is a constituency-level one. There is no constituency weight matrix in this data release and the local-authority field is degenerate, so a seat-level cross of the two incidence metrics is not producible, and no approximation to one is offered in its place. Region is the finest geography reported, and it is a weaker cut: regional means compress exactly the horizontal variation the exercise is designed to expose.

\subsection{The Känzig caveat}

The most serious objection to this paper is that it measures the wrong channel. \citet{kanzig2023} shows that when energy shocks hit, poorer households lose disproportionately not because their budget shares are higher but because their employment and earnings are more cyclically exposed, and that general-equilibrium and indirect effects account for roughly two-thirds of the household response. A static, use-side microsimulation captures none of that. \citet{goulder2019} sharpen the point: the use-side effects a consumption microsimulation measures are regressive, the source-side effects it omits are progressive, and net incidence depends on both. \citet{ohlendorf2021}'s meta-analysis is the summary statement --- whether an energy shock is judged regressive depends substantially on which channels the method admits.

I take this seriously rather than deflecting it, and it constrains the claims in three ways.

First, magnitude. If the use-side channel is roughly a third of the total, the losses reported here are a lower bound on total household incidence, and the appropriate reading of a bottom-decile loss of \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent of income is that it is the part attributable to the price of energy alone, not that household's total exposure to the conflict. A reader wanting a total should multiply, acknowledging that the multiplier is borrowed from a different country and a different identification strategy.

Second, ordering. Känzig's mechanism operates through employment, which is more concentrated among lower-income households than energy budget shares are. The omitted channel therefore almost certainly reinforces the regressivity reported here in percentage terms --- but it may well \emph{reverse} the cash-terms ordering, since employment losses in pounds are larger higher up the earnings distribution. Since the cash ordering here is already non-monotonic and driven by vehicle ownership, a general-equilibrium version of this exercise would not merely rescale the profile; it would redraw it.

Third, policy inference. The compensation metrics are internally consistent --- they compare instruments against the same shock in the same model --- so the \emph{ranking} of policies is more robust than the levels. But a policy scored as fully compensating a household on its use-side loss is not thereby compensating that household, and no policy in the scorecard addresses the income channel at all. The instruments that do --- automatic stabilisers, the Universal Credit taper, statutory sick pay --- sit outside the shock being modelled and inside the model being used, which is an argument for the general-equilibrium extension rather than against the exercise.

There is also a countervailing consideration. \citet{pallotti2024} find that for the euro-area 2021--23 episode, heterogeneity in household outcomes was driven mainly by nominal net asset positions rather than by energy basket composition. If the same holds for the UK, both the use-side channel modelled here and the employment channel Känzig emphasises are second to a balance-sheet channel that requires an up-to-date wealth distribution to measure. The model's wealth imputation rests on Wealth and Assets Survey round 7 (2018--20), which predates the entire rate cycle, so this paper cannot speak to it.

\subsection{Three gaps this paper makes visible}

Each of the three limitations most binding on this analysis is also a well-defined and, as far as I can establish, unfilled research question.

\paragraph{(a) UK wholesale-to-retail pass-through in the price-cap era.} The coefficient $\phi_f$ and lag $\ell$ in equation~\eqref{eq:passthrough} are the load-bearing parameters of Step 1, and they are calibrated from the published structure of the cap rather than estimated. That is because there is no well-cited econometric estimate of wholesale-to-retail energy price pass-through for the UK under the default tariff cap. The pre-cap literature is not transferable: the cap replaced a competitive-retail pricing mechanism with a formula, changing both the horizon and the functional form of transmission, and the formula has been amended repeatedly since 2019 --- most substantially in the 2022 methodology review --- so the estimation problem is a regime-switching one on a short sample. The data exist. Ofgem publishes the cap decompositions, the averaging windows and the announcement dates; NBP and TTF forward curves are observable daily; DESNZ publishes retail price statistics. The identification comes from the announcement calendar itself: the cap is a deterministic function of forwards over a pre-announced window, so the mapping is estimable and its residual --- the part of retail prices not explained by the formula --- is the object of interest for fixed-tariff and out-of-cap customers. This is the most immediately tractable of the three gaps and would tighten every number in this paper, including the lag whose consequences Appendix~\ref{app:caplag} reports.

\paragraph{(b) Energy arrears as an outcome variable.} The paper's outcome is a cost increase. The observable manifestation of the 2026 shock, however, is debt: JRF report around 1.5 million families in energy arrears and 3.8 million households owing roughly \pounds 7.5 billion. Arrears are not a mechanical function of the cost increase, because they depend on the household's liquidity position, its access to credit, supplier forbearance practice, and the prepayment-versus-credit-meter split. Modelling them requires an intertemporal margin the model does not have, and modelling the policy response to them requires representing debt-related allowances --- which now sit inside the cap itself, so that arrears feed back into everybody's unit rate. That feedback is a genuinely interesting object: a shock that pushes some households into arrears raises the debt allowance in the next cap period and hence raises bills for all households, a within-consumer cross-subsidy with its own incidence. Nobody has quantified it for the UK. It would also change the policy ranking, since a social tariff that prevents arrears has a fiscal return the current scorecard does not credit it with.

\paragraph{(c) Off-gas-grid and rural horizontal losers --- untested here.} \citet{douenne2020} shows for France that a flat recycling scheme is progressive on average while creating large horizontal losers concentrated among rural and off-grid poor households --- people whose heating fuel is unregulated, whose homes are older and less efficient, and whose transport is non-substitutable. There is no UK counterpart to that result, and this paper does not supply one, because the data will not support the split it requires. The microdata contain no primary-heating-fuel indicator and no gas-grid-connection flag, so losses cannot be split on gas-grid status at all --- not unreliably, but not at all --- and no imputed stand-in is offered. The gap therefore remains exactly as open as Douenne left it, and it is a live one: roughly four million UK properties are off the gas grid, heating oil and LPG sit wholly outside the price cap and outside the Warm Home Discount's implicit design assumptions, and these households were repeatedly omitted from 2022 support schemes until belated top-ups. The finding that motor fuel dominates the loss makes the rural question sharper still, since rural transport dependence and off-grid heating coincide in the same households. A dedicated study combining EPC records, off-grid fuel price series and rural transport dependence would fill it; adding a heating-fuel indicator to the UK microdata is the prerequisite, and it is the third of the upstream contributions in Appendix~\ref{app:upstream}.

\subsection{Where this leaves the macro-to-micro link}

The Seventh Carbon Budget established ``macro model plus distributional annex'' as the expected format for UK official analysis, but the annex is typically produced by a separate method on separate data, so the macro and micro numbers are not mutually consistent. What this paper does is small but structural: it fixes a single explicit transmission mechanism --- the cap formula for domestic energy, the pump-price identity for road fuel --- as the bridge, so that the household results are a deterministic function of the macro scenario and can be regenerated when the scenario changes. The bridge is currently one-way and covers only the price channel, and it currently sits alongside the tax-benefit model rather than inside it, because the model has no price channel to host it. Moving it inside, extending it to the income channel, and closing the loop so that the household response feeds back into aggregate demand is the natural continuation, and gap (a) is its prerequisite.
