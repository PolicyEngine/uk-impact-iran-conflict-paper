\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the exercise establishes}

Five things, in descending order of how much they change the way the 2026 shock should be discussed. All annual figures refer to one window: March 2026 to February 2027, the twelve months from the shock.

\paragraph{Motor fuel is the largest single channel, and no bill instrument reaches it.} Motor fuel carries \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate household loss in the main specification --- over the modelled window, against an aggregate of \pounds \genAggSpendRealisedBn{} billion and a mean household loss of \pounds \genCentralLossMean{}, or \genCentralLossPctIncome{} per cent of equivalised income after housing costs --- against \genGasShareOfLoss{} per cent for gas and \genElecShareOfLoss{} per cent for electricity. It is a bare majority rather than a dominant one, and the paper is careful not to present it as more.

That distinction is the substantive change in this version and it is worth stating in its own right. The motor-fuel share turns almost entirely on the ratio of the gas and pump sustained fractions. On a pre-war baseline solved from both published 2026 caps those fractions are \genGasSustainedFraction{} and \genPumpSustainedFraction{} per cent, with gas the larger. Earlier drafts of this paper used the observed July 2026 cap as a pre-war baseline, which it is not --- the war was already inside its forward-curve observation window --- and the solve compensated by driving the gas fraction down to roughly a fifth. The large gas--pump asymmetry that produced motor-fuel majorities in the sixties and seventies was therefore a consequence of a mis-specified counterfactual rather than a property of how the two channels transmit. The share is also calibration-dependent in its own right: \genSpecFuelShareMinPct{} to \genSpecFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent across the seven specifications, \genDomesticLegNbpFuelShareMinPct{} to \genDomesticLegNbpFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent on the pre-war gas price alone, and \genGasProfileFuelShareMinPct{} to \genGasProfileFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent on the assumed monthly shape of the gas peak.

The claim the paper defends is the one that holds across that whole range rather than the point estimate: a large part of this shock --- never less than \genSpecFuelShareMinPct{} per cent of it --- arrives through a channel that every instrument in the Autumn Budget debate leaves untouched, because social tariff, discounted block, Warm Home Discount and VAT zero-rating all operate on the domestic bill. That is a channel result, not a targeting result, and it binds before any question of design does. It also explains the shape of the cash gradient, which is driven by vehicle ownership rather than by dwelling size or energy efficiency, and which consequently does not rise monotonically across deciles.

\paragraph{A regulatory mechanism can carry a macro scenario to a household price vector --- but only against a counterfactual it does not itself supply.} The cap formula, its averaging window, its quarterly quantisation and its non-commodity components together determine how much of a wholesale move reaches a bill and when, and this paper fixes that mechanism explicitly rather than assuming a pass-through coefficient. The mechanism also creates the identification problem it solves. Because the cap prices a forward window that closes weeks before the charge period, no published cap after the shock date is a pre-shock object, and the pre-war level must be solved rather than observed. Two published caps and the formula identify it exactly, at \pounds \genCapBaselineLevel{}, reproducing both to floating point.

The corollary discovered along the way is that the cap channel and the pump channel arrive at different speeds: pump prices pass through in weeks, the cap with a lag of one and a half quarters that follows from Ofgem's own observation windows, so a window chosen for one channel mis-states the other. That is why both legs of this paper are annualised over the same twelve months, and why the calendar-2026 comparison against the Resolution Foundation re-runs both legs on that window rather than mixing one leg's window with another's. Appendix~\ref{app:caplag} reports what the lag is worth once the phase-in profile is built from each cap period's real observation window: \pounds \genCapLagRangeGbp{} per household across lags of one to four quarters, or \genCapLagSpreadPct{} per cent of the central figure. The domestic leg's large sensitivity is not the lag at all; it is the assumed monthly shape of the gas peak, which moves the solved counterfactual cap from \pounds \genGasProfileCapMinGbp{} to \pounds \genGasProfileCapMaxGbp{} and the aggregate from \pounds \genGasProfileAggMinBn{} to \pounds \genGasProfileAggMaxBn{} billion, and leaves two cells of the sweep with no solution at all. That is the paper's real fragility and it is reported in the text.

\paragraph{The burden falls in income and the cash loss does not; the steepness is uncertain by more than a factor of two.} Decile one loses \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent of income against decile ten's \genDecileTenLossPct{} per cent, a ratio of \genDecileRatioPct{} to one. The \emph{direction} is robust: the burden falls in income in every specification, every macro scenario and every cell of the scenario grid. The \emph{magnitude} is not, and it is reported four ways within a single specification before any calibration is varied: \genDecileRatioPct{} to one headline, \genDecileRatioFromDecileTwoPct{} measured from decile two, and \genDecileRatioWinsorisedPct{} and \genDecileRatioFromDecileTwoWinsorisedPct{} winsorised on those two bases. Recalibrating motor-fuel spend to the ONS decile profile gives \genOnsFuelDecileRatioPct{} to one.

Three measurement problems bear on the magnitude and none is resolved here. The numerator problem is that the microdata over-impute motor-fuel spend to poor households, imputing an almost flat spending profile (\pounds \genFuelSpendDecileOneRawGbp{} in decile one against \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileTenRawGbp{} in decile ten) where ONS Family Spending gives \pounds \genOnsBenchFuelDecileOneGbp{} against \pounds \genOnsBenchFuelDecileTenGbp{} --- and, running the other way and compounding it, under-impute domestic energy by roughly a quarter, \pounds \genModelDomesticMeanGbp{} against ONS's \pounds \genOnsBenchDomesticMeanGbp{}. The denominator problem is that on the equivalised after-housing-costs concept the analysis divides by, \genDecileOneZeroIncomeShare{} per cent of decile one has non-positive income, and those households are dropped, along with the \genCoverageExcludedSharePct{} per cent that the model marks with a \(-1\) sentinel for the same reason.

The third is a concept problem rather than a data problem, and it is the one a reader should carry away. The decile ranking is built on equivalised \emph{before}-housing-costs income, person-weighted; the burdens are measured on equivalised \emph{after}-housing-costs income. Agreement between the two is \genDecileRankAgreementEquivAhcPrecisePct{} per cent. Every gradient in this paper is therefore a cross-concept statistic, ranked on one income concept and measured on another, and the two diverge most among exactly the low-income renters who carry the steep end of the gradient. That is a limitation rather than a detail, its sign is not determined a priori, and rebuilding the ranking on the burden concept is the most useful robustness exercise this paper does not contain.

The gradient is notably stable in one respect worth separating from the rest: measured on the domestic channel alone it is \genDomesticOnlyDecileRatioPct{} to one in every one of the seven specifications and in every cell of the scenario grid. The all-channel figure moves with the calibration because the channel mix does; the domestic-only figure does not move at all, which locates the variation in the fuel imputation rather than in the domestic leg.

In cash the ordering is the \citet{fetzer2024} one at the endpoints and considerably messier in between: \pounds\genDecileOneLossGbp{} in decile one against \pounds\genDecileTenLossGbp{} in decile ten, with the peak in neither. That non-monotonicity is a property of the main specification and not of all of them. Under both ONS calibrations the cash profile is strictly monotone increasing and peaks at decile ten --- which is what one would expect, since the interior peak is produced by the same flat motor-fuel imputation those calibrations correct, and is a further reason to read the decile-eight spike as an artefact rather than a finding.

The within-decile dispersion result requires the same correction, and it is a larger one. The mean within-decile p90--p10 range is \genWithinDecileRangePct{} percentage points against a between-decile range of \genBetweenDecileRangePct{} points, which taken at face value replicates \citet{cronin2019} on UK data. It does not survive dropping decile one: excluding it the mean within-decile range is \genWithinDecileRangeExDOnePct{} points, \emph{below} the between-decile figure, and on a median-based measure it is \genWithinDecileMedianRangePct{} points. Decile one's own range is \genDecileOneRangePct{} points against \genWithinDecileRangeMinExDOnePct{} to \genWithinDecileRangePct{} for the other nine. The numerical claim rests on the one decile in which a fifth of households have non-positive equivalised income, and it is not made here. The qualitative case for reporting within-decile outcomes does not rest on it: \citet{cronin2019} and \citet{sallee2019} establish that a transfer conditioned on observables leaves horizontal losers inside every cell of the conditioning variable whether or not the within-cell spread exceeds the between-cell one, and a decile-average scorecard cannot see them either way. That case stands, and it is made that way here. The within-decile count must in turn be read with its own limits: it is knife-edge, and comparing instruments of very different size on it will reverse policy conclusions that a continuous measure leaves standing (Section~\ref{sec:results-policy}). The measures to report together are the count, the share of aggregate loss offset, and the residual loss, each at a feasible envelope.

\paragraph{Eligibility, not generosity, is the binding margin on an exchequer envelope --- by a factor the data do not pin down.} Held at their \emph{feasible} maxima --- a 100 per cent discount on the whole shocked bill, a payment covering the eligible population's entire loss --- the social tariff and the Warm Home Discount expansion offset only \genSocialTariffEnvelopeOffsetPct{} and \genWhdEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss, because there is nothing left to give the population they can see, and neither can absorb a \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion envelope at all. Spend the same envelope on \emph{eligibility}, holding generosity exactly at each sponsor's own stated parameter, and they offset \genSocialTariffEligibilityOffsetPct{} and \genWhdEligibilityOffsetPct{} per cent. The same instrument, the same generosity, several times the loss closed. Saturation is in fact complete before the feasible maximum is reached: raising the social tariff's discount from 35 to 100 per cent moves the offset only from \genSocialTariffStatedOffsetPct{} to \genSocialTariffCappedOffsetPct{} per cent, and at that maximum the instrument pays about \pounds \genSocialTariffCappedGainPerEligibleGbp{} per eligible household against a mean loss among them of \pounds \genMeansTestedMeanLossGbp{}, so it overcompensates and this paper says so. The factor by which eligibility beats generosity is not pinned down: it depends on how little of the aggregate loss the means-tested population is imputed to carry, and under a parity fuel imputation their share rises from \genMeansTestedShareOfLossPct{} to \genMeansTestedShareOfLossParityPct{} per cent and the advantage falls to \genTargetingMultipleUnderParity{} times, with a participation-corrected specification at \genMeansTestedShareOfLossParticipationPct{} per cent in between. The direction survives all three; the factor is a range, not a point. This is the paper's coverage finding arriving from the fiscal side: an instrument conditioned on benefit receipt is bounded by the size of the set the condition can see, which in this model is \genMeansTestedHouseholdsM{} million households against a Universal Credit caseload of 7.2 million, and on this shock that bound binds before the Chancellor's budget constraint does. It has a direct implication for the Autumn Budget debate, which is conducted almost entirely in the currency of how generous each scheme should be. The more consequential question is who is in it. Two further qualifications travel with the result: the model's own under-coverage overstates how much reach there is left to buy, and widening eligibility is modelled here as a parameter rather than costed as the administrative project it would actually be. A third is worth naming because of where the margin leads --- the Warm Home Discount's widened-eligibility row reaches \genWhdEligibleSharePct{} per cent of households at \pounds 150 each, which is a universal flat rebate rather than a widened means test.

\paragraph{Where an open tax-benefit model stops.} PolicyEngine UK has no price channel: energy quantities are imputed inputs, the regulated cap enters only through defunct subsidy machinery, the one contributed lever named for household energy bills is read by nothing, the motor-fuel price parameter is implicitly unit-elastic, and the Warm Home Discount does not exist. Establishing that precisely, item by item, is a reusable result for anyone attempting macro-to-micro energy work on an open UK model, and it is specific enough to be fixed rather than merely lamented. The corresponding upstream contributions are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:upstream}.

One claim this paper does \emph{not} make is a constituency-level one. There is no constituency weight matrix in this data release and the local-authority field is degenerate, so a seat-level cross of the two incidence metrics is not producible, and no approximation to one is offered in its place. Region is the finest geography reported, and it is a weaker cut: regional means compress exactly the horizontal variation the exercise is designed to expose.

\subsection{The Känzig caveat}

The most serious objection to this paper is that it measures the wrong channel. \citet{kanzig2023} shows that when energy shocks hit, poorer households lose disproportionately not because their budget shares are higher but because their employment and earnings are more cyclically exposed, and that general-equilibrium and indirect effects account for roughly two-thirds of the household response. A static, use-side microsimulation captures none of that. \citet{goulder2019} sharpen the point: the use-side effects a consumption microsimulation measures are regressive, the source-side effects it omits are progressive, and net incidence depends on both. \citet{ohlendorf2021}'s meta-analysis is the summary statement --- whether an energy shock is judged regressive depends substantially on which channels the method admits.

I take this seriously rather than deflecting it, and it constrains the claims in three ways.

First, magnitude. If the use-side channel is roughly a third of the total, the losses reported here are a lower bound on total household incidence, and the appropriate reading of a bottom-decile loss of \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent of income is that it is the part attributable to the price of energy alone, not that household's total exposure to the conflict. A reader wanting a total should multiply, acknowledging that the multiplier is borrowed from a different country and a different identification strategy.

Second, ordering. Känzig's mechanism operates through employment, which is more concentrated among lower-income households than energy budget shares are. The omitted channel therefore almost certainly reinforces the regressivity reported here in percentage terms --- but it may well \emph{reverse} the cash-terms ordering, since employment losses in pounds are larger higher up the earnings distribution. Since the cash ordering here is already non-monotonic and driven by vehicle ownership, a general-equilibrium version of this exercise would not merely rescale the profile; it would redraw it.

Third, policy inference. The compensation metrics are internally consistent --- they compare instruments against the same shock in the same model --- so the \emph{ranking} of policies is more robust than the levels, provided the instruments are compared at a common envelope and on more than the knife-edge count; a ranking taken from mismatched budgets and a single binary metric is not robust at all. But a policy scored as fully compensating a household on its use-side loss is not thereby compensating that household, and no policy in the scorecard addresses the income channel at all. The instruments that do --- automatic stabilisers, the Universal Credit taper, statutory sick pay --- sit outside the shock being modelled and inside the model being used, which is an argument for the general-equilibrium extension rather than against the exercise.

There is also a countervailing consideration. \citet{pallotti2024} find that for the euro-area 2021--23 episode, heterogeneity in household outcomes was driven mainly by nominal net asset positions rather than by energy basket composition. If the same holds for the UK, both the use-side channel modelled here and the employment channel Känzig emphasises are second to a balance-sheet channel that requires an up-to-date wealth distribution to measure. The model's wealth imputation rests on Wealth and Assets Survey round 7 (2018--20), which predates the entire rate cycle, so this paper cannot speak to it.

\subsection{Three gaps this paper makes visible}

Each of the three limitations most binding on this analysis is also a well-defined and, as far as I can establish, unfilled research question.

\paragraph{(a) UK wholesale-to-retail pass-through in the price-cap era.} The coefficient $\phi_f$ and lag $\ell$ in equation~\eqref{eq:passthrough} are the load-bearing parameters of Step 1, and they are calibrated from the published structure of the cap rather than estimated. That is because there is no well-cited econometric estimate of wholesale-to-retail energy price pass-through for the UK under the default tariff cap. The pre-cap literature is not transferable: the cap replaced a competitive-retail pricing mechanism with a formula, changing both the horizon and the functional form of transmission, and the formula has been amended repeatedly since 2019 --- most substantially in the 2022 methodology review --- so the estimation problem is a regime-switching one on a short sample. The data exist. Ofgem publishes the cap decompositions, the averaging windows and the announcement dates; NBP and TTF forward curves are observable daily; DESNZ publishes retail price statistics. The identification comes from the announcement calendar itself: the cap is a deterministic function of forwards over a pre-announced window, so the mapping is estimable and its residual --- the part of retail prices not explained by the formula --- is the object of interest for fixed-tariff and out-of-cap customers. This is the most immediately tractable of the three gaps and would tighten every number in this paper, including the lag whose consequences Appendix~\ref{app:caplag} reports.

\paragraph{(b) Energy arrears as an outcome variable.} The paper's outcome is a cost increase. The observable manifestation of the 2026 shock, however, is debt: JRF report around 1.5 million families in energy arrears and 3.8 million households owing roughly \pounds 7.5 billion. Arrears are not a mechanical function of the cost increase, because they depend on the household's liquidity position, its access to credit, supplier forbearance practice, and the prepayment-versus-credit-meter split. Modelling them requires an intertemporal margin the model does not have, and modelling the policy response to them requires representing debt-related allowances --- which now sit inside the cap itself, so that arrears feed back into everybody's unit rate. That feedback is a genuinely interesting object: a shock that pushes some households into arrears raises the debt allowance in the next cap period and hence raises bills for all households, a within-consumer cross-subsidy with its own incidence. Nobody has quantified it for the UK. It would also change the policy ranking, since a social tariff that prevents arrears has a fiscal return the current scorecard does not credit it with.

\paragraph{(c) Off-gas-grid and rural horizontal losers --- untested here.} \citet{douenne2020} shows for France that a flat recycling scheme is progressive on average while creating large horizontal losers concentrated among rural and off-grid poor households --- people whose heating fuel is unregulated, whose homes are older and less efficient, and whose transport is non-substitutable. There is no UK counterpart to that result, and this paper does not supply one, because the data will not support the split it requires. The microdata contain no primary-heating-fuel indicator and no gas-grid-connection flag, so losses cannot be split on gas-grid status at all --- not unreliably, but not at all --- and no imputed stand-in is offered. The gap therefore remains exactly as open as Douenne left it, and it is a live one: roughly four million UK properties are off the gas grid, heating oil and LPG sit wholly outside the price cap and outside the Warm Home Discount's implicit design assumptions, and these households were repeatedly omitted from 2022 support schemes until belated top-ups. The finding that motor fuel carries a large part of the loss on every calibration makes the rural question sharper still, since rural transport dependence and off-grid heating coincide in the same households. A dedicated study combining EPC records, off-grid fuel price series and rural transport dependence would fill it; adding a heating-fuel indicator to the UK microdata is the prerequisite, and it is the third of the upstream contributions in Appendix~\ref{app:upstream}.

\subsection{Where this leaves the macro-to-micro link}

The Seventh Carbon Budget established ``macro model plus distributional annex'' as the expected format for UK official analysis, but the annex is typically produced by a separate method on separate data, so the macro and micro numbers are not mutually consistent. What this paper does is small but structural: it fixes a single explicit transmission mechanism as the bridge --- the cap formula for domestic energy, and for road fuel a pass-through coefficient fitted to observed pump-price outturns rather than a duty-and-VAT accounting identity (Section~\ref{sec:step1}) --- so that the household results are a deterministic function of the macro scenario and can be regenerated when the scenario changes. The bridge is currently one-way and covers only the price channel, and it currently sits alongside the tax-benefit model rather than inside it, because the model has no price channel to host it. Moving it inside, extending it to the income channel, and closing the loop so that the household response feeds back into aggregate demand is the natural continuation, and gap (a) is its prerequisite.
