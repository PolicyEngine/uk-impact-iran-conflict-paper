\section{The Autumn Budget 2026 choice}
\label{sec:policy}

The results in Section~\ref{sec:results} bear directly on a decision that has not yet been taken. The Chancellor has ruled out universal support of the 2022 kind and, as trailed in press reporting rather than in any published Treasury document, is said to favour income-based help --- in practice, a social tariff --- while the Joseph Rowntree Foundation and the TUC have pressed for a universal discounted block costing around \pounds \genJrfStatedCostBn{} billion \citep{jrf2026}. I have located no HM Treasury or Budget paper stating the first intention and treat it throughout as a reported signal rather than as policy. This section sets out what the designs do differently, and it reaches two conclusions the debate does not anticipate. The first is that the choice between a targeted and a universal \emph{bill} instrument is a smaller choice than either side treats it as, because both are confined to the same channel. The second is that the margin on which an exchequer envelope is best spent is \emph{eligibility}, not generosity: held at their feasible maxima the two means-tested instruments offset a small fraction of the aggregate loss, because there is nothing left to give the population they can see, while spending the same envelope on reach at their sponsors' own stated generosity offsets several times as much. That second conclusion is conditional on an imputation this paper cannot validate, and the condition is set out where the finding is stated rather than in a footnote.

\subsection{The constraint that binds before targeting does}
\label{sec:policy-channel}

Every instrument in the live debate --- social tariff, discounted block, Warm Home Discount, VAT zero-rating --- operates on the domestic energy bill. Motor fuel carries \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate household loss in the main specification, and between \genSpecFuelShareMinPct{} and \genSpecFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent across every calibration reported in Section~\ref{sec:results-fuel}. Whether it constitutes a majority depends on the calibration, and this paper does not claim a large one; that it constitutes a large part of the loss does not depend on the calibration. The domestic-bill policy debate is therefore aimed at part of the problem, and it is aimed there by construction rather than by design failure. No calibration of a bill-based instrument, however generous or however well targeted, can compensate a loss it does not touch.

This is the deepest reason the scorecard in Table~\ref{tab:scorecard} reads as poorly as it does, and it should be separated from the targeting question that occupies the debate. A social tariff that perfectly identified every household in fuel poverty and fully offset its domestic bill increase would still leave the motor-fuel loss untouched --- \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate in the main specification, and never less than \genSpecFuelShareMinPct{} per cent on any calibration here. Targeting design determines who gets reached within the share that bill instruments can address; the channel constraint determines the size of that share, and on no calibration in this paper is that share the whole shock.

\subsection{Two designs, one disagreement --- and one channel}

A \emph{means-tested social tariff} conditions a discounted unit rate on receipt of a qualifying benefit. Its appeal is arithmetic: every pound of spend goes to a household that has already passed a means test, so it leads the field on both conventional targeting metrics. Table~\ref{tab:scorecard} confirms that it does: \genSocialTariffShareBottomThree{} per cent of its spend reaches deciles one to three, against \genJrfShareBottomThree{} per cent for the block, at a cost of \pounds\genSocialTariffCostBn{} billion.

The \emph{universal discounted block} does something structurally different. It prices the first half of typical consumption at a discounted rate and everything above it at the full rate, with a per-child allowance recognising that household need scales with size. Because the discount applies to a fixed quantity rather than to all consumption, its cash value is roughly flat across the distribution while high-consumption households --- disproportionately affluent, per \citet{fetzer2024} --- pay the full marginal rate on the bulk of their usage. It is therefore neither a universal price subsidy of the Energy Price Guarantee type, which cost around \pounds 23 billion and was regressive in cash terms precisely because it subsidised every unit, nor a transfer. JRF argue, on operational rather than distributional grounds, that a social tariff cannot be built in time for winter 2026: there is no register of eligible households that is both current and complete, and constructing one is a multi-year data-sharing project.

On the uncompensated count the two designs are far apart, and in the direction the universal case predicts: at each sponsor's own cost the social tariff leaves \genSocialTariffUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losing households short of their loss and the block \genJrfUncompensatedShare{} per cent. Most of that gap is size rather than design, since the block costs \pounds \genJrfCostBn{} billion against the social tariff's \pounds \genSocialTariffCostBn{} billion, which is why the common-envelope comparison below is the one to read.

How much of the difference is design and how much is money is answered by the common envelope --- but only once the envelope is spent on something each instrument can actually do. Held at its \emph{feasible} maximum, which for a bill discount is a 100 per cent discount on the eligible population's whole shocked bill, the social tariff can absorb only \pounds \genSocialTariffAbsorbableBn{} billion of a \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion envelope and offsets \genSocialTariffEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss, with a mean residual loss of \pounds \genSocialTariffEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{}. The block absorbs the whole envelope and offsets \genJrfEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent, with a mean residual of \pounds \genJrfEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{}. The gap is wide and it is not principally a gap in generosity: the social tariff has run out of eligible households long before it has run out of money.

That is the saturation mechanism stated correctly. A means-tested instrument concentrates spending on a population small enough that an envelope of this size over-compensates much of it, and every pound past that point closes nothing, because it lands on households whose loss is already covered; a broad instrument keeps finding losing households that are still short. Scaling the parameter until the envelope is exhausted, rather than stopping at what the instrument can do, produces a \genSocialTariffScaledParameterPct{} per cent bill discount and a \pounds \genWhdScaledParameterGbp{} Warm Home Discount. Those rows appear in Table~\ref{tab:envelope} under generic names --- a proportional domestic-bill subsidy at \genSocialTariffScaledParameterPct{} per cent on the means-tested population, a flat payment of \pounds \genWhdScaledParameterGbp{} a year to the same population --- because they are not the policies whose names they would otherwise carry, and no conclusion in this paper rests on them.

One note on generosity, which is a statement about bases rather than about designs. JRF cost their proposal at roughly \pounds \genJrfStatedCostBn{} billion; the block implemented here --- a 50 per cent discount on the level of the first half of median consumption, plus \pounds 60 per child, with the child count taken from the model --- costs \pounds\genJrfCostBn{} billion. The two figures cannot be read against each other. JRF size the block on Ofgem's typical-consumption basis, which puts the half-block at \pounds \genJrfBlockOfgemBasisGbp{}; this model's weighted median domestic bill puts it at \pounds \genJrfBlockModelledGbp{}, about half as much. A comparison of costs across bases that differ by a factor of two is a comparison of two different quantities of energy, not of two designs, and no claim that this paper's version is more or less generous than JRF's is made here. What does not depend on the base is the channel constraint that caps how far the uncompensated share could fall at any budget.

\subsection{The crux: the households outside the means test}

The anchor statistic of every Budget submission is that approximately 40 per cent of households struggling to heat their homes are not on means-tested benefits (JRF's figure, on their own definition of struggling). Earlier drafts of this paper offered a comparable-looking figure: the share of households losing more than five per cent of net income that sit outside the modelled means-tested system. That statistic is not reported here, and the reason is arithmetic rather than uncertainty. With a modelled means-tested population of \genMeansTestedShareHouseholds{} per cent of households, the share of \emph{any} group falling outside it cannot go below about \genLargeLoserCeilingPct{} per cent however the shock is distributed. A figure in the high nineties is therefore close to what a coin would produce and carries almost no information about the incidence of this shock. It is dropped rather than qualified.

What the model does support is the structural point, which does not need a percentage.

A means test that is perfectly accurate on the population it covers still leaves the entire uncovered population at zero compensation, and that population is not a random sample of the non-poor: it is concentrated among low-income households above the benefit taper, households with savings above the capital limit, pensioners on modest occupational incomes, the self-employed with volatile earnings, and non-claimants. It also, in this exercise, includes everyone whose loss is a motor-fuel loss, which no bill instrument reaches at any income level.

The Warm Home Discount reform reported for the 2026--27 scheme year --- property-cost test scrapped, the \pounds 150 payment automatic in England and Wales, around 2.7 million additional households brought in --- would narrow this gap without closing it, because eligibility still runs through benefit receipt. Here too the description rests on press reporting: I have located no published Government document setting the reform out in those terms, and it is modelled as reported rather than as legislated. In the scorecard the expanded Warm Home Discount is the best-targeted instrument on the between-decile metric, sending \genWhdShareBottomThree{} per cent of a \pounds\genWhdCostBn{} billion outlay to deciles one to three, while leaving \genWhdUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losers short of their full loss. Part of that is its small size, and part is that a flat payment conditioned on benefit receipt cannot see the losing households outside it. That combination is the paper's compensation problem in a single row.

\subsection{Eligibility, not generosity, is the binding margin}
\label{sec:policy-eligibility}

This is the paper's central policy finding, and it is a stronger one than the claim it replaces. Take the two means-tested instruments and give each the same \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion. Spend it on \emph{generosity} and neither can absorb it: at a 100 per cent discount on the whole shocked domestic bill the social tariff exhausts at \pounds \genSocialTariffAbsorbableBn{} billion and offsets \genSocialTariffEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss; at the payment that covers the eligible population's entire loss the Warm Home Discount expansion exhausts at \pounds \genWhdAbsorbableBn{} billion and offsets \genWhdEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent. There is simply nothing left to give the population these instruments can see. Now hold generosity exactly at each sponsor's own stated parameter --- a 35 per cent discount, a \pounds 150 payment --- and spend the envelope on \emph{reach} instead, extending eligibility beyond the modelled means-tested population. The social tariff then offsets \genSocialTariffEligibilityOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss at a cost of \pounds \genSocialTariffEligibilityCostBn{} billion, and the Warm Home Discount \genWhdEligibilityOffsetPct{} per cent at \pounds \genWhdEligibilityCostBn{} billion. The same money, the same instrument, the same generosity: several times the loss closed.

Three things have to be said with that comparison rather than after it, and each weakens it.

First, saturation is complete at the sponsor's own parameter, not merely at the feasible maximum. Raising the social tariff's discount from 35 to 100 per cent --- nearly a threefold increase in generosity --- moves the offset from \genSocialTariffStatedOffsetPct{} to \genSocialTariffCappedOffsetPct{} per cent. There is no interior point at which extra generosity buys anything.

Second, the instruments plainly overcompensate at that maximum, and this paper makes no claim otherwise. At its feasible maximum the social tariff pays roughly \pounds \genSocialTariffCappedGainPerEligibleGbp{} to each eligible household against a mean loss among them of \pounds \genMeansTestedMeanLossGbp{}. Any statement that no household is overcompensated in these rows is false and is not made here.

Third, and most consequentially, the size of the eligibility advantage rests on how little of the aggregate loss the means-tested population is imputed to carry, and that imputation cannot be validated (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Means-tested households are given \pounds \genMeansTestedFuelSpendRawGbp{} of annual motor-fuel spending against \pounds \genNonMeansTestedFuelSpendRawGbp{} for everyone else, and \genZeroFuelShareMeansTestedRawPct{} per cent of them record no motor fuel at all against \genZeroFuelShareOverallRawPct{} per cent of all households of all households. A parity specification, which gives them the population's own fuel profile, raises their share of the aggregate loss from \genMeansTestedShareOfLossPct{} to \genMeansTestedShareOfLossParityPct{} per cent, and the eligibility advantage falls from \genTargetingMultipleStated{} times to \genTargetingMultipleUnderParity{}. A participation-corrected specification, which fixes only the zero-fuel rate, gives \genMeansTestedShareOfLossParticipationPct{} per cent and \genTargetingMultipleUnderParticipation{} times. The finding is therefore that eligibility beats generosity by a factor somewhere between \genTargetingMultipleUnderParity{} and \genTargetingMultipleStated{}, conditional on a fuel imputation this paper flags as defective in the same section that reports it. The direction is not in doubt on any of the three specifications. The factor is, and the range is what should be quoted.

Within those limits the comparison is at least clean in construction, because nothing in either arm is infeasible. The feasible-maximum arm asks the instrument to do the most it can do; the widened-eligibility arm asks it to do the same thing to more people. Neither requires a discount above 100 per cent or a negative rate of tax. But one of the two eligibility rows should be read for what it is rather than for its name: the Warm Home Discount's widened-eligibility row reaches \genWhdEligibleSharePct{} per cent of households at \pounds 150 each, which is not a widened Warm Home Discount at all. It is arithmetically a universal flat rebate at a different parameter, and the fact that this is where the eligibility margin leads is itself part of the finding.

It also converges on the paper's own coverage finding from the fiscal side rather than the incidence side, and the two are the same fact. The modelled means-tested population is \genMeansTestedHouseholdsM{} million households, \genMeansTestedShareHouseholds{} per cent of the total, against a Universal Credit caseload alone of 7.2 million households in May 2026 (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}); widening eligibility to \genSocialTariffEligibleSharePct{} per cent of households is what it takes to spend \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion at the sponsor's own generosity. An instrument conditioned on benefit receipt is bounded not by the Chancellor's willingness to pay but by the size of the set the condition can see, and on this shock that bound binds first. JRF's operational argument --- that no register of eligible households exists that is both current and complete --- is therefore not a delivery quibble alongside the distributional argument. It is the distributional argument.

Two qualifications belong with the finding rather than after it. First, the model's own means-tested population is well under half the administrative caseload, so the room the eligibility arm has to expand is overstated here: a means test that already reached its full caseload would have less coverage left to buy, and the gap between the two arms would be smaller than \genSocialTariffEnvelopeOffsetPct{} against \genSocialTariffEligibilityOffsetPct{} per cent. Second, widening eligibility is an administrative act this paper models as a parameter and does not cost: identifying households outside the benefit system is exactly the problem that makes a social tariff hard to build, and an envelope spent on reach is spent on something that does not yet exist. What the result establishes is where the return to spending lies, not that the spending is easy.

A caveat of the paper's own making applies to both means-tested rows. The modelled means-tested population is \genMeansTestedShareHouseholds{} per cent of households --- \genMeansTestedHouseholdsM{} million --- against a Universal Credit caseload alone of 7.2 million households, a shortfall of a factor of two and a half on the largest means-tested benefit (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The reach of the social tariff and the Warm Home Discount is therefore materially understated here and their uncompensated shares are an upper bound rather than an estimate. The ranking against the untargeted instruments is robust to this; the exact percentages are not.

\subsection{VAT zero-rating: an arithmetic ceiling}

Zero-rating VAT on domestic fuel and power is the most frequently proposed instrument in the public debate. At its own cost of \pounds\genVatCutCostBn{} billion it leaves \genVatCutUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losing households short of their full loss. That number is correct and nearly uninformative: it counts a household left a pound short identically to one left five hundred pounds short, it invites comparison against instruments costing several times as much, and it is computed against a loss that is itself an upper bound.

Nor can the instrument be rescued or condemned by a common-envelope comparison, because it cannot occupy one. The reduced rate on domestic fuel and power is five points; removing all five is the whole of the instrument, and it costs \pounds \genVatCutCostBn{} billion. Scaled to \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion it becomes a proportional domestic-bill subsidy at \genVatCutScaledParameterPct{} per cent --- a negative rate of VAT --- and any ranking generated at that parameter is a ranking of something else.

What survives is the arithmetic ceiling itself, which is the honest finding about this instrument and does not need an envelope comparison to state. Zero-rating returns at most $1 - 1/1.05 = 4.8$ per cent of a VAT-inclusive domestic bill, against a domestic bill increase which, on the annual applied basis every result in this paper uses, is \genAnnualPhaseInGasPct{} per cent of a steady-state gas move of \genGasUnitRatePct{} per cent and \genAnnualPhaseInElecPct{} per cent of a steady-state electricity move of \genElecUnitRatePct{} per cent (Section~\ref{sec:results-prices}) --- an order of magnitude larger either way. At its own feasible maximum --- which is also its own stated cost, the two coinciding for this instrument alone --- it offsets \genVatCutEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss and leaves a mean residual of \pounds \genVatCutEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{}. That is a real number and a modest one, it is a full order of magnitude short of the shock, and no amount of exchequer generosity moves it, because the instrument has no parameter left to turn. On the conventional targeting metrics it also remains the weakest instrument here --- \genVatCutShareBottomThree{} per cent of spend to the bottom three deciles, and the highest cost per pound of bottom-decile gain in the table at \genWorstCostPerPound{}, a dimensionless ratio rather than a sum of money. It reaches no motor fuel at all, road fuel already bearing standard-rate VAT.

There is also a live complication specific to this row: Ofgem's temporary removal of VAT on electricity from 1 October 2026 may already sit in the baseline against which this instrument is scored (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), in which case part of the relief credited here has already been granted. That would make the modest figure above an overstatement rather than an understatement.

\subsection{Against the ``always target'' consensus}

The grey-literature consensus after 2022 is unambiguous: compensate incomes, not prices, and target. \citet{ari2022} is the standard reference, and the Bruegel fiscal tracker's finding that only around a third of European energy support was targeted is the standard evidence. The reasoning is familiar --- a price subsidy distorts the marginal incentive to conserve, its cost scales with consumption and therefore with affluence, and it blunts the price signal that is supposed to induce adjustment.

The results here speak against that consensus in its strong form, though narrowly. The two instruments that compensate the most losing households are the two that do not means-test: the universal discounted block, which fully compensates \genJrfFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent of losers at \pounds\genJrfCostBn{} billion, and the flat untargeted IPPR rebate, which fully compensates \genRebateFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent at \pounds\genRebateCostBn{} billion. The two best-targeted instruments, at their own much smaller costs, compensate \genSocialTariffFullyCompensatedShare{} and \genWhdFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent. Part of that difference is generosity, and saying so is the honest correction. But the common envelope does not simply narrow the gap: at a feasible common envelope the means-tested instruments cannot spend it, and what separates the instruments is how many losing households each can \emph{see}. On the conventional metrics the untargeted instruments look poor --- the rebate sends \genRebateShareBottomThree{} per cent to the bottom three deciles at a cost-per-pound ratio of \genRebateCostPerPound{}, against \genBestCostPerPound{} for the best-targeted instrument --- which is precisely the point. The metrics that endorse targeting and the metrics that measure whether losers are made whole rank the options differently.

This is \citet{levell2025}'s result in applied form. Their treatment of the 2022--23 UK episode finds that the constrained-optimal policy includes a \emph{strictly positive} untargeted component, shrinking but never vanishing as the transfer instrument is allowed to condition on more information. The reason is exactly the coverage gap above: an untargeted instrument reaches households that no observable-based transfer can identify, and when the unidentified losing population is large --- here, overwhelmingly large --- the insurance value of that reach outweighs the deadweight loss. \citet{sallee2019} supplies the general statement: when transfers must condition on observables, compensating every loser is not merely expensive but infeasible, and the residual is not noise. \citet{cronin2019} and \citet{douenne2020} establish the same point from the incidence side.

Three qualifications keep this from being read as a blanket endorsement of universality. First, the rebate is expensive relative to what it achieves for the poorest: it is a broadly-compensating instrument, not an efficient one, and a Chancellor with a binding envelope faces a genuine trade-off rather than a free lunch. Second, its performance here rests on a flat cash payment against a loss distribution whose cash gradient is comparatively shallow; against a shock with a steeper cash gradient the same instrument would perform worse. Third, the saturation mechanism that drives the eligibility result depends on how many households a means-tested instrument can actually find, and the model finds well under half the Universal Credit caseload (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}); a means test reaching its full administrative population would saturate later than the one scored here, and the gap between the generosity and eligibility arms of Section~\ref{sec:policy-eligibility} would be correspondingly smaller. The claim defended is narrow and specific: for this shock, on these instruments, and at these coverage rates, targeting buys between-decile efficiency at the cost of leaving most losers short, and the return to an extra pound of exchequer money lies on the eligibility margin rather than the generosity one.

\subsection{Financing}

The financing side is scored separately, because netting it into the household distribution would conflate two distinct incidence questions.

IPPR's proposal is to claw back windfalls accruing to regulated network companies --- whose allowed returns were set against a very different cost of capital and whose revenues are indexed --- and return the proceeds as a flat household rebate of around \pounds 183. Its distinctive feature is that its financing is close to a pure rent tax and therefore carries little efficiency cost, which is not true of the alternatives.

The other financing routes on the table are the successor arrangements to the Energy Profits Levy on oil and gas production, and an increase in the Electricity Generator Levy from 45 to 55 per cent. Both are windfall instruments whose yield is mechanically correlated with the shock being compensated, which is an attractive property: the fiscal cost of support and the revenue that pays for it move together, so the package is closer to self-financing in the adverse scenario than in the baseline. Both also carry investment-margin costs that a static microsimulation cannot evaluate and that the source-side literature \citep{goulder2019} suggests are progressive in incidence --- reducing returns to capital falls on capital owners, who are concentrated at the top --- so excluding them from the household results understates the progressivity of the financed package as a whole. I report the household side of each policy and the aggregate cost, and note the direction of the omitted source-side effect rather than attempting to quantify it.

Finally, none of the five instruments addresses the motor-fuel channel. A fuel-duty response would, and it is conspicuously absent from the Budget debate this paper reads. Scoring one is straightforward in this framework and is left to further work only because the duty response actually under consideration was not on the table at the time of writing.
