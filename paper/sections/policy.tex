\section{The Autumn Budget 2026 choice}
\label{sec:policy}

The results in Section~\ref{sec:results} bear directly on a decision that has not yet been taken. The Chancellor has ruled out universal support of the 2022 kind and signalled income-based help --- in practice, a social tariff --- while the Joseph Rowntree Foundation and the TUC have pressed for a universal discounted block costing around \pounds 5 billion. This section sets out what the designs do differently, and it reaches two conclusions the debate does not anticipate. The first is that the choice between a targeted and a universal \emph{bill} instrument is a smaller choice than either side treats it as, because both are confined to the same channel. The second is that once the instruments are compared at a common exchequer envelope and on continuous measures rather than on a knife-edge count, the ordering the debate assumes --- targeting first, breadth last --- does not hold.

\subsection{The constraint that binds before targeting does}
\label{sec:policy-channel}

Every instrument in the live debate --- social tariff, discounted block, Warm Home Discount, VAT zero-rating --- operates on the domestic energy bill. Motor fuel carries \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate household loss in the main specification, and between \genSpecFuelShareMinPct{} and \genSpecFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent across every calibration reported in Section~\ref{sec:results-fuel}. Whether that constitutes a majority depends on the calibration; that it constitutes a large part of the loss does not. The domestic-bill policy debate is therefore aimed at part of the problem, and it is aimed there by construction rather than by design failure. No calibration of a bill-based instrument, however generous or however well targeted, can compensate a loss it does not touch.

This is the deepest reason the scorecard in Table~\ref{tab:scorecard} reads as poorly as it does, and it should be separated from the targeting question that occupies the debate. A social tariff that perfectly identified every household in fuel poverty and fully offset its domestic bill increase would still leave the motor-fuel loss untouched --- \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate in the main specification, and never less than \genSpecFuelShareMinPct{} per cent on any calibration here. Targeting design determines who gets reached within the share that bill instruments can address; the channel constraint determines the size of that share, and on no calibration in this paper is that share the whole shock.

\subsection{Two designs, one disagreement --- and one channel}

A \emph{means-tested social tariff} conditions a discounted unit rate on receipt of a qualifying benefit. Its appeal is arithmetic: every pound of spend goes to a household that has already passed a means test, so on the two conventional targeting metrics it dominates. Table~\ref{tab:scorecard} confirms that it does: \genSocialTariffShareBottomThree{} per cent of its spend reaches deciles one to three, against \genJrfShareBottomThree{} per cent for the block, at a cost of \pounds\genSocialTariffCostBn{} billion.

The \emph{universal discounted block} does something structurally different. It prices the first half of typical consumption at a discounted rate and everything above it at the full rate, with a per-child allowance recognising that household need scales with size. Because the discount applies to a fixed quantity rather than to all consumption, its cash value is roughly flat across the distribution while high-consumption households --- disproportionately affluent, per \citet{fetzer2024} --- pay the full marginal rate on the bulk of their usage. It is therefore neither a universal price subsidy of the Energy Price Guarantee type, which cost around \pounds 23 billion and was regressive in cash terms precisely because it subsidised every unit, nor a transfer. JRF argue, on operational rather than distributional grounds, that a social tariff cannot be built in time for winter 2026: there is no register of eligible households that is both current and complete, and constructing one is a multi-year data-sharing project.

On the uncompensated count the two designs are now far apart, and in the direction the universal case predicts: at each sponsor's own cost the social tariff leaves \genSocialTariffUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losing households short of their loss and the block \genJrfUncompensatedShare{} per cent. An earlier version of this paper reported the two as indistinguishable on this metric. That finding was an artefact of scoring a mis-specified block --- a discount on the shock component rather than on the level --- and it is withdrawn.

How much of the difference is design and how much is money is answered by the common envelope, and the answer is: mostly money, but not entirely. Scaled to \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion each, the block offsets \genJrfEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss against the social tariff's \genSocialTariffEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent, with mean residual losses of \pounds \genJrfEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{} and \pounds \genSocialTariffEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{}. The gap narrows and does not close. The mechanism is saturation: the social tariff's budget is concentrated on a modelled means-tested population small enough that a \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion envelope over-compensates much of it, so the marginal pound closes nothing, while the block keeps finding losing households that are still short. The social tariff concentrates a budget on the bottom three deciles; the block spreads it across the distribution and, on this shock, closes more of the loss for it.

One honest note on generosity, and it is a correction. JRF cost their proposal at roughly \pounds 5 billion. An earlier version of this paper scored a discount on the \emph{shock component} of the block, costing under \pounds 2 billion, and concluded from the gap that the scheme scored here was materially less generous than the one JRF propose. That was a specification error, not a calibration difference: JRF propose a discounted rate on the \emph{level} of the block. Scored as proposed --- a 50 per cent discount on the level of the first half of median consumption, plus \pounds 60 per child, with the child count taken from the model rather than proxied by household size --- it costs \pounds\genJrfCostBn{} billion, so the block scored here is now \emph{more} generous than JRF's own costing. The earlier conclusion is withdrawn in both its parts: this is not an under-funded version of their scheme, and the uncompensated share reported for it cannot be attributed to under-funding. What does not change is the channel constraint that caps how far that share could fall at any budget.

\subsection{The crux: the households outside the means test}

The anchor statistic of every Budget submission is that approximately 40 per cent of households struggling to heat their homes are not on means-tested benefits (JRF's figure, on their own definition of struggling). My comparable figure is measured differently: of households losing more than five per cent of net income to the shock, \genLargeLoserOutsideMeansTest{} per cent sit outside the \emph{modelled} means-tested system. That figure needs its qualifier stated in the same breath as the number. It is an output of a model whose means-tested population is well under half the administrative Universal Credit caseload (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), so it is an upper bound on a modelled quantity rather than a statement about how many large losers in Britain are outside the benefit system --- the true share is certainly lower, and I do not know by how much. It and JRF's figure are not the same object and should not be conflated, but both point the same way, and the direction is what the policy argument rests on.

A means test that is perfectly accurate on the population it covers still leaves the entire uncovered population at zero compensation, and that population is not a random sample of the non-poor: it is concentrated among low-income households above the benefit taper, households with savings above the capital limit, pensioners on modest occupational incomes, the self-employed with volatile earnings, and non-claimants. It also, in this exercise, includes everyone whose loss is a motor-fuel loss, which no bill instrument reaches at any income level.

The Warm Home Discount reform that took effect for the 2026--27 scheme year --- qualifying date 23 August 2026, property-cost test scrapped, the \pounds 150 payment automatic in England and Wales, around 2.7 million additional households brought in --- narrows this gap but does not close it, because eligibility still runs through benefit receipt. In the scorecard the expanded Warm Home Discount is the best-targeted instrument on the between-decile metric, sending \genWhdShareBottomThree{} per cent of a \pounds\genWhdCostBn{} billion outlay to deciles one to three, while leaving \genWhdUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losers short of their full loss and offsetting \genWhdEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss when scaled to the common envelope. Part of that is its small size, and part is that a flat payment conditioned on benefit receipt cannot see the losing households outside it. That combination is the paper's compensation problem in a single row.

A caveat of the paper's own making applies to both means-tested rows. The modelled means-tested population is \genMeansTestedShareHouseholds{} per cent of households --- \genMeansTestedHouseholdsM{} million --- against a Universal Credit caseload alone of 7.2 million households, a shortfall of a factor of two and a half on the largest means-tested benefit (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). The reach of the social tariff and the Warm Home Discount is therefore materially understated here and their uncompensated shares are an upper bound rather than an estimate. The ranking against the untargeted instruments is robust to this; the exact percentages are not.

\subsection{VAT zero-rating: the instrument the scorecard was mis-scoring}

Zero-rating VAT on domestic fuel and power is the most frequently proposed instrument in the public debate, and earlier versions of this paper called it the worst performer in the scorecard on the strength of a single number: at its own cost of \pounds\genVatCutCostBn{} billion it leaves \genVatCutUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losing households short of their full loss. That number is correct and it is nearly uninformative. It counts a household left a pound short identically to one left five hundred pounds short, it was being compared against instruments costing several times as much, and it is computed against a loss that is itself an upper bound.

On the measures that ask how much of the loss is actually closed, and at a common \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion envelope, VAT zero-rating is the best of the five: it offsets \genVatCutEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss and leaves the lowest mean residual loss in the table, \pounds \genVatCutEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{}. The reason is that it saturates last. A means-tested instrument given a large envelope soon pays households more than they lost, and every pound past that point closes nothing; a broad instrument keeps finding losers who are still short. This is a real property of the compensation problem under a shock whose losers are spread across the distribution, and it is not visible on either of the conventional targeting metrics, on which VAT zero-rating remains the weakest instrument here --- \genVatCutShareBottomThree{} per cent of spend to the bottom three deciles and the highest cost per pound of bottom-decile gain, \pounds \genWorstCostPerPound{}.

Two things are unchanged and should not be lost in the correction. The arithmetic ceiling is real: the reduced rate is 5 per cent, so zero-rating returns at most $1 - 1/1.05 = 4.8$ per cent of a VAT-inclusive domestic bill, against a domestic bill increase of \genGasUnitRatePct{} per cent on gas and \genElecUnitRatePct{} per cent on electricity, which is why it fully compensates almost nobody at its own cost. And it reaches no motor fuel at all, road fuel already bearing standard-rate VAT. The instrument that closes the most loss per pound of envelope is still an instrument that cannot close the loss. There is also a live complication specific to this row: Ofgem's temporary removal of VAT on electricity from 1 October 2026 may already sit in the baseline against which this instrument is scored (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), in which case part of the relief credited here has already been granted.

\subsection{Against the ``always target'' consensus}

The grey-literature consensus after 2022 is unambiguous: compensate incomes, not prices, and target. \citet{ari2022} is the standard reference, and the Bruegel fiscal tracker's finding that only around a third of European energy support was targeted is the standard evidence. The reasoning is familiar --- a price subsidy distorts the marginal incentive to conserve, its cost scales with consumption and therefore with affluence, and it blunts the price signal that is supposed to induce adjustment.

The results here speak against that consensus in its strong form, though for a narrower reason than the earlier version of this paper claimed. The two instruments that compensate the most losing households are the two that do not means-test: the universal discounted block, which fully compensates \genJrfFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent of losers at \pounds\genJrfCostBn{} billion, and the flat untargeted IPPR rebate, which fully compensates \genRebateFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent at \pounds\genRebateCostBn{} billion. The two best-targeted instruments, at their own much smaller costs, compensate \genSocialTariffFullyCompensatedShare{} and \genWhdFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent. Part of that difference is generosity, and saying so is the honest correction: at a common envelope the gaps narrow, and what then separates the instruments is how quickly each saturates rather than universality as such. On the conventional metrics the untargeted instruments look poor --- the rebate sends \genRebateShareBottomThree{} per cent to the bottom three deciles at \pounds \genRebateCostPerPound{} per pound of bottom-decile gain, against \pounds \genBestCostPerPound{} for the best-targeted instrument --- which is precisely the point. The metrics that endorse targeting and the metrics that measure whether losers are made whole rank the options differently.

This is \citet{levell2025}'s result in applied form. Their treatment of the 2022--23 UK episode finds that the constrained-optimal policy includes a \emph{strictly positive} untargeted component, shrinking but never vanishing as the transfer instrument is allowed to condition on more information. The reason is exactly the coverage gap above: an untargeted instrument reaches households that no observable-based transfer can identify, and when the unidentified losing population is large --- here, overwhelmingly large --- the insurance value of that reach outweighs the deadweight loss. \citet{sallee2019} supplies the general statement: when transfers must condition on observables, compensating every loser is not merely expensive but infeasible, and the residual is not noise. \citet{cronin2019} and \citet{douenne2020} establish the same point from the incidence side.

Three qualifications keep this from being read as a blanket endorsement of universality. First, the rebate is expensive relative to what it achieves for the poorest: it is a broadly-compensating instrument, not an efficient one, and a Chancellor with a binding envelope faces a genuine trade-off rather than a free lunch. Second, its performance here rests on a flat cash payment against a loss distribution whose cash gradient is comparatively shallow; against a shock with a steeper cash gradient the same instrument would perform worse. Third, the saturation mechanism that drives the common-envelope result depends on how many households a means-tested instrument can actually find, and the model finds well under half the Universal Credit caseload (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}); a means test reaching its full administrative population would saturate later than the one scored here. The claim defended is narrow and specific: for this shock, on these instruments, and at these coverage rates, targeting buys between-decile efficiency at the cost of leaving most losers short, and broader instruments close more of the loss per pound of envelope.

\subsection{Financing}

The financing side is scored separately, because netting it into the household distribution would conflate two distinct incidence questions.

IPPR's proposal is to claw back windfalls accruing to regulated network companies --- whose allowed returns were set against a very different cost of capital and whose revenues are indexed --- and return the proceeds as a flat household rebate of around \pounds 183. Its distinctive feature is that its financing is close to a pure rent tax and therefore carries little efficiency cost, which is not true of the alternatives.

The other financing routes on the table are the successor arrangements to the Energy Profits Levy on oil and gas production, and an increase in the Electricity Generator Levy from 45 to 55 per cent. Both are windfall instruments whose yield is mechanically correlated with the shock being compensated, which is an attractive property: the fiscal cost of support and the revenue that pays for it move together, so the package is closer to self-financing in the adverse scenario than in the baseline. Both also carry investment-margin costs that a static microsimulation cannot evaluate and that the source-side literature \citep{goulder2019} suggests are progressive in incidence --- reducing returns to capital falls on capital owners, who are concentrated at the top --- so excluding them from the household results understates the progressivity of the financed package as a whole. I report the household side of each policy and the aggregate cost, and note the direction of the omitted source-side effect rather than attempting to quantify it.

Finally, none of the five instruments addresses the motor-fuel channel. A fuel-duty response would, and it is conspicuously absent from the Budget debate this paper reads. Scoring one is straightforward in this framework and is left to further work only because the duty response actually under consideration was not on the table at the time of writing.
