\section{The Autumn Budget 2026 choice}
\label{sec:policy}

The results in Section~\ref{sec:results} bear directly on a decision that has not yet been taken. The Chancellor has ruled out universal support of the 2022 kind and signalled income-based help --- in practice, a social tariff --- while the Joseph Rowntree Foundation and the TUC have pressed for a universal discounted block costing around \pounds 5 billion. This section sets out what the designs do differently, and it reaches a conclusion the debate does not anticipate: on the metric that matters, the targeted and universal \emph{bill} instruments are indistinguishable, and the only instrument that compensates a majority of losers is the one nobody is arguing for.

\subsection{The constraint that binds before targeting does}
\label{sec:policy-channel}

Every instrument in the live debate --- social tariff, discounted block, Warm Home Discount, VAT zero-rating --- operates on the domestic energy bill. But \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate household loss in the central scenario runs through motor fuel, not through the domestic bill. The entire domestic-bill policy debate is therefore aimed at about a third of the problem, and it is aimed there by construction rather than by design failure. No calibration of a bill-based instrument, however generous or however well targeted, can compensate a loss it does not touch.

This is the deepest reason the scorecard in Table~\ref{tab:scorecard} reads as poorly as it does, and it should be separated from the targeting question that occupies the debate. A social tariff that perfectly identified every household in fuel poverty and fully offset its domestic bill increase would still leave two-thirds of the aggregate loss uncompensated. Targeting design determines who gets reached within the third that bill instruments can address; the channel constraint determines the size of that third.

\subsection{Two designs, one disagreement --- and no difference in the outcome}

A \emph{means-tested social tariff} conditions a discounted unit rate on receipt of a qualifying benefit. Its appeal is arithmetic: every pound of spend goes to a household that has already passed a means test, so on the two conventional targeting metrics it dominates. Table~\ref{tab:scorecard} confirms that it does: \genSocialTariffShareBottomThree{} per cent of its spend reaches deciles one to three, against \genJrfShareBottomThree{} per cent for the block, at a cost of \pounds\genSocialTariffCostBn{} billion.

The \emph{universal discounted block} does something structurally different. It prices the first half of typical consumption at a discounted rate and everything above it at the full rate, with a per-child allowance recognising that household need scales with size. Because the discount applies to a fixed quantity rather than to all consumption, its cash value is roughly flat across the distribution while high-consumption households --- disproportionately affluent, per \citet{fetzer2024} --- pay the full marginal rate on the bulk of their usage. It is therefore neither a universal price subsidy of the Energy Price Guarantee type, which cost around \pounds 23 billion and was regressive in cash terms precisely because it subsidised every unit, nor a transfer. JRF argue, on operational rather than distributional grounds, that a social tariff cannot be built in time for winter 2026: there is no register of eligible households that is both current and complete, and constructing one is a multi-year data-sharing project.

The finding that matters is that the two designs are \emph{indistinguishable} on the outcome metric. Both leave \genSocialTariffUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losing households uncompensated --- \genSocialTariffUncompensatedShare{} per cent for the social tariff and \genJrfUncompensatedShare{} per cent for the block, identical to the precision the model supports. The intuition that the universal design must reach more losers because it is universal does not survive contact with the numbers, and the reason is the channel constraint of Section~\ref{sec:policy-channel}: the block reaches more households, but the amount it delivers to the marginal household is far below that household's total loss, so reaching them does not compensate them. Their genuine difference is in \emph{who} is reached and by how much, not in the headline count: the social tariff concentrates a smaller budget on the bottom three deciles, while the block spreads a larger one across the distribution.

One honest note on generosity. JRF cost their proposal at roughly \pounds 5 billion; the parameterisation scored here --- a 50 per cent discount on the shock component of the first half of median consumption, plus \pounds 60 per child --- costs \pounds\genJrfCostBn{} billion. Our block is therefore materially \emph{less} generous than the one JRF propose, and the results here should not be read as a scoring of JRF's own scheme. A more generous parameterisation would lower the uncompensated share; it would not change the channel constraint that caps how far it could fall.

\subsection{The crux: the households outside the means test}

The anchor statistic of every Budget submission is that approximately 40 per cent of households struggling to heat their homes are not on means-tested benefits (JRF's figure, on their own definition of struggling). Our comparable figure is measured differently and is starker: of households losing more than five per cent of net income to the shock, \genLargeLoserOutsideMeansTest{} per cent sit outside the modelled means-tested system entirely. The two numbers are not the same object and should not be conflated --- JRF measure self-reported difficulty heating the home, we measure a modelled loss relative to income --- but they point the same way.

A means test that is perfectly accurate on the population it covers still leaves the entire uncovered population at zero compensation, and that population is not a random sample of the non-poor: it is concentrated among low-income households above the benefit taper, households with savings above the capital limit, pensioners on modest occupational incomes, the self-employed with volatile earnings, and non-claimants. It also, in this exercise, includes everyone whose loss is a motor-fuel loss, which no bill instrument reaches at any income level.

The Warm Home Discount reform that took effect for the 2026--27 scheme year --- qualifying date 23 August 2026, property-cost test scrapped, the \pounds 150 payment automatic in England and Wales, around 2.7 million additional households brought in --- narrows this gap but does not close it, because eligibility still runs through benefit receipt. In the scorecard the expanded Warm Home Discount is the best-targeted instrument on the between-decile metric, sending \genWhdShareBottomThree{} per cent of a \pounds\genWhdCostBn{} billion outlay to deciles one to three, and simultaneously the \emph{worst} on the outcome metric, leaving \genWhdUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losers uncompensated. That combination is the paper's compensation problem in a single row.

A caveat of the paper's own making applies to both means-tested rows. The modelled means-tested population is \genMeansTestedShareHouseholds{} per cent of households against administrative caseloads implying considerably more (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), so the reach of the social tariff and the Warm Home Discount is understated here and their uncompensated shares are an upper bound. The ranking against the untargeted instruments is robust to this; the exact percentages are not.

\subsection{VAT zero-rating compensates nobody}

Zero-rating VAT on domestic fuel and power is the most frequently proposed instrument in the public debate and the worst performer in the scorecard: it costs \pounds\genVatCutCostBn{} billion, sends only \genVatCutShareBottomThree{} per cent of that to the bottom three deciles, and leaves \genVatCutUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losing households uncompensated. Not a majority --- all of them.

The arithmetic is unforgiving and worth stating plainly, because it is not a modelling artefact. The reduced rate is 5 per cent, so zero-rating returns at most $1 - 1/1.05 = 4.8$ per cent of a VAT-inclusive domestic bill. The domestic price rise in the central scenario is of the order of \genGasUnitRatePct{} per cent on gas and \genElecUnitRatePct{} per cent on electricity. A 4.8 per cent rebate cannot offset a rise several times its size, and the instrument reaches none of the \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the loss carried by motor fuel --- road fuel already bears standard-rate VAT, which zero-rating domestic fuel does not touch. The result is an instrument that transfers real money, transfers it regressively in cash terms because it scales with bill size, and fully compensates no one.

\subsection{Against the ``always target'' consensus}

The grey-literature consensus after 2022 is unambiguous: compensate incomes, not prices, and target. \citet{ari2022} is the standard reference, and the Bruegel fiscal tracker's finding that only around a third of European energy support was targeted is the standard evidence. The reasoning is familiar --- a price subsidy distorts the marginal incentive to conserve, its cost scales with consumption and therefore with affluence, and it blunts the price signal that is supposed to induce adjustment.

The results here speak against that consensus in its strong form. The only instrument in the scorecard that compensates a majority of losing households is the \emph{flat, universal, untargeted} IPPR rebate: at \pounds\genRebateCostBn{} billion it leaves \genRebateUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losers uncompensated, meaning it fully compensates \genRebateFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent of them, while the two best-targeted instruments compensate barely one loser in eight. On the conventional metrics the rebate looks poor --- \genRebateShareBottomThree{} per cent to the bottom three deciles, and the worst cost per pound of bottom-decile gain in the table at \genWorstCostPerPound{} against \genBestCostPerPound{} for the best --- which is precisely the point. The metrics that endorse targeting and the metric that measures whether losers are made whole rank the options in opposite orders.

This is \citet{levell2025}'s result in applied form. Their treatment of the 2022--23 UK episode finds that the constrained-optimal policy includes a \emph{strictly positive} untargeted component, shrinking but never vanishing as the transfer instrument is allowed to condition on more information. The reason is exactly the coverage gap above: an untargeted instrument reaches households that no observable-based transfer can identify, and when the unidentified losing population is large --- here, overwhelmingly large --- the insurance value of that reach outweighs the deadweight loss. \citet{sallee2019} supplies the general statement: when transfers must condition on observables, compensating every loser is not merely expensive but infeasible, and the residual is not noise. \citet{cronin2019} and \citet{douenne2020} establish the same point from the incidence side.

Two qualifications keep this from being read as a blanket endorsement of universality. First, the rebate is expensive relative to what it achieves for the poorest: it is a majority-compensating instrument, not an efficient one, and a Chancellor with a binding envelope faces a genuine trade-off rather than a free lunch. Second, its performance here rests on a flat cash payment against a loss distribution whose cash gradient is comparatively shallow; against a shock with a steeper cash gradient the same instrument would perform worse. The claim defended is narrow and specific: for this shock, on these instruments, targeting buys between-decile efficiency at the cost of leaving nearly seven losers in eight uncompensated, and the untargeted alternative does not.

\subsection{Financing}

The financing side is scored separately, because netting it into the household distribution would conflate two distinct incidence questions.

IPPR's proposal is to claw back windfalls accruing to regulated network companies --- whose allowed returns were set against a very different cost of capital and whose revenues are indexed --- and return the proceeds as a flat household rebate of around \pounds 183. Its distinctive feature is that its financing is close to a pure rent tax and therefore carries little efficiency cost, which is not true of the alternatives.

The other financing routes on the table are the successor arrangements to the Energy Profits Levy on oil and gas production, and an increase in the Electricity Generator Levy from 45 to 55 per cent. Both are windfall instruments whose yield is mechanically correlated with the shock being compensated, which is an attractive property: the fiscal cost of support and the revenue that pays for it move together, so the package is closer to self-financing in the adverse scenario than in the baseline. Both also carry investment-margin costs that a static microsimulation cannot evaluate and that the source-side literature \citep{goulder2019} suggests are progressive in incidence --- reducing returns to capital falls on capital owners, who are concentrated at the top --- so excluding them from the household results understates the progressivity of the financed package as a whole. I report the household side of each policy and the aggregate cost, and note the direction of the omitted source-side effect rather than attempting to quantify it.

Finally, none of the five instruments addresses the motor-fuel channel. A fuel-duty response would, and it is conspicuously absent from the Budget debate this paper reads. Scoring one is straightforward in this framework and is left to further work only because the duty response actually under consideration was not on the table at the time of writing.
