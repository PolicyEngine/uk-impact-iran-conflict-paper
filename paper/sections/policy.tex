\section{The Autumn Budget 2026 choice}
\label{sec:policy}

The Chancellor has ruled out universal support of the 2022 kind and, as trailed in press reporting rather than in any published Treasury document, is said to favour a social tariff, while the Joseph Rowntree Foundation and the TUC press for a universal discounted block costing around \pounds \genJrfStatedCostBn{} billion \citep{jrf2026}. Two conclusions follow that the debate does not anticipate: the choice between a targeted and a universal \emph{bill} instrument is smaller than either side treats it as, because both are confined to the same channel; and the margin on which an envelope is best spent is \emph{eligibility}, not generosity.

\subsection{The constraint that binds before targeting does}
\label{sec:policy-channel}

Every instrument in the live debate --- social tariff, discounted block, Warm Home Discount, VAT zero-rating --- operates on the domestic energy bill, while motor fuel carries \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate loss in the main specification and between \genSpecFuelShareMinPct{} and \genSpecFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent across every calibration in Section~\ref{sec:results-fuel}. Whether that is a majority depends on the calibration; that it is a large part of the loss does not. A social tariff that perfectly identified every household in fuel poverty and fully offset its domestic bill increase would still leave that share untouched. Targeting design determines who is reached within the share bill instruments can address; the channel constraint determines the size of that share, and on no calibration here is it the whole shock.

\subsection{Two designs, one disagreement}

A \emph{means-tested social tariff} conditions a discounted unit rate on receipt of a qualifying benefit, so every pound goes to a household that has passed a means test: \genSocialTariffShareBottomThree{} per cent of its spend reaches deciles one to three, against \genJrfShareBottomThree{} per cent for the block, at \pounds\genSocialTariffCostBn{} billion. The \emph{universal discounted block} is structurally different. It prices the first half of typical consumption at a discounted rate and everything above at the full rate, with a per-child allowance; because the discount applies to a fixed quantity, its cash value is roughly flat across the distribution while high-consumption households --- disproportionately affluent, per \citet{fetzer2024} --- pay the full marginal rate on the bulk of their usage. It is therefore neither a universal price subsidy of the Energy Price Guarantee type, regressive in cash precisely because it subsidised every unit, nor a transfer. JRF argue on operational grounds that a social tariff cannot be built in time for winter 2026: no register of eligible households exists that is both current and complete, and constructing one is a multi-year data-sharing project.

On the uncompensated count the two are far apart in the direction the universal case predicts --- \genSocialTariffUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losing households short under the social tariff against \genJrfUncompensatedShare{} per cent under the block --- but most of that gap is size, the block costing \pounds \genJrfCostBn{} billion against \pounds \genSocialTariffCostBn{} billion. The common envelope answers how much is design, provided it is spent on something each instrument can do. At its \emph{feasible} maximum the social tariff can absorb only \pounds \genSocialTariffAbsorbableBn{} billion of a \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion envelope and offsets \genSocialTariffEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss, with a mean residual of \pounds \genSocialTariffEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{}; the block absorbs the whole envelope and offsets \genJrfEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent, with a mean residual of \pounds \genJrfEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{}. The social tariff has run out of eligible households long before it has run out of money. The block scored here is also the more generous instrument by a parameter this paper chose rather than one JRF published --- \genJrfModelledDiscountPct{} per cent against the \genJrfImpliedDiscountPct{} per cent their own total implies on the same block (Section~\ref{sec:results-policy}) --- but that does not touch the channel constraint capping how far the uncompensated share could fall at any budget.

\subsection{The households outside the means test}

The anchor statistic of every Budget submission is that approximately 40 per cent of households struggling to heat their homes are not on means-tested benefits (JRF's figure, on their own definition). This paper reports no comparable percentage of its own, for the arithmetic reason given in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. The structural point does not need one. A means test perfectly accurate on the population it covers still leaves the entire uncovered population at zero compensation, and that population is not a random sample of the non-poor: it is concentrated among low-income households above the benefit taper, households with savings above the capital limit, pensioners on modest occupational incomes, the self-employed with volatile earnings, and non-claimants. It also includes everyone whose loss is a motor-fuel loss, which no bill instrument reaches at any income level. The Warm Home Discount reform reported for 2026--27 --- property-cost test scrapped, payment automatic, around 2.7 million additional households --- would narrow the gap without closing it, because eligibility still runs through benefit receipt. In the scorecard it is the best-targeted instrument on the between-decile metric, sending \genWhdShareBottomThree{} per cent of a \pounds\genWhdCostBn{} billion outlay to deciles one to three while leaving \genWhdUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losers short: the paper's compensation problem in a single row.

\subsection{Eligibility, not generosity, is the binding margin}
\label{sec:policy-eligibility}

Offer each means-tested instrument the same \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion. Spent on \emph{generosity} neither can absorb it: at a 100 per cent discount on the whole shocked domestic bill the social tariff exhausts at \pounds \genSocialTariffAbsorbableBn{} billion and offsets \genSocialTariffEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss, and at a flat payment of \pounds \genWhdFeasibleMaxParameterGbp{} --- the \emph{mean} shocked domestic bill of the same population --- the Warm Home Discount exhausts at \pounds \genWhdAbsorbableBn{} billion and offsets \genWhdEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent. Those ceilings are the same figure by construction, so this is one saturation result and not two: there is nothing left to give the population these instruments can see. Hold generosity at each sponsor's own parameter and spend on \emph{reach} instead, and the social tariff offsets \genSocialTariffEligibilityOffsetPct{} per cent at \pounds \genSocialTariffEligibilityCostBn{} billion and the Warm Home Discount \genWhdEligibilityOffsetPct{} per cent at \pounds \genWhdEligibilityCostBn{} billion --- the same instrument and the same generosity, and a great deal more loss closed, but \emph{not} the same money, the eligibility arm spending roughly a quarter more than the generosity arm can absorb. Stated per pound of exchequer cost the eligibility arm still wins on both instruments and by a wide margin, and that is the form in which the finding should be quoted.

Three things weaken it and belong with it rather than after it. First, saturation is complete at the sponsor's own parameter, not merely at the feasible maximum: raising the social tariff's discount from 35 to 100 per cent moves the offset from \genSocialTariffStatedOffsetPct{} to \genSocialTariffCappedOffsetPct{} per cent, so there is no interior point at which extra generosity buys anything. Second, the instruments plainly overcompensate at that maximum, paying roughly \pounds \genSocialTariffCappedGainPerEligibleGbp{} to each eligible household against a mean loss among them of \pounds \genMeansTestedMeanLossGbp{}. Third, the size of the advantage rests on how little of the aggregate loss the means-tested population is imputed to carry, and that imputation cannot be validated (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}): a parity specification raises their share from \genMeansTestedShareOfLossPct{} to \genMeansTestedShareOfLossParityPct{} per cent and the advantage falls to \genTargetingMultipleUnderParity{} times, while a participation-corrected specification gives \genMeansTestedShareOfLossParticipationPct{} per cent and \genTargetingMultipleUnderParticipation{} times. Eligibility beats generosity by a factor somewhere between \genTargetingMultipleUnderParity{} and roughly six; the direction is not in doubt on any of the three specifications, the factor is, and the range is what should be quoted.

The widened-eligibility arm admits non-claimants in ascending order of equivalised after-housing-costs income, which presupposes exactly the informational capability this paper argues does not exist. It is therefore a \emph{perfect-observability upper bound} on what an envelope spent on reach could achieve as \emph{targeting}, and the rows keep that label. It is not an upper bound on the offset. \genSocialTariffAdmissionRuleCount{} admission rules are scored at the same envelope (Appendix~\ref{app:envelope}); the default income rule \genSocialTariffAdmissionDefaultIsWorstOffset{} the \emph{lowest} offset of the four, and every rule --- including admission by lottery, at \genSocialTariffAdmissionRandomOffsetPct{} per cent --- beats the \genSocialTariffEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent the generosity arm manages at its ceiling. What observability buys is targeting, and there the assumption does all the work: the share of spend reaching deciles one to three collapses from \genSocialTariffAdmissionAhcBottomThreePct{} per cent under income ranking to \genSocialTariffAdmissionRandomBottomThreePct{} under the lottery.

Two further qualifications travel with the result. The model's own means-tested population is well under half the administrative caseload, so the room the eligibility arm has to expand is overstated here and the gap between the arms would be smaller. And widening eligibility is modelled as a parameter and not costed: identifying households outside the benefit system is exactly the problem that makes a social tariff hard to build, so the result establishes where the return to spending lies, not that the spending is easy. One eligibility row should also be read for what it is rather than for its name: the Warm Home Discount's widened row reaches \genWhdEligibleSharePct{} per cent of households at \pounds 150 each, which is arithmetically a universal flat rebate, and that this is where the margin leads is itself part of the finding. JRF's operational argument --- that no current and complete register exists --- is therefore not a delivery quibble alongside the distributional argument. It is the distributional argument.

\subsection{VAT zero-rating: an arithmetic ceiling}

At its own cost of \pounds\genVatCutCostBn{} billion, zero-rating leaves \genVatCutUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losing households short --- correct and nearly uninformative, since the count treats a household left a pound short identically to one left five hundred short and is computed against a loss that is itself an upper bound. Nor can the instrument occupy a common envelope: the reduced rate is five points, removing them is the whole instrument, and scaling to \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion would require a negative rate of VAT.

What survives is the arithmetic ceiling. Zero-rating returns at most \genVatCutMaxOffsetPct{} per cent of a VAT-inclusive domestic bill against applied rises of \genAppliedGasUnitRatePct{} per cent on gas and \genAppliedElecUnitRatePct{} on electricity --- a factor of two to three rather than an order of magnitude, so the ceiling binds less hard than the usual language implies. At its feasible maximum, which for this instrument is also its stated cost, it offsets \genVatCutEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss with a mean residual of \pounds \genVatCutEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{}: more than three times the \genSocialTariffCappedOffsetPct{} per cent the social tariff manages at its own ceiling, for a little over half the cost. On offset per pound it outperforms both means-tested instruments at their ceilings, by reaching almost every household rather than the right ones --- the same coverage mechanism as the eligibility result. On the conventional metrics it remains the weakest instrument here, at \genVatCutShareBottomThree{} per cent of spend to the bottom three deciles and the highest cost per pound of bottom-decile gain at \genWorstCostPerPound{}, and it reaches no motor fuel, road fuel already bearing standard-rate VAT. One complication is specific to this row: Ofgem's temporary removal of VAT on electricity may already sit in the baseline against which it is scored (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), which would make the figure above an overstatement.

\subsection{Against the ``always target'' consensus}

The results speak against the post-2022 consensus in its strong form, though narrowly. The two instruments compensating the most losing households are the two that do not means-test: the block, fully compensating \genJrfFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent of losers at \pounds\genJrfCostBn{} billion, and the flat IPPR rebate, \genRebateFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent at \pounds\genRebateCostBn{} billion, against \genSocialTariffFullyCompensatedShare{} and \genWhdFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent for the two best-targeted instruments at their own much smaller costs. Part of that is generosity. But at a feasible common envelope the means-tested instruments cannot spend it, and what separates the instruments is how many losing households each can \emph{see}. On the conventional metrics the untargeted instruments look poor --- the rebate at a cost-per-pound ratio of \genRebateCostPerPound{} against \genBestCostPerPound{} for the best-targeted instrument --- which is precisely the point: the metrics that endorse targeting and the metrics that measure whether losers are made whole rank the options differently.

This is \citet{levell2025}'s result in applied form. Their constrained-optimal policy includes a strictly positive untargeted component, shrinking but never vanishing as the transfer instrument conditions on more information, for exactly the coverage reason above: an untargeted instrument reaches households no observable-based transfer can identify, and when the unidentified losing population is large the insurance value of that reach outweighs the deadweight loss. \citet{sallee2019} supplies the general statement, \citet{cronin2019} and \citet{douenne2020} the incidence-side counterpart.

Three qualifications keep this from being a blanket endorsement of universality. The rebate is expensive relative to what it achieves for the poorest --- broadly compensating rather than efficient, so a Chancellor with a binding envelope faces a genuine trade-off. Its performance rests on a flat cash payment against a loss distribution whose cash gradient is comparatively shallow; against a steeper cash gradient it would perform worse. And the saturation mechanism depends on how many households a means test can find, which here is well under half the Universal Credit caseload, so a means test at full coverage would saturate later and the gap between the two arms would be smaller. The claim defended is narrow: for this shock, on these instruments and at these coverage rates, targeting buys between-decile efficiency at the cost of leaving most losers short, and the return to an extra pound lies on the eligibility margin.

\subsection{Financing}

The financing side is scored separately, because netting it into the household distribution would conflate two distinct incidence questions. IPPR propose clawing back windfalls accruing to regulated network companies and returning the proceeds as a flat rebate of around \pounds 183; its distinctive feature is that the financing is close to a pure rent tax and therefore carries little efficiency cost. The other routes on the table --- successor arrangements to the Energy Profits Levy, and an increase in the Electricity Generator Levy from 45 to 55 per cent --- are windfall instruments whose yield is mechanically correlated with the shock being compensated, so the package is closer to self-financing in the adverse scenario than in the baseline. Both carry investment-margin costs a static microsimulation cannot evaluate and which the source-side literature \citep{goulder2019} suggests are progressive in incidence, so excluding them understates the progressivity of the financed package as a whole.

None of the five instruments addresses the motor-fuel channel. A fuel-duty response would, and it is conspicuously absent from the Budget debate this paper reads; scoring one is straightforward in this framework and is left to further work.
