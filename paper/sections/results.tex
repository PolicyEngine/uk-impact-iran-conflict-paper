\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

I report the price path first, then the aggregate and mean loss, then incidence by decile on both metrics, then the within-decile dispersion that the decile averages conceal, then the decomposition of the loss by fuel---which is the section that reframes the policy problem---then incidence by region, and finally the policy scorecard. Unless stated otherwise, figures are for the realised 2026 scenario; the NIESR baseline and adverse scenarios bound it and are reported alongside in Table~\ref{tab:scenario}. All losses are annual, in 2026 prices, and expressed both in pounds and as a share of equivalised disposable income on the HBAI concept. All estimates are gross of any policy response until Section~\ref{sec:results-policy}, static, use-side only, and computed at zero demand elasticity, which makes them an explicit upper bound for a given price path.

\subsection{The price path}
\label{sec:results-prices}

Under the realised scenario the annualised gas unit rate rises \genGasUnitRatePct{} per cent and the electricity unit rate \genElecUnitRatePct{} per cent, the asymmetry reflecting the smaller wholesale share of the electricity cap and the different lag structure of the two fuels. The annualised dual-fuel cap for a typical household reaches \pounds \genCapAnnualised{} against \pounds \genCapBaselineLevel{} in the pre-shock baseline. On the road-fuel side the wholesale move enters far more directly: pump prices track crude with a lag of weeks rather than quarters and with no cap to attenuate them, which is the mechanical reason for the fuel decomposition reported in Section~\ref{sec:results-fuel}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.85\textwidth]{../results/figures/fig1_price_path.png}
\caption{Wholesale gas and oil paths and the implied Ofgem cap and pump-price paths, by scenario. The horizontal displacement between the wholesale series and the cap series is the two-to-three-quarter averaging lag; the vertical compression is the wholesale share of the bill. The pump-price series shows neither, which is why road fuel dominates the loss.}
\label{fig:pricepath}

Figure~\ref{fig:pricepath} makes the two transmission features of the domestic cap visible at once: the lag between the wholesale move and the household bill, and the attenuation from the non-commodity components of the cap. It is also the figure that shows why peak-based bill-rise estimates in the policy literature exceed annualised ones. One consequence of the lag deserves emphasis, and is developed in the appendix: because the cap moves in quarterly steps, the choice of lag reallocates the loss across calendar years without changing its cumulative size, so the cumulative figure is the one to read and the annual figure is an attribution convention.

\subsection{Aggregate and mean loss}
\label{sec:results-aggregate}

Additional household spending on gas, electricity and motor fuel under the realised scenario totals \pounds \genAggSpendRealisedBn{} billion, or \pounds \genCentralLossMean{} for the mean household, equal to \genCentralLossPctIncome{} per cent of equivalised disposable income on the aggregate ratio. The bounding scenarios put the aggregate at \pounds \genAggSpendBaselineBn{} billion under the NIESR baseline and \pounds \genAggSpendAdverseBn{} billion under the adverse \$140 case, a range of better than two to one that is driven entirely by the assumed commodity path rather than by anything in the microdata.

The aggregate admits one external check, and it is the aggregate rather than the mean that admits it. The Resolution Foundation, working independently and from a different method, estimate that a sustained return to the 2026 peaks would see British households spend an extra \pounds 11 billion on energy and road fuel in 2026 relative to early-2026 prices. That is the same object as the figure reported here: extra household outlay on domestic energy and motor fuel, over the same calendar year, for the same population, against the same family of counterfactuals. My main specification of \pounds \genAggSpendRealisedBn{} billion sits a little \emph{below} the Resolution Foundation's figure and the peak-fuel upper bound of \pounds \genPeakFuelAggBn{} billion sits above it, so the specification range brackets the independent estimate. That is the strongest form of external agreement available here, and a more informative one than a point match would be, because it locates the published estimate inside a range whose two ends correspond to a stated and identifiable modelling choice --- whether the observed pump-price peak is damped to an annual average or applied for a full year.

No comparable external check exists for the \emph{mean household} loss of \pounds \genCentralLossMean{}. Published UK figures of similar magnitude measure different objects --- typical dual-fuel bill increases, or whole-income counterfactuals for a median working-age household over a fiscal year --- and agreement with any of them would be coincidental rather than evidential. The mean is reported here as an unvalidated model output.

As a share of income the burden falls steeply and almost monotonically, from \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent in decile one to \genDecileTenLossPct{} per cent in decile ten, a ratio of \genDecileRatioPct{} to one. The one interruption is decile eight, at \genDecileEightLossPct{} per cent, which sits above deciles six, seven and nine; the reason is the same one that governs the cash profile.

That ratio should be read with a specific and material caveat attached, and it is better stated here than deferred. Motor fuel carries the majority of the loss, and the consumption fusion over-imputes motor fuel at the bottom of the income distribution: the average decile-one household is assigned \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileOneRawGbp{} of annual motor-fuel spend against \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileTenRawGbp{} in decile ten, an almost flat profile, where ONS Family Spending gives \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileOneOnsGbp{} against \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileTenOnsGbp{}. The participation margin shows the same problem directly --- the top and bottom deciles record an identical share of households buying any motor fuel, against National Travel Survey evidence that 40 per cent of the poorest quintile has no car and only 14 per cent of the richest. Some of decile one's measured burden is therefore fuel it does not buy. Rescaling decile motor-fuel spend to the ONS profile and re-running the pipeline --- the \emph{ONS-calibrated} specification --- leaves the aggregate essentially unchanged at \pounds \genOnsFuelAggBn{} billion but cuts the decile-one burden from \genDecileOneLossPct{} to \genOnsFuelDecileOneLossPct{} per cent (\pounds \genDecileOneLossGbp{} to \pounds \genOnsFuelDecileOneLossGbp{}), leaves decile ten at \genOnsFuelDecileTenLossPct{} per cent, and nearly halves the ratio, from \genDecileRatioPct{} to \genOnsFuelDecileRatioPct{} to one. Both specifications are reported throughout. The qualitative conclusion is common to them: the burden falls steeply in income while the cash loss does not. The magnitude of that steepness is uncertain by roughly a factor of two, and no claim in this paper rests on the difference between a ratio of \genDecileRatioPct{} and one of \genOnsFuelDecileRatioPct{}.

In pounds the picture is different, and contrary to the standard presentation the cash loss does \emph{not} rise monotonically across deciles. It runs from \pounds \genDecileOneLossGbp{} in decile one to \pounds \genDecileTenLossGbp{} in decile ten, so the endpoints are ordered as \citet{fetzer2024} would predict, but the maximum falls on decile \emph{eight}, at \pounds \genDecileEightLossGbp{}, with decile nine at \pounds \genDecileNineLossGbp{} also above the top decile; and decile one loses more in cash than deciles two and three. The Fetzer--Gazze--Bishop result---that affluent areas are more exposed in pounds---therefore holds in this data as a broad tendency and fails as a monotone ranking. Reporting it as monotone, as the policy literature often does when it quotes only the endpoints, would overstate how cleanly a consumption-proportional subsidy tracks income.

Two distinct objections can be made to the percentage gradient, and they require different answers. The first is about the \emph{denominator}: that the gradient is an artefact of dividing by small incomes at the bottom. That objection does not land, and the evidence against it is specific. The gradient survives on medians rather than aggregate ratios, and in fact widens: the median household in decile one loses \genDecileOneMedianLossPct{} per cent of income against \genDecileTenMedianLossPct{} per cent in decile ten, a ratio of \genMedianDecileRatioPct{} to one against \genDecileRatioPct{} to one on the aggregate ratio. A median is immune to a handful of near-zero denominators by construction. Decile one's median equivalised disposable income is \pounds \genDecileOneMedianIncomeGbp{}, which is a low income but not a degenerate one, and only \genDecileOneZeroIncomeShare{} per cent of decile-one households report zero or negative income, so the population capable of generating an explosive ratio is negligible.

The second objection is about the \emph{numerator}, and the median evidence has nothing to say about it: a median burden computed from an over-imputed motor-fuel spend is over-imputed too. This is the objection that bites, and the relevant test is the ONS-calibrated specification above rather than the median check. That test materially reduces the gradient --- from \genDecileRatioPct{} to \genOnsFuelDecileRatioPct{} to one --- and it should be read as the honest bound on how steep the gradient is. What survives both tests, and what the paper concludes from, is the qualitative pair: the burden falls sharply in income, and the cash loss does not fall with it. What does not survive is any precise reading of the ratio itself.

Two features of the bottom decile are nonetheless real and should not be smoothed over. Its aggregate-ratio burden of \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent is roughly three times its median burden of \genDecileOneMedianLossPct{} per cent, which tells us that the decile-one mean is pulled up by a genuinely extreme upper tail rather than by measurement noise; and that tail is the subject of the next subsection.

It is also the reason the uncompensated-loser count in Section~\ref{sec:results-policy} does work that a decile-average incidence table cannot.

\subsection{What the loss is made of: motor fuel dominates}
\label{sec:results-fuel}

Figure~\ref{fig:fueldecomp} decomposes the aggregate loss by fuel, and it is the single result in this paper most likely to change how the Autumn Budget question is posed.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.85\textwidth]{../results/figures/fig5_fuel_decomposition.png}
\caption{Decomposition of the aggregate household loss into motor fuel, gas and electricity, by scenario and by income decile. Motor fuel carries a majority of the loss in every scenario, and the domestic-bill channels together account for less than half of the total.}
\label{fig:fueldecomp}
\end{figure}

Motor fuel accounts for \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate loss under the realised scenario in the main specification, and \genPeakFuelMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent in the peak-fuel upper bound that applies the observed pump-price peak undamped for a full year; the ONS-calibrated specification gives \genOnsFuelMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent. The honest statement of this result is a range: motor fuel carries somewhere between \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} and \genPeakFuelMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the loss, and a majority of it on every specification considered. In the main specification gas accounts for \genGasShareOfLoss{} per cent and electricity for \genElecShareOfLoss{} per cent, so the domestic bill as a whole carries \genDomesticShareOfLoss{} per cent. The split is stable across macro scenarios as well as across these calibrations: under the NIESR adverse case it is \genMotorFuelShareOfLossAdverse{} per cent, \genGasShareOfLossAdverse{} per cent and \genElecShareOfLossAdverse{} per cent respectively. This is not primarily a domestic energy bill shock. It is more a pump price shock than a bill shock, and that is true at both ends of the calibration range.

Three things follow. The first is mechanical and explains Section~\ref{sec:results-decile}: the non-monotone cash profile is a vehicle-ownership profile, not a dwelling-efficiency profile. Car ownership, vehicle count and annual mileage all rise with income and then flatten or fall at the very top, where households are more urban, better served by public transport and more likely to hold a single low-mileage vehicle. Decile eight is where high mileage and multiple vehicles coincide most strongly, which is why it, and not decile ten, bears the largest cash loss. The efficiency channel that \citet{fetzer2024} identify is present in the gas component but is outweighed at the aggregate.

The second is that the transmission mechanism is quite different from the one the price-cap literature analyses. The domestic channel is lagged, capped and attenuated to the wholesale share of the bill; the road-fuel channel is prompt, uncapped, and passes through close to one for one net of duty. The same wholesale move therefore reaches households mainly through the channel with the least institutional damping, which no analysis conducted in cap-path terms alone will show.

The third is the policy implication, and it is severe. Every instrument in the live debate---the means-tested social tariff, the JRF universal discounted block, the Warm Home Discount expansion, and VAT zero-rating of domestic fuel---operates on the domestic bill. Each is therefore confined \emph{by construction} to \genDomesticShareOfLoss{} per cent of the loss in the main specification, and to less than that under the peak-fuel bound --- at most about half of it, on any calibration --- regardless of how well it is targeted within that share. When Section~\ref{sec:results-policy} finds that all four leave most losers uncompensated, the binding constraint is not targeting precision. It is that they are aimed at the smaller part of the shock. Fuel duty, which is the natural instrument on the larger part, is absent from the debate; I return to this in Section~\ref{sec:policy} while noting that its own distributional and environmental properties are far from straightforward.

\subsection{Incidence by region}
\label{sec:results-region}

The finest geography available on this microdata is the region, of which there are twelve including the three devolved nations. Constituency-level estimates, which would be the more useful cut for a Parliament that votes by seat, are not possible here: the microdata carry no constituency weight matrix and the local-authority field is a degenerate default. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} sets out what would be required to restore that analysis. Region is a coarse cut, and I report it as a description rather than as a causal claim about places.

Unlike the decile comparison, the pound and percentage rankings here are broadly concordant: the regions that lose most in per cent also tend to lose most in pounds, because between-region income differences and between-region car-dependence differences point the same way. London and the South East are simultaneously the highest-income and the least car-dependent regions, and Northern Ireland's low position reflects both income and its distinct energy mix. The regional result is therefore a weaker version of the decile result rather than an independent one, and I do not build on it.

On the conventional targeting metric the ranking is the expected one. The Warm Home Discount expansion sends \genWhdShareBottomThree{} per cent of its spend to deciles one to three and the means-tested social tariff \genSocialTariffShareBottomThree{} per cent, against \genJrfShareBottomThree{} per cent for the JRF universal block, \genRebateShareBottomThree{} per cent for the flat rebate and \genVatCutShareBottomThree{} per cent for VAT zero-rating. Cost per pound of bottom-decile gain runs from \pounds \genBestCostPerPound{} for the Warm Home Discount expansion to \pounds \genWorstCostPerPound{} for the VAT cut. If targeting efficiency were the objective, the argument would end here in favour of means-testing.

It does not end here, for three reasons.

First, the contrast one would expect between the social tariff and the JRF block on within-decile coverage does not survive contact with the data. Both leave \genSocialTariffUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losing households uncompensated---the figures are equal to the reported precision---at costs of \pounds \genSocialTariffCostBn{} billion and \pounds \genJrfCostBn{} billion respectively. The universal block's advantage is in \emph{who} it reaches, not in how many it leaves short: it reaches losing households outside the means-tested system that the social tariff cannot see, while failing to reach them with enough money to close their loss. Figure~\ref{fig:uncompensated} shows the difference in composition that the headline count conceals, and Figure~\ref{fig:benefitstatus} shows it by benefit status directly. It is also worth noting that my parameterisation of the JRF block costs \pounds \genJrfCostBn{} billion against JRF's own costing of around \pounds 5 billion, so what I score is a less generous scheme than the one they propose; the coverage finding is about the design, but the compensation adequacy finding is conditional on my calibration and would improve under theirs.

Second, VAT zero-rating compensates nobody fully: \genVatCutUncompensatedShare{} per cent of losers remain uncompensated. This is arithmetic rather than a modelling artefact. Removing a five per cent reduced rate is at most a \genVatCutMaxOffsetPct{} per cent reduction in the domestic bill, which cannot offset a domestic price rise of the order of \genGasUnitRatePct{} per cent, and it reaches none of the motor-fuel channel that carries \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent or more of the loss. It is the worst option on every metric here and its appeal is administrative speed.

Third, the only option that compensates a majority of losers is the one with no targeting at all. The IPPR flat rebate leaves \genRebateUncompensatedShare{} per cent uncompensated, meaning \genRebateFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent are fully compensated, at a cost of \pounds \genRebateCostBn{} billion---roughly four times the social tariff. Part of that is simply generosity, and a social tariff funded to \pounds \genRebateCostBn{} billion would perform better than the one scored here. But part is structural: a flat cash payment is the only instrument in the set that is not tied to the domestic bill, so it is the only one not confined to the \genDomesticShareOfLoss{} per cent of the loss that the bill represents. Universality is doing less work here than channel-independence is. That reading is consistent with \citet{levell2025}, whose optimum includes a strictly positive price subsidy precisely because observable-based transfers cannot reach every loser, and with \citet{sallee2019} on the infeasibility of compensating all losers from observables.

One caveat applies to the two means-tested rows specifically, and it is larger than a caveat usually is. PolicyEngine's modelled means-tested population is \genMeansTestedSharePct{} per cent of households --- \genMeansTestedHouseholdsM{} million --- against a Universal Credit caseload alone of 7.2 million households in May 2026. The model captures roughly two in five Universal Credit households, so the social tariff's and the Warm Home Discount expansion's reach is materially understated and their uncompensated shares correspondingly overstated; those two figures are upper bounds on the uncompensated count, not estimates of it. This does not affect the VAT or flat-rebate rows, which do not condition on benefit receipt, and it does not overturn the ranking, since closing the entire gap between modelled and administrative caseloads would not bring either instrument near the flat rebate's coverage.
