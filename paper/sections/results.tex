\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

I report the price path first, then the aggregate and mean loss, then incidence by decile on both metrics, then the within-decile dispersion that the decile averages conceal, then the decomposition of the loss by fuel---which is the section that reframes the policy problem---then incidence by region, and finally the policy scorecard. Unless stated otherwise, figures are for the realised 2026 scenario; the NIESR baseline and adverse scenarios bound it and are reported alongside in Table~\ref{tab:scenario}. All losses are annual, in 2026 prices, and expressed both in pounds and as a share of equivalised household net income after housing costs on the HBAI scale (Section~\ref{sec:step2}); households with non-positive equivalised income are dropped from percentage statistics and the dropped weight is reported. All estimates are gross of any policy response until Section~\ref{sec:results-policy}, static, use-side only, and computed at zero demand elasticity, which makes them an explicit upper bound for a given price path.

\subsection{The price path}
\label{sec:results-prices}

Under the realised scenario the annualised gas bill rises \genGasUnitRatePct{} per cent and the electricity bill \genElecUnitRatePct{} per cent, the asymmetry reflecting the smaller wholesale share of the electricity cap and the different lag structure of the two fuels. Both are bill figures rather than unit rates: because standing charges do not move with wholesale costs, the corresponding unit-rate increases are larger. The dual-fuel cap for a typical household settles at \pounds \genCapAnnualised{} against \pounds \genCapBaselineLevel{} in the pre-shock baseline; that is the steady-state level the cap reaches, not the level households pay on average over 2026, which is lower because the cap arrives in quarterly steps part-way through the year. On the road-fuel side the wholesale move enters far more directly: pump prices track crude with a lag of weeks rather than quarters and with no cap to attenuate them, which is the mechanical reason for the fuel decomposition reported in Section~\ref{sec:results-fuel}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.85\textwidth]{../results/figures/fig1_price_path.png}
\caption{Wholesale gas and oil paths and the implied Ofgem cap and pump-price paths, by scenario. The horizontal displacement between the wholesale series and the cap series is the two-to-three-quarter averaging lag; the vertical compression is the wholesale share of the bill. The pump-price series shows neither, which is why road fuel dominates the loss.}
\label{fig:pricepath}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:pricepath} makes the two transmission features of the domestic cap visible at once: the lag between the wholesale move and the household bill, and the attenuation from the non-commodity components of the cap. It is also the figure that shows why peak-based bill-rise estimates in the policy literature exceed annualised ones. One consequence of the lag deserves emphasis, and is developed in the appendix: because the cap moves in quarterly steps, the choice of lag reallocates the loss across calendar years without changing its cumulative size, so the cumulative figure is the one to read and the annual figure is an attribution convention.

\subsection{Aggregate and mean loss}
\label{sec:results-aggregate}

Additional household spending on gas, electricity and motor fuel under the realised scenario totals \pounds \genAggSpendRealisedBn{} billion, or \pounds \genCentralLossMean{} for the mean household, equal to \genCentralLossPctIncome{} per cent of equivalised disposable income on the aggregate ratio. Two accounting choices move that figure more than any parameter does. Charging the steady-state cap level for the whole year instead of the consumption-weighted average of the quarterly levels raises it to \pounds \genSteadyStateAggBn{} billion (\pounds \genSteadyStateLossMean{} per household); damping the gas and pump peaks by a common fraction rather than by fuel-specific ones raises it to \pounds \genSymDampAggBn{} billion (\pounds \genSymDampLossMean{}); and applying the observed pump peak undamped for twelve months raises it to \pounds \genPeakFuelAggBn{} billion (\pounds \genPeakFuelLossMean{}). Table~\ref{tab:specifications} in the appendix sets out all seven specifications on a common set of statistics, and every headline in this section is stated with its specification attached. The bounding scenarios put the aggregate at \pounds \genAggSpendBaselineBn{} billion under the NIESR baseline and \pounds \genAggSpendAdverseBn{} billion under the adverse \$140 case, a range of better than two to one that is driven entirely by the assumed commodity path rather than by anything in the microdata.

The aggregate admits one external check, and it is the aggregate rather than the mean that admits it. The Resolution Foundation, working independently and from a different method, estimate that a sustained return to the 2026 peaks would see British households spend an extra \pounds 11 billion on energy and road fuel in 2026 relative to early-2026 prices. That is the same object as the figure reported here: extra household outlay on domestic energy and motor fuel, over the same calendar year, for the same population, against the same family of counterfactuals. My main specification of \pounds \genAggSpendRealisedBn{} billion sits a little \emph{below} the Resolution Foundation's figure and the peak-fuel upper bound of \pounds \genPeakFuelAggBn{} billion sits above it, so the specification range brackets the independent estimate. That is the strongest form of external agreement available here, and a more informative one than a point match would be, because it locates the published estimate inside a range whose two ends correspond to a stated and identifiable modelling choice --- whether the observed pump-price peak is damped to an annual average or applied for a full year.

No comparable external check exists for the \emph{mean household} loss of \pounds \genCentralLossMean{}. Published UK figures of similar magnitude measure different objects --- typical dual-fuel bill increases, or whole-income counterfactuals for a median working-age household over a fiscal year --- and agreement with any of them would be coincidental rather than evidential. The mean is reported here as an unvalidated model output.

As a share of income the burden falls steeply and almost monotonically, from \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent in decile one to \genDecileTenLossPct{} per cent in decile ten, a ratio of \genDecileRatioPct{} to one. The one interruption is decile eight, at \genDecileEightLossPct{} per cent, which sits above deciles six, seven and nine; the reason is the same one that governs the cash profile.

In pounds the picture is different, and contrary to the standard presentation the cash loss does \emph{not} rise monotonically across deciles. It runs from \pounds \genDecileOneLossGbp{} in decile one to \pounds \genDecileTenLossGbp{} in decile ten, so the endpoints are ordered as \citet{fetzer2024} would predict, but the maximum falls on decile \emph{eight}, at \pounds \genDecileEightLossGbp{}, with decile nine at \pounds \genDecileNineLossGbp{} also above the top decile; and decile one loses more in cash than deciles two and three. The Fetzer--Gazze--Bishop result---that affluent areas are more exposed in pounds---therefore holds in this data as a broad tendency and fails as a monotone ranking. Reporting it as monotone, as the policy literature often does when it quotes only the endpoints, would overstate how cleanly a consumption-proportional subsidy tracks income.

\subsubsection*{How robust is the gradient?}

The direction of the percentage gradient is robust. It is negative in income in every specification this paper runs --- main, steady-state, symmetric damping, peak-fuel, both ONS calibrations, and the unequivalised robustness line --- and in every cell of the scenario grid in Appendix~\ref{app:grid}. Its \emph{magnitude} is not robust, and the honest range is roughly \genOnsFuelDecileRatioPct{} to \genDecileRatioPct{} to one. Three things move it, and they should be separated because they are different kinds of problem.

\paragraph{The denominator, and a rebuttal this paper withdraws.} Earlier versions of this paper met the objection that the gradient is manufactured by small denominators at the bottom with a specific piece of evidence: that only a fraction of one per cent of decile one had zero or negative income. That figure was computed on unequivalised net income, which is not the concept the paper uses or claims. On the equivalised after-housing-costs concept the correct figure is \genDecileOneZeroIncomeShare{} per cent of decile one --- two orders of magnitude larger, and large enough that the rebuttal does not stand. Nor is that the whole of it: every one of the \genCoverageExcludedHouseholdsM{} million households (\genCoverageExcludedSharePct{} per cent of the total) that fall outside deciles one to ten has non-positive equivalised income, and they were being silently dropped from the decile tables while remaining in the denominator of the aggregate normalisation. Percentage statistics in this version drop non-positive incomes explicitly and report the dropped weight, and shares of the aggregate loss are normalised on the deciles displayed.

What replaces the rebuttal is not a counter-argument but a statement of what is and is not known. The gradient survives on medians, where decile one loses \genDecileOneMedianLossPct{} per cent against decile ten's \genDecileTenMedianLossPct{} per cent --- a median is immune to a handful of near-zero denominators by construction --- and decile one's median equivalised income of \pounds \genDecileOneMedianIncomeGbp{} is low but not degenerate. That is evidence that the gradient is not \emph{only} a small-denominator artefact. It is not evidence that the tail is unproblematic. A fifth of the bottom decile having non-positive measured income on the concept the analysis divides by is a genuine measurement problem in the FRS income concept, it is not one this paper solves, and the burden of decile one should be read with that attached.

\paragraph{The denominator concept itself.} The switch from unequivalised to equivalised income changes the gradient substantially, and the change is reported rather than absorbed. On the unequivalised basis the same shock gives a mean burden of \genUnequivLossPctIncome{} per cent, a decile-one burden of \genUnequivDecileOneLossPct{} per cent against \genUnequivDecileTenLossPct{} per cent in decile ten, and a ratio of \genUnequivDecileRatioPct{} to one, against \genDecileRatioPct{} on the equivalised basis. Equivalisation steepens the gradient because it scales small households' incomes up and large households' down, and low-income households are disproportionately small. The equivalised figure is the one the paper reports, because it is the concept the published distributional literature uses and the one that makes the comparison in Section~\ref{sec:literature} meaningful; the unequivalised line is here so that the effect of the correction is visible.

\paragraph{The numerator.} Motor fuel carries a large share of the loss and the consumption fusion over-imputes motor fuel at the bottom of the income distribution: the average decile-one household is assigned \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileOneRawGbp{} of annual motor-fuel spend against \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileTenRawGbp{} in decile ten, an almost flat profile, where ONS Family Spending gives \pounds \genOnsBenchFuelDecileOneGbp{} against \pounds \genOnsBenchFuelDecileTenGbp{}. The participation margin shows the same problem directly --- the top and bottom deciles record an identical share of households buying any motor fuel, against National Travel Survey evidence that 40 per cent of the poorest quintile has no car and only 14 per cent of the richest. Some of decile one's measured burden is therefore fuel it does not buy. Rescaling decile motor-fuel spend to the ONS \emph{shape} --- the ONS-calibrated specification --- leaves the aggregate essentially unchanged at \pounds \genOnsFuelAggBn{} billion but cuts the decile-one burden from \genDecileOneLossPct{} to \genOnsFuelDecileOneLossPct{} per cent (\pounds \genDecileOneLossGbp{} to \pounds \genOnsFuelDecileOneLossGbp{}) and takes the ratio to \genOnsFuelDecileRatioPct{} to one. Decile ten does not stay put under this correction, and an earlier draft said it did: its burden rises from \genDecileTenLossPct{} to \genOnsFuelDecileTenLossPct{} per cent and its cash loss from \pounds \genDecileTenLossGbp{} to \pounds \genOnsFuelDecileTenLossGbp{}, because the rescaling moves motor fuel up the distribution as well as down it. Correcting both spending \emph{levels} to ONS as well as the shape --- which also corrects the domestic under-imputation documented in Section~\ref{sec:limitations} --- gives \pounds \genOnsLevelsAggBn{} billion, a decile-one burden of \genOnsLevelsDecileOneLossPct{} per cent against \genOnsLevelsDecileTenLossPct{} per cent, and a ratio of \genOnsLevelsDecileRatioPct{} to one.

Taken together: the burden falls sharply in income, and the cash loss does not. That pair is common to every specification and is what the paper concludes from. No claim in this paper rests on the difference between a ratio of \genDecileRatioPct{} and one of \genOnsFuelDecileRatioPct{}, and none should be read as resting on it.

Two features of the bottom decile are nonetheless real and should not be smoothed over. Its aggregate-ratio burden of \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent is well above its median burden of \genDecileOneMedianLossPct{} per cent, which tells us that the decile-one mean is pulled up by a genuinely extreme upper tail rather than by measurement noise alone; and that tail is the subject of the next subsection.

\subsection{What the loss is made of: a large motor-fuel share, and how much it moves}
\label{sec:results-fuel}

Figure~\ref{fig:fueldecomp} decomposes the aggregate loss by fuel, and it is the single result in this paper most likely to change how the Autumn Budget question is posed.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.85\textwidth]{../results/figures/fig5_fuel_decomposition.png}
\caption{Decomposition of the aggregate household loss into motor fuel, gas and electricity, by scenario and by income decile. Motor fuel carries the largest single share in the main specification; the share is calibration-dependent and its range across specifications is reported in the text and in Table~\ref{tab:specifications}.}
\label{fig:fueldecomp}
\end{figure}

Motor fuel accounts for \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate loss under the realised scenario in the main specification, against \genGasShareOfLoss{} per cent for gas and \genElecShareOfLoss{} per cent for electricity, so the domestic bill as a whole carries \genDomesticShareOfLoss{} per cent. That is the largest single channel and a majority of the loss. It is also the number in this paper that moves most with calibration, and the previous version of this section defended a majority share that the specifications do not uniformly support. The result is therefore restated at the strength the evidence carries.

\paragraph{The robust claim.} Motor fuel carries a large share of the aggregate loss --- between \genSpecFuelShareMinPct{} and \genSpecFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent across the seven specifications reported here, and as little as \genSymDampOnsLevelsMotorFuelShare{} per cent when two of the adverse calibration choices are combined --- and a majority of it under most of them, including the one I regard as central. A substantial part of the shock runs through a channel that no domestic-bill instrument touches, on every calibration, and the size of that part is never small.

\paragraph{What overturns the majority, and what does not.} Taken one at a time, none of the specification choices overturns it, though two bring it to the edge. The steady-state accounting basis gives \genSteadyStateMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent; the ONS decile-shape correction \genOnsFuelMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent; the peak-fuel upper bound \genPeakFuelMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent; damping the gas and pump peaks by a \emph{common} fraction rather than by the fuel-specific pair \genSymDampMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent; and correcting both spending levels to ONS \genOnsLevelsMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent. The last two sit on the boundary, and combining them with each other or with the steady-state basis takes the share clearly into minority territory: symmetric damping on the steady-state basis gives \genSymDampSteadyMotorFuelShare{} per cent and symmetric damping with ONS-consistent spending levels \genSymDampOnsLevelsMotorFuelShare{} per cent. Nor is the share stable across the macro scenarios: it is \genMotorFuelShareOfLossAdverse{} per cent under the NIESR adverse case against \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent in the realised central one, so an earlier draft's claim that the split is ``stable across macro scenarios'' does not hold. And the single unswept parameter that moves it most is the pre-war gas price, which takes the share from \genDomesticLegNbpFuelShareMinPct{} to \genDomesticLegNbpFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent on its own. An earlier draft described the share as stable across macro scenarios and as a majority on any calibration. Neither statement is correct and both are withdrawn.

\paragraph{Why the ratio of the damping fractions is what matters.} The motor-fuel share depends almost entirely on the ratio of the gas and pump sustained fractions, at \genGasSustainedFraction{} and \genPumpSustainedFraction{} per cent respectively in the main specification, because that ratio is what sets the relative size of the two legs of the shock. The argument for the fuel-specific pair is given in Section~\ref{sec:step1} and I continue to think it right: the cap is a mechanical function of a lagged forward curve averaged over a fixed window and smooths a spike heavily, whereas pump prices track spot crude within weeks and retain much more of a peak inside the year. But it is a calibration argument, not an identification result, and a reader who prefers the symmetric assumption gets a minority motor-fuel share. That is the honest position, and it is why the range rather than the point is what this paper asks to be quoted on.

Three things follow, and the first two are unaffected by where in the range the share sits.

The first is mechanical and explains Section~\ref{sec:results-decile}: the non-monotone cash profile is a vehicle-ownership profile, not a dwelling-efficiency profile. Car ownership, vehicle count and annual mileage all rise with income and then flatten or fall at the very top. Decile eight's cash spike has previously been narrated purely as mileage, and the data do not settle that: the diesel uplift in this scenario is \genDieselUpliftPct{} per cent against petrol's \genPetrolUpliftPct{} per cent, so any decile with an above-average diesel share of motor-fuel spend takes a larger proportional hit for the same mileage. Petrol spend, diesel spend and the diesel share are reported by decile so that the reader can see which mechanism is doing the work rather than take the narration on trust. The efficiency channel that \citet{fetzer2024} identify is present in the gas component but is outweighed at the aggregate.

The second is that the transmission mechanism is quite different from the one the price-cap literature analyses. The domestic channel is lagged, capped and attenuated to the wholesale share of the bill; the road-fuel channel is prompt and uncapped. The same wholesale move therefore reaches households substantially through the channel with the least institutional damping, which no analysis conducted in cap-path terms alone will show.

The third is the policy implication, and it survives the whole calibration range even though its magnitude does not. Every instrument in the live debate---the means-tested social tariff, the JRF universal discounted block, the Warm Home Discount expansion, and VAT zero-rating---operates on the domestic bill. Each is therefore confined \emph{by construction} to the domestic share of the loss: \genDomesticShareOfLoss{} per cent in the main specification, and between \genPeakFuelDomesticShareOfLoss{} and \genOnsLevelsDomesticShareOfLoss{} per cent across the seven specifications run here. Under no calibration in this paper does a bill instrument reach the whole shock, and under most it cannot reach even half. When Section~\ref{sec:results-policy} finds that all four leave many losers uncompensated, the binding constraint is not targeting precision; it is that they are aimed at part of the shock. Fuel duty, which is the natural instrument on the other part, is absent from the debate; I return to this in Section~\ref{sec:policy} while noting that its own distributional and environmental properties are far from straightforward.

London and the South East are simultaneously the highest-income and the least car-dependent regions, and Northern Ireland's low position reflects both income and its distinct energy mix. The regional result is therefore a weaker version of the decile result rather than an independent one, and I do not build on it.

On the conventional targeting metric the ranking is the expected one. The Warm Home Discount expansion sends \genWhdShareBottomThree{} per cent of its spend to deciles one to three and the means-tested social tariff \genSocialTariffShareBottomThree{} per cent, against \genJrfShareBottomThree{} per cent for the JRF universal block, \genRebateShareBottomThree{} per cent for the flat rebate and \genVatCutShareBottomThree{} per cent for VAT zero-rating. Cost per pound of bottom-decile gain is lowest for the Warm Home Discount expansion at \pounds \genBestCostPerPound{}; the flat rebate costs \pounds \genRebateCostPerPound{} and the VAT cut \pounds \genWorstCostPerPound{}, which is the highest in the table. (An earlier version of this paper attached the VAT cut's figure to the rebate, in a sentence carrying the paper's targeting point; the correction matters, because the rebate is not the least targeting-efficient instrument in the set.) If targeting efficiency were the objective, the argument would end here in favour of means-testing.

It does not end here, and revising the scoring has changed two of the conclusions this section previously drew.

\paragraph{The JRF block, correctly specified, is more generous than JRF's own proposal.} The block scored in earlier versions discounted only the shock component of the first half of typical consumption. JRF propose a discounted rate on the \emph{level} of the block, which is a substantially larger instrument. Scored as proposed, it costs \pounds \genJrfCostBn{} billion against JRF's published costing of around \pounds 5 billion --- so what is scored here is \emph{more} generous than their scheme, and the earlier statement that this paper scored a less generous version, with the implication that JRF's own design would perform better, is withdrawn. The comparison against JRF's costing now runs the other way, and any residual gap between their figure and mine is a difference in parameterisation of a bigger instrument, not evidence that mine is under-funded.

\paragraph{At a common envelope, VAT zero-rating performs best, not worst.} The uncompensated-loser count is a knife-edge measure --- a household left one pound short counts exactly as one left five hundred pounds short --- and it was previously being applied across instruments costing \pounds \genVatCutCostBn{} billion and \pounds \genRebateCostBn{} billion as though the comparison were like for like. It was not. Scaled to a common \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion envelope and scored on continuous measures, VAT zero-rating offsets \genVatCutEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss and leaves a mean residual loss of \pounds \genVatCutEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{} --- the largest share offset and the smallest residual of the five. The social tariff at the same envelope offsets \genSocialTariffEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent with a mean residual of \pounds \genSocialTariffEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{}, the JRF block \genJrfEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent and \pounds \genJrfEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{}, the Warm Home Discount expansion \genWhdEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent and \pounds \genWhdEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{}, and the flat rebate \genRebateEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent and \pounds \genRebateEnvelopeResidualMeanGbp{}.

The mechanism is saturation, and it is worth stating because it generalises beyond this scorecard. A means-tested instrument concentrates spending on a population that a fixed envelope soon over-compensates; past that point the marginal pound delivers no reduction in aggregate residual loss, because it lands on households whose loss is already covered. A broad instrument keeps finding uncovered losers. The conventional targeting metrics cannot see this, because they measure where money goes rather than what it closes. The earlier claim that VAT zero-rating ``compensates nobody'' was true of the uncompensated count at the instrument's own small cost and false of everything else about it, and it is withdrawn.

\paragraph{What has not changed, and what the count now says.} The uncompensated count still tells one true thing the continuous measures do not: how many losing households are left short at all. At each instrument's own stated cost the two means-tested instruments leave the great majority short --- \genSocialTariffUncompensatedShare{} per cent for the social tariff and \genWhdUncompensatedShare{} per cent for the Warm Home Discount expansion --- while the two broad instruments do not: the correctly-specified JRF block leaves \genJrfUncompensatedShare{} per cent uncompensated and fully compensates \genJrfFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent, and the flat rebate leaves \genRebateUncompensatedShare{} per cent and fully compensates \genRebateFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent. The earlier finding that the targeted and universal bill instruments were indistinguishable on this count was an artefact of the JRF mis-specification and is withdrawn: correctly specified, the block outperforms the social tariff on the count by a wide margin, and also outperforms the untargeted flat rebate. But the spread is substantially a statement about relative generosity --- these are instruments costing \pounds \genSocialTariffCostBn{}, \pounds \genWhdCostBn{}, \pounds \genJrfCostBn{} and \pounds \genRebateCostBn{} billion --- and about which channel each acts on, and only partly about targeting design, which is why the common-envelope comparison above is the one to read. The channel constraint of Section~\ref{sec:results-fuel} is what no envelope fixes. A bill instrument scaled to any budget still reaches none of the motor-fuel loss, and that is why the flat rebate --- the only instrument in the set not tied to the domestic bill --- is the only one whose reach is not capped by the domestic share of the loss.

One caveat applies to the two means-tested rows specifically, and it is larger than a caveat usually is. PolicyEngine's modelled means-tested population is \genMeansTestedShareHouseholds{} per cent of households --- \genMeansTestedHouseholdsM{} million --- against a Universal Credit caseload alone of 7.2 million households in May 2026. The model captures roughly two in five Universal Credit households, so the social tariff's and the Warm Home Discount expansion's reach is materially understated and their uncompensated shares correspondingly overstated; those two figures are upper bounds on the uncompensated count, not estimates of it. This does not affect the VAT or flat-rebate rows, which do not condition on benefit receipt. It does bear on the common-envelope comparison, and in a known direction: a means-tested instrument that reached its full administrative caseload would spread the same envelope over a larger population, which reduces the saturation that drives the result above. How far it would go is not something this model can answer, so the common-envelope ranking should be read as conditional on the modelled caseload, with the direction of the bias stated rather than assumed away.
