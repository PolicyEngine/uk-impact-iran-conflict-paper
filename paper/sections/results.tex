I report the price path first, then the aggregate and mean loss, then incidence by decile on both metrics, then the within-decile dispersion that the decile averages conceal, then the decomposition of the loss by fuel---which is the section that reframes the policy problem---then incidence by region, and finally the policy scorecard. Unless stated otherwise, figures are for the realised 2026 scenario; the NIESR baseline and adverse scenarios bound it and are reported alongside in Table~\ref{tab:scenario}. All losses are annual, in 2026 prices, and expressed both in pounds and as a share of equivalised household net income after housing costs on the HBAI scale (Section~\ref{sec:step2}); households with non-positive equivalised income are dropped from percentage statistics and the dropped weight is reported. All estimates are gross of any policy response until Section~\ref{sec:results-policy}, static, use-side only, and computed at zero demand elasticity, which makes them an explicit upper bound for a given price path.

\subsection{The price path}
\label{sec:results-prices}

Two price factors have to be kept apart, and this paper reports the second. The \emph{steady-state} factor is the level the cap eventually reaches: under the realised scenario the gas bill settles \genGasUnitRatePct{} per cent and the electricity bill \genElecUnitRatePct{} per cent above baseline, the asymmetry reflecting the smaller wholesale share of the electricity cap and the different lag structure of the two fuels. The \emph{annual applied} factor is what households pay on average across the modelled window of March 2026 to February 2027, and it is smaller, because the cap arrives in quarterly steps part-way through the window: the phase-in weights actually applied are \genAnnualPhaseInGasPct{} per cent of the steady-state gas move and \genAnnualPhaseInElecPct{} per cent of the electricity move. Every incidence figure in this section is computed on the annual applied factor. Both are bill figures rather than unit rates: because standing charges do not move with wholesale costs, the corresponding unit-rate increases are larger. The war-consistent October 2026 dual-fuel cap for a typical household is \pounds \genCapAnnualised{} --- Ofgem's confirmed \pounds 1{,}723 \citep{ofgem2026cap} plus the roughly \pounds 45 of electricity-VAT relief that is unrelated to the war, on the 2026 typical-consumption basis \citep{ofgem2026tdcv} --- against a \emph{solved} pre-war counterfactual of \pounds \genCapBaselineLevel{}. That baseline is not a published cap and could not be: the observation window behind the July 2026 cap already contained the war, which is why the pre-war level is solved from both published caps rather than read off either (Section~\ref{sec:step1}). On the solved path the October cap moves \genCapAnchorMovePct{} per cent, with July already carrying \genCapBaseMovePct{} per cent. On the road-fuel side the wholesale move enters far more directly: pump prices track crude with a lag of weeks rather than quarters and with no cap to attenuate them, which is the mechanical reason for the fuel decomposition reported in Section~\ref{sec:results-fuel}.

Figure~\ref{fig:pricepath} makes the two transmission features of the domestic cap visible at once: the lag between the wholesale move and the household bill, and the attenuation from the non-commodity components of the cap. It is also the figure that shows why peak-based bill-rise estimates in the policy literature exceed annualised ones. One consequence of the lag deserves emphasis, and is developed in the appendix: because the cap moves in quarterly steps, the choice of lag shifts how much of the domestic pass-through falls inside the modelled window. With both legs annualised over the twelve months from the shock and the phase-in profile built from each cap period's own observation window, that sensitivity is small --- \pounds \genCapLagRangeGbp{} per household across lags of one to four quarters. The large sensitivity in the domestic leg is elsewhere, in the assumed monthly shape of the gas peak that the solve consumes (Appendix~\ref{app:domesticleg}).

\subsection{Aggregate and mean loss}
\label{sec:results-aggregate}

Over the modelled window --- March 2026 to February 2027, the twelve months from the shock --- additional household spending on gas, electricity and motor fuel under the realised scenario totals \pounds \genAggSpendRealisedBn{} billion, or \pounds \genCentralLossMean{} for the mean household, equal to \genCentralLossPctIncome{} per cent of equivalised disposable income on the aggregate ratio. The mean loss splits \pounds \genDomesticMeanLossGbp{} domestic against \pounds \genMotorFuelMeanLossGbp{} motor fuel. The window is stated with the figure throughout, because it is not calendar 2026. Two accounting choices move that figure more than any parameter does. Charging the steady-state cap level for the whole year instead of the consumption-weighted average of the quarterly levels raises it to \pounds \genSteadyStateAggBn{} billion (\pounds \genSteadyStateLossMean{} per household); damping the gas and pump peaks by a common fraction rather than by fuel-specific ones raises it to \pounds \genSymDampAggBn{} billion (\pounds \genSymDampLossMean{}); and applying the observed pump peak undamped for twelve months raises it to \pounds \genPeakFuelAggBn{} billion (\pounds \genPeakFuelLossMean{}). Table~\ref{tab:specifications} in the appendix sets out all seven specifications on a common set of statistics, and every headline in this section is stated with its specification attached. The bounding scenarios put the aggregate at \pounds \genAggSpendBaselineBn{} billion under the NIESR baseline and \pounds \genAggSpendAdverseBn{} billion under the adverse \$140 case, a range of better than two to one that is driven entirely by the assumed commodity path rather than by anything in the microdata.

The aggregate admits one external check, and it is the aggregate rather than the mean that admits it. The Resolution Foundation, working independently and from a different method, estimate that a sustained return to the 2026 peaks would see British households spend an extra \pounds 11 billion on energy and road fuel in \emph{calendar} 2026 relative to early-2026 prices \citep{rf2026outlook}. That is the same object as the figure reported here --- extra household outlay on domestic energy and motor fuel, for the same population, against the same family of counterfactuals --- but not over the same twelve months, and the difference is not cosmetic, since calendar 2026 opens two months before the shock and closes before the cap has fully moved. The comparison is therefore run on the Resolution Foundation's own window, with \emph{both} legs re-annualised on calendar 2026 and the peak-fuel variant re-run on that window too. On that single window the damped specification gives \pounds \genCalendarAggBn{} billion and the peak-fuel upper bound \pounds \genPeakFuelCalendarAggBn{} billion, so the two bracket the Resolution Foundation's \pounds 11 billion. My own headline of \pounds \genAggSpendRealisedBn{} billion is stated separately, on the March 2026 to February 2027 window, and is not part of the bracket.

That the bracket is now built on one window is what makes it a comparison. A range whose lower end came from one window and whose upper end came from another would contain the published estimate for reasons having nothing to do with agreement, and no weight could be placed on it. As constructed, the two ends differ in exactly one stated modelling choice --- whether the observed pump-price peak is damped to an annual average or applied for the full year --- which is the appropriate form of external validation for a pipeline whose components have not previously been joined.

No comparable external check exists for the \emph{mean household} loss of \pounds \genCentralLossMean{}. Published UK figures of similar magnitude measure different objects --- typical dual-fuel bill increases, or whole-income counterfactuals for a median working-age household over a fiscal year --- and agreement with any of them would be coincidental rather than evidential. The mean is reported here as an unvalidated model output.

As a share of income the burden falls steeply and almost monotonically, from \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent in decile one to \genDecileTenLossPct{} per cent in decile ten, a ratio of \genDecileRatioPct{} to one. Both ends of that ratio should be read with the ranking caveat of Section~\ref{sec:limitations} attached: households are sorted on equivalised \emph{before}-housing-costs income and their burdens measured on equivalised \emph{after}-housing-costs income, which are different concepts and agree only \genDecileRankAgreementEquivAhcPrecisePct{} per cent of the time. The one interruption is decile eight, at \genDecileEightLossPct{} per cent, which sits above deciles six, seven and nine; the reason is the same one that governs the cash profile.

In pounds the picture is different. In the main specification the cash loss does \emph{not} rise monotonically across deciles: it runs from \pounds \genDecileOneLossGbp{} in decile one to \pounds \genDecileTenLossGbp{} in decile ten, so the endpoints are ordered as \citet{fetzer2024} would predict, but the maximum falls on decile \emph{eight}, at \pounds \genDecileEightLossGbp{}, with decile nine at \pounds \genDecileNineLossGbp{} also above the top decile, and decile one loses more in cash than deciles two and three.

That non-monotonicity is a property of the main specification and not of every specification. Under both ONS calibrations the cash profile is strictly monotone increasing and peaks at decile ten: correcting the motor-fuel decile shape gives a profile rising from \pounds \genOnsFuelDecileOneLossGbp{} in decile one to \pounds \genOnsFuelDecileTenLossGbp{} in decile ten with no interior peak, and correcting both spending levels gives \pounds \genOnsLevelsDecileOneLossGbp{} to \pounds \genOnsLevelsDecileTenLossGbp{}, likewise monotone. That is what one would expect, since the interior peak is produced by the same flat motor-fuel imputation the ONS calibrations correct, and it is a further reason to read the decile-eight spike as a data artefact rather than as a finding. Table~\ref{tab:decile} reports the full profile under each calibration so the reader can see which is which.

 The claim that survives is the weaker and correct one: the Fetzer--Gazze--Bishop ordering holds at the endpoints under every specification here, while whether it holds as a monotone ranking depends on the motor-fuel imputation. Reporting it as monotone, as the policy literature often does when it quotes only the endpoints, still overstates how cleanly a consumption-proportional subsidy tracks income, but the interior shape is not something this paper can establish.

\subsubsection*{How robust is the gradient?}

The direction of the percentage gradient is robust. It is negative in income in every specification this paper runs --- main, steady-state, symmetric damping, peak-fuel, both ONS calibrations, and the unequivalised robustness line --- and in every cell of the scenario grid in Appendix~\ref{app:grid}. Its \emph{magnitude} is not.

Before the specification variants, the gradient should be read against three measurements of the \emph{same} specification, because how much of it is the bottom decile is a prior question to how much of it is the calibration. The headline decile-one to decile-ten ratio is \genDecileRatioPct{} to one. Measured from decile \emph{two} instead --- which drops the decile in which \genDecileOneZeroIncomeShare{} per cent of households have non-positive equivalised income and the burden denominator is least trustworthy --- it is \genDecileRatioFromDecileTwoPct{} to one, less than half as steep. Winsorising the household burden distribution before aggregating gives \genDecileRatioWinsorisedPct{} to one on the headline basis and \genDecileRatioFromDecileTwoWinsorisedPct{} from decile two. All four are reported, and the pattern across them is the finding: the direction is robust to every one of these choices, and the magnitude ranges over more than a factor of two within a single specification, before any calibration is varied. A reader who wants one number from this paper should be told there is not one.

Three further things move the magnitude, and they should be separated because they are different kinds of problem.

\paragraph{The bottom of the distribution, where the denominator is least trustworthy.} A standard objection is that a steep income gradient is manufactured by small denominators at the bottom. On the equivalised after-housing-costs concept this paper divides by, that objection has real force: \genDecileOneZeroIncomeShare{} per cent of decile one has zero or negative income, and all \genCoverageExcludedHouseholdsM{} million households (\genCoverageExcludedSharePct{} per cent of the total) outside deciles one to ten carry the model's \(-1\) sentinel for non-positive equivalised income. Percentage statistics drop non-positive incomes explicitly and report the dropped weight, and shares of the aggregate loss are normalised on the deciles displayed. The from-decile-two ratio of \genDecileRatioFromDecileTwoPct{} above is the quantitative answer to the objection, and it is a partial concession rather than a rebuttal.

What can be said beyond that is a statement of what is and is not known. The gradient survives on medians, where decile one loses \genDecileOneMedianLossPct{} per cent against decile ten's \genDecileTenMedianLossPct{} per cent --- a median is immune to a handful of near-zero denominators by construction --- and decile one's median equivalised income of \pounds \genDecileOneMedianIncomeGbp{} is low but not degenerate. That is evidence that the gradient is not \emph{only} a small-denominator artefact. It is not evidence that the tail is unproblematic. A fifth of the bottom decile having non-positive measured income on the concept the analysis divides by is a genuine measurement problem in the FRS income concept, it is not one this paper solves, and the burden of decile one should be read with that attached.

\paragraph{The denominator concept itself.} Equivalisation changes the gradient substantially, and the choice is reported rather than absorbed. On the unequivalised basis the same shock gives a mean burden of \genUnequivLossPctIncome{} per cent, a decile-one burden of \genUnequivDecileOneLossPct{} per cent against \genUnequivDecileTenLossPct{} per cent in decile ten, and a ratio of \genUnequivDecileRatioPct{} to one, against \genDecileRatioPct{} on the equivalised basis. Equivalisation steepens the gradient because it scales small households' incomes up and large households' down, and low-income households are disproportionately small. The equivalised figure is the one the paper reports, because it is the concept the published distributional literature uses and the one that makes the comparison in Section~\ref{sec:literature} meaningful; the unequivalised line is here so that the effect of the correction is visible.

\paragraph{The numerator.} Motor fuel carries a large share of the loss and the consumption fusion over-imputes motor fuel at the bottom of the income distribution: the average decile-one household is assigned \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileOneRawGbp{} of annual motor-fuel spend against \pounds \genFuelSpendDecileTenRawGbp{} in decile ten, an almost flat profile, where ONS Family Spending gives \pounds \genOnsBenchFuelDecileOneGbp{} against \pounds \genOnsBenchFuelDecileTenGbp{}. The participation margin shows the same problem directly --- the top and bottom deciles record an identical share of households buying any motor fuel, against National Travel Survey evidence that 40 per cent of the poorest quintile has no car and only 14 per cent of the richest. Some of decile one's measured burden is therefore fuel it does not buy. Rescaling decile motor-fuel spend to the ONS \emph{shape} --- the ONS-calibrated specification --- leaves the aggregate essentially unchanged at \pounds \genOnsFuelAggBn{} billion but cuts the decile-one burden from \genDecileOneLossPct{} to \genOnsFuelDecileOneLossPct{} per cent (\pounds \genDecileOneLossGbp{} to \pounds \genOnsFuelDecileOneLossGbp{}) and takes the ratio to \genOnsFuelDecileRatioPct{} to one. Decile ten does not stay put under this correction: its burden rises from \genDecileTenLossPct{} to \genOnsFuelDecileTenLossPct{} per cent and its cash loss from \pounds \genDecileTenLossGbp{} to \pounds \genOnsFuelDecileTenLossGbp{}, because the rescaling moves motor fuel up the distribution as well as down it. Correcting both spending \emph{levels} to ONS as well as the shape --- which also corrects the domestic under-imputation documented in Section~\ref{sec:limitations} --- gives \pounds \genOnsLevelsAggBn{} billion, a decile-one burden of \genOnsLevelsDecileOneLossPct{} per cent against \genOnsLevelsDecileTenLossPct{} per cent, and a ratio of \genOnsLevelsDecileRatioPct{} to one.

Taken together: the burden falls sharply in income, and the cash loss does not. That pair is common to every specification and is what the paper concludes from. No claim in this paper rests on the difference between a ratio of \genDecileRatioPct{} and one of \genOnsFuelDecileRatioPct{}, and none should be read as resting on it.

Two features of the bottom decile are nonetheless real and should not be smoothed over. Its aggregate-ratio burden of \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent is well above its median burden of \genDecileOneMedianLossPct{} per cent, which tells us that the decile-one mean is pulled up by a genuinely extreme upper tail rather than by measurement noise alone; and that tail is the subject of the next subsection.

The interdecile range of the mean percentage loss---the vertical, between-decile spread---is \genBetweenDecileRangePct{} percentage points. The mean within-decile p90--p10 range is \genWithinDecileRangePct{} percentage points. Taken at face value that says horizontal dispersion inside deciles exceeds vertical dispersion across them, which would be the quantitative form of the \citet{cronin2019} result. It would also be misleading: the comparison is carried entirely by decile one.

Decile one's p90--p10 range is \genDecileOneRangePct{} percentage points. The other nine deciles run from \genWithinDecileRangeMinExDOnePct{} to \genWithinDecileRangePct{} points, and the mean within-decile range excluding decile one is \genWithinDecileRangeExDOnePct{} points --- \emph{below} the between-decile range of \genBetweenDecileRangePct{}. On a median-based measure of within-decile spread, which is less exposed to decile one's tail, the figure is \genWithinDecileMedianRangePct{} points, well below it. So the inequality holds on the mean and fails on nine deciles out of ten, and it rests on the decile in which \genDecileOneZeroIncomeShare{} per cent of households have non-positive equivalised income --- that is, on the part of the distribution Section~\ref{sec:results-decile} has just identified as a measurement problem the paper does not solve. The numerical claim is accordingly not made.

The case for reporting within-decile outcomes does not depend on that inequality. \citet{cronin2019}, \citet{douenne2020} and \citet{sallee2019} establish that a transfer conditioned on observables leaves horizontal losers inside every cell of the conditioning variable, whether or not the within-cell spread happens to exceed the between-cell one; a decile-average scorecard cannot see them either way. And decile one's dispersion is real even if it cannot carry a general claim: its ninetieth percentile burden is \genDecileOnePNinetyLossPct{} per cent of income against a median of \genDecileOneMedianLossPct{} per cent, \genDecileOneShareAboveFivePct{} per cent of the decile loses more than five per cent of net income and \genDecileOneShareAboveTenPct{} per cent loses more than ten. These are the households the policy debate is nominally about, and no instrument calibrated on a decile mean will find them. It is also the reason the uncompensated-loser count in Section~\ref{sec:results-policy} does work that a decile-average incidence table cannot.

\subsection{What the loss is made of: the largest single channel, and why it is no longer a large majority}
\label{sec:results-fuel}

Figure~\ref{fig:fueldecomp} decomposes the aggregate loss by fuel, and it is the single result in this paper most likely to change how the Autumn Budget question is posed.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.85\textwidth]{../results/figures/fig5_fuel_decomposition.png}
\caption{Decomposition of the aggregate household loss into motor fuel, gas and electricity, by scenario and by income decile. Motor fuel carries the largest single share in the main specification; the share is calibration-dependent and its range across specifications is reported in the text and in Table~\ref{tab:specifications}.}
\label{fig:fueldecomp}
\end{figure}

Motor fuel accounts for \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate loss under the realised scenario in the main specification, against \genGasShareOfLoss{} per cent for gas and \genElecShareOfLoss{} per cent for electricity, so the domestic bill as a whole carries \genDomesticShareOfLoss{} per cent. That is the largest single channel, and it is a bare majority.

\paragraph{Why the ratio of the two damping fractions is the whole of this result.} This subsection opens with a mechanism rather than a number, because the mechanism moves the answer more than the movement between any two specifications reported below. The motor-fuel share of the loss depends almost entirely on the \emph{ratio} of the gas and pump sustained fractions --- the shares of each fuel's observed peak that survive into an annual average --- because that ratio sets the relative size of the two legs. On the solved pre-war baseline those fractions are \genGasSustainedFraction{} per cent for gas and \genPumpSustainedFraction{} per cent for pump prices. The gas fraction is now the \emph{larger} of the two.

On the defective baseline it was roughly a fifth, a third of the pump fraction, and that is what produced motor-fuel majorities in the sixties and seventies across earlier drafts. That value was not measuring the persistence of the gas shock. It was absorbing the baseline error: with the observed post-shock July cap standing in for a pre-war level, the solve had to shrink the gas leg to reproduce the published caps, and it did so by driving the sustained fraction down. Correct the baseline and the fraction goes where the wholesale data actually put it. The gas--pump asymmetry that carried the paper's compositional headline was therefore substantially an artefact, and no part of that headline should be read as having survived.

\paragraph{The claim that stands.} Motor fuel is the largest single channel at \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent, and carries a large part of the loss on every calibration --- between \genSpecFuelShareMinPct{} and \genSpecFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent across the seven specifications reported here. It is a majority under some of them and not others, and the paper does not claim a large majority under any. What is invariant is that a substantial part of the shock, never below about a third, runs through a channel that no domestic-bill instrument touches.

\paragraph{Where in the range each specification sits.} The steady-state accounting basis gives \genSteadyStateMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent; the ONS decile-shape correction \genOnsFuelMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent; the peak-fuel upper bound \genPeakFuelMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent; damping the gas and pump peaks by a \emph{common} fraction rather than by the fuel-specific pair \genSymDampMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent; and correcting both spending levels to ONS \genOnsLevelsMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent. Combining the adverse choices takes the share into clear minority territory: symmetric damping on the steady-state basis gives \genSymDampSteadyMotorFuelShare{} per cent and symmetric damping with ONS-consistent spending levels \genSymDampOnsLevelsMotorFuelShare{} per cent. The share is not stable across macro scenarios either --- \genMotorFuelShareOfLossAdverse{} per cent under the NIESR adverse case against \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent in the realised central one.

Two things move it more than any specification choice does. The first is the pre-war gas price, which takes the share from \genDomesticLegNbpFuelShareMinPct{} to \genDomesticLegNbpFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent on its own. The second, and larger, is the assumed monthly shape of the gas peak that the pre-war solve consumes: sweeping it moves the share from \genGasProfileFuelShareMinPct{} to \genGasProfileFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent, and two cells of that sweep admit no solution at all (Section~\ref{sec:step1}, Appendix~\ref{app:domesticleg}). A range that wide is the reason this paper quotes a range rather than a point, and the reason it declines to defend a majority.

Three things follow, and the first two are unaffected by where in the range the share sits.

The first is mechanical and explains Section~\ref{sec:results-decile}: the non-monotone cash profile is a vehicle-ownership profile, not a dwelling-efficiency profile. Car ownership, vehicle count and annual mileage all rise with income and then flatten or fall at the very top. It is tempting to narrate decile eight's cash spike purely as mileage, and the data do not settle that: the diesel uplift in this scenario is \genDieselUpliftPct{} per cent against petrol's \genPetrolUpliftPct{} per cent, so any decile with an above-average diesel share of motor-fuel spend takes a larger proportional hit for the same mileage. Petrol spend, diesel spend and the diesel share are reported by decile so that the reader can see which mechanism is doing the work rather than take the narration on trust. The efficiency channel that \citet{fetzer2024} identify is present in the gas component but is outweighed at the aggregate.

The second is that the transmission mechanism is quite different from the one the price-cap literature analyses. The domestic channel is lagged, capped and attenuated to the wholesale share of the bill; the road-fuel channel is prompt and uncapped. The same wholesale move therefore reaches households substantially through the channel with the least institutional damping, which no analysis conducted in cap-path terms alone will show.

The third is the policy implication, and it survives the whole calibration range even though its magnitude does not. Every instrument in the live debate---the means-tested social tariff, the JRF universal discounted block, the Warm Home Discount expansion, and VAT zero-rating---operates on the domestic bill. Each is therefore confined \emph{by construction} to the domestic share of the loss: \genDomesticShareOfLoss{} per cent in the main specification, and between \genPeakFuelDomesticShareOfLoss{} and \genOnsLevelsDomesticShareOfLoss{} per cent across the seven specifications run here. Under no calibration in this paper does a bill instrument reach the whole shock, and under most it cannot reach even half. When Section~\ref{sec:results-policy} finds that all four leave many losers uncompensated, the binding constraint is not targeting precision; it is that they are aimed at part of the shock. Fuel duty, which is the natural instrument on the other part, is absent from the debate; I return to this in Section~\ref{sec:policy} while noting that its own distributional and environmental properties are far from straightforward.

The regional gradient runs from the North East, at \genRegionMaxLossPct{} per cent of income and \pounds \genRegionMaxLossGbp{}, to London at \genRegionMinLossPct{} per cent and \pounds \genRegionMinLossGbp{}. That spread is larger than a regional energy-exposure difference has any obvious right to be, and it is not benchmarked against any external source; the North East is the lowest-income English region, and a mean cash loss there far above Wales and several times London's is more plausibly the motor-fuel imputation defect of Section~\ref{sec:limitations} reappearing at regional level than a real feature of regional exposure. I therefore report the regional profile as a description, flag it as the least reliable table in the paper, and build nothing on it. Unlike the decile comparison, the pound and percentage rankings here are broadly concordant: the regions that lose most in per cent also tend to lose most in pounds, because between-region income differences and between-region car-dependence differences point the same way. London and the South East are simultaneously the highest-income and the least car-dependent regions, and Northern Ireland's low position reflects both income and its distinct energy mix. The regional result is therefore a weaker version of the decile result rather than an independent one, and I do not build on it.

On the conventional targeting metric the ranking is the expected one. The Warm Home Discount expansion sends \genWhdShareBottomThree{} per cent of its spend to deciles one to three and the means-tested social tariff \genSocialTariffShareBottomThree{} per cent, against \genJrfShareBottomThree{} per cent for the JRF universal block, \genRebateShareBottomThree{} per cent for the flat rebate and \genVatCutShareBottomThree{} per cent for VAT zero-rating. Cost per pound of bottom-decile gain --- a dimensionless ratio of pounds spent to pounds delivered, carrying no currency symbol --- is lowest for the Warm Home Discount expansion at \genBestCostPerPound{}; the flat rebate is at \genRebateCostPerPound{} and the VAT cut at \genWorstCostPerPound{}, the highest in the table. If targeting efficiency were the objective, the argument would end here in favour of means-testing.

It does not end here.

\paragraph{The JRF block, and why its cost cannot be compared with JRF's own.} The block is implemented as JRF propose it, a discount on the \emph{level} of the first half of typical consumption plus a per-child allowance, and it costs \pounds \genJrfCostBn{} billion against JRF's published \pounds \genJrfStatedCostBn{} billion. That gap is not a generosity finding, because the two are not sized on the same block. JRF define typical consumption on Ofgem's typical-consumption basis, which puts the half-block at \pounds \genJrfBlockOfgemBasisGbp{}; this model's weighted median domestic bill puts it at \pounds \genJrfBlockModelledGbp{}, roughly half. Statements in either direction about which scheme is more generous compare two different quantities of energy, and none is made here. What is comparable is the design and the incidence it produces.

\paragraph{Eligibility, not generosity, is the binding margin.} The uncompensated-loser count is a knife-edge measure --- a household left one pound short counts exactly as one left five hundred pounds short --- and applying it across instruments of very different size does not compare like with like. Holding the exchequer envelope fixed corrects that, but only if each instrument is held to a parameter it can actually take. Two cannot: scaled to \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion the social tariff implies a discount of \genSocialTariffScaledParameterPct{} per cent of the bill and the Warm Home Discount a payment of \pounds \genWhdScaledParameterGbp{}, while zero-rating cannot absorb the envelope at all --- the reduced rate is five points, so the instrument tops out at \pounds \genVatCutCostBn{} billion. A ranking generated at those parameters is not a ranking of the policies named.

Held instead to their feasible maxima, the two means-tested instruments offset \genSocialTariffCappedOffsetPct{} and \genWhdCappedOffsetPct{} per cent of the aggregate loss. Widening eligibility at each sponsor's own generosity offsets \genSocialTariffEligibilityOffsetPct{} and \genWhdEligibilityOffsetPct{} per cent --- several times as much for the same money. The margin that matters is therefore who is reached rather than how deep the discount runs, which is the same conclusion the coverage evidence of Section~\ref{sec:limitations} reaches from the opposite direction: the model's means-tested population is \genMeansTestedHouseholdsM{} million households against a Universal Credit caseload alone of over seven million.

Two qualifications belong with that comparison rather than after it. First, saturation is already complete at the sponsor's own parameter, which is a sharper statement than the feasible-maximum row makes: raising the social tariff's discount from 35 to 100 per cent moves the offset only from \genSocialTariffStatedOffsetPct{} to \genSocialTariffCappedOffsetPct{} per cent. Second, at that feasible maximum the instrument pays roughly \pounds \genSocialTariffCappedGainPerEligibleGbp{} to each eligible household against a mean loss among them of \pounds \genMeansTestedMeanLossGbp{}. It plainly overcompensates, and no claim that these instruments leave no household overcompensated is made in this paper.

The size of the eligibility advantage is also conditional on how much of the aggregate loss the means-tested population is imputed to carry, and that imputation cannot be validated here (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}). Under a parity specification the means-tested share of the loss rises from \genMeansTestedShareOfLossPct{} to \genMeansTestedShareOfLossParityPct{} per cent and the advantage falls from \genTargetingMultipleStated{} times to \genTargetingMultipleUnderParity{}; a participation-corrected specification gives \genMeansTestedShareOfLossParticipationPct{} per cent and \genTargetingMultipleUnderParticipation{} times. The direction survives all three specifications. The factor does not, and it is the range that should be quoted.

The mechanism is saturation, and it is worth stating because it generalises beyond this scorecard. A means-tested instrument concentrates spending on a population that a fixed envelope soon over-compensates; past that point the marginal pound delivers no reduction in aggregate residual loss, because it lands on households whose loss is already covered. A broad instrument keeps finding uncovered losers. The conventional targeting metrics cannot see this, because they measure where money goes rather than what it closes. A statement that VAT zero-rating ``compensates nobody'' is true of the uncompensated count at the instrument's own small cost and false of everything else about it.

\paragraph{What the count says.} The uncompensated count tells one true thing the continuous measures do not: how many losing households are left short at all. At each instrument's own stated cost the two means-tested instruments leave the great majority short --- \genSocialTariffUncompensatedShare{} per cent for the social tariff and \genWhdUncompensatedShare{} per cent for the Warm Home Discount expansion --- while the two broad instruments do not: the correctly-specified JRF block leaves \genJrfUncompensatedShare{} per cent uncompensated and fully compensates \genJrfFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent, and the flat rebate leaves \genRebateUncompensatedShare{} per cent and fully compensates \genRebateFullyCompensatedShare{} per cent. Correctly specified, the block outperforms the social tariff on the count by a wide margin, and also outperforms the untargeted flat rebate. But the spread is substantially a statement about relative generosity --- these are instruments costing \pounds \genSocialTariffCostBn{}, \pounds \genWhdCostBn{}, \pounds \genJrfCostBn{} and \pounds \genRebateCostBn{} billion --- and about which channel each acts on, and only partly about targeting design, which is why the common-envelope comparison above is the one to read. The channel constraint of Section~\ref{sec:results-fuel} is what no envelope fixes. A bill instrument scaled to any budget still reaches none of the motor-fuel loss, and that is why the flat rebate --- the only instrument in the set not tied to the domestic bill --- is the only one whose reach is not capped by the domestic share of the loss.

One caveat applies to the two means-tested rows specifically, and it is larger than a caveat usually is. PolicyEngine's modelled means-tested population is \genMeansTestedShareHouseholds{} per cent of households --- \genMeansTestedHouseholdsM{} million --- against a Universal Credit caseload alone of 7.2 million households in May 2026. The model captures roughly two in five Universal Credit households, so the social tariff's and the Warm Home Discount expansion's reach is materially understated and their uncompensated shares correspondingly overstated; those two figures are upper bounds on the uncompensated count, not estimates of it. This does not affect the VAT or flat-rebate rows, which do not condition on benefit receipt. It does bear on the common-envelope comparison, and in a known direction: a means-tested instrument that reached its full administrative caseload would spread the same envelope over a larger population, which reduces the saturation that drives the result above. How far it would go is not something this model can answer, so the common-envelope ranking should be read as conditional on the modelled caseload, with the direction of the bias stated rather than assumed away.
