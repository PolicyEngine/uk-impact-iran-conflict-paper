\begin{abstract}
\noindent I trace the 2026 Iran conflict energy shock from wholesale markets to individual UK households. Macroeconomic scenarios for oil and gas are mapped into an Ofgem price-cap path and a pump-price path, with gas and electricity shocked asymmetrically because gas sets the British electricity price roughly 85 per cent of the time, and the resulting price vector is passed through PolicyEngine UK's Family Resources Survey microdata. In the main specification --- domestic prices annualised as the consumption-weighted average of the quarterly cap levels, all percentages taken on equivalised after-housing-costs income --- the mean household loses \pounds \genCentralLossMean{} a year, equal to \genCentralLossPctIncome{} per cent of equivalised disposable income, giving an aggregate of \pounds \genAggSpendRealisedBn{} billion against the Resolution Foundation's independently published \pounds 11 billion of extra household energy and fuel spend at sustained peaks; an upper-bound specification that applies observed peak pump prices undamped for a full year gives \pounds \genPeakFuelAggBn{} billion, so the two specifications bracket the only comparable published estimate. The incidence is regressive in per cent and non-monotonic in pounds: the bottom decile loses \genDecileOneLossPct{} per cent of income against \genDecileTenLossPct{} per cent for the top, while in cash the largest loss falls on decile eight (\pounds \genDecileEightLossGbp{}), not decile ten (\pounds \genDecileTenLossGbp{}). The composition of the loss is the paper's most consequential finding and also its most calibration-dependent: motor fuel carries \genMotorFuelShareOfLoss{} per cent of the aggregate loss in the main specification and between \genSpecFuelShareMinPct{} and \genSpecFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent across the seven specifications I run, but the majority does not survive every combination of them --- damping the gas and pump peaks by a common fraction takes it to \genSymDampSteadyMotorFuelShare{} per cent on the steady-state basis and \genSymDampOnsLevelsMotorFuelShare{} per cent on ONS-consistent spending levels --- and it moves between \genDomesticLegNbpFuelShareMinPct{} and \genDomesticLegNbpFuelShareMaxPct{} per cent on the pre-war gas price assumption alone. Motor fuel is therefore a large share of the loss and a majority under most but not all calibrations. What holds across that entire range is the policy point: every instrument in the Budget debate --- social tariff, discounted block, Warm Home Discount, VAT zero-rating --- acts on the domestic bill and therefore addresses only part of the shock by construction, which is a channel constraint rather than a targeting failure. Scored at a common \pounds \genEnvelopeBn{} billion envelope and on continuous measures, VAT zero-rating offsets the largest share of the aggregate loss (\genVatCutEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent) and leaves the smallest mean residual, while the means-tested instruments saturate, offsetting \genSocialTariffEnvelopeOffsetPct{} per cent, because additional money lands on households already over-compensated. Within-decile dispersion of the burden exceeds the between-decile gradient. The direction of the percentage gradient is robust to every specification I run; its magnitude is not, ranging from \genOnsFuelDecileRatioPct{} to \genDecileRatioPct{} to one, and the low-income tail is a genuine measurement problem: on the equivalised concept \genDecileOneZeroIncomeShare{} per cent of the bottom decile has non-positive income, so percentage statistics drop those households and report the dropped weight. The analysis is static and use-side only, and is therefore a lower bound on total incidence.
\end{abstract}
